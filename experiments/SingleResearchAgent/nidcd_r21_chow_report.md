# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project aims to identify the neural activity during continuous speech that distinguishes children with persistent stuttering (pCWS) from those who have recovered (rCWS) and their typically fluent peers (CWNS). It will use an innovative fMRI methodology, spatial independent component analysis denoising, to overcome motion artifacts and separate brain activity underlying fluent speech from activity time-locked to individual stuttering events. The most valuable expected knowledge is the first functional neuroimaging evidence in children that characterizes the neural signatures of stuttering persistence versus recovery in an ecologically valid context, thereby advancing mechanistic understanding of the disorder and its natural compensation.

The main supporting evidence is the exceptional team of Ho Ming Chow, Soo-Eun Chang, and Nan Bernstein Ratner, whose combined expertise in fMRI methods, childhood stuttering cohorts, and speech analysis directly enables the project. Critically, the team has already developed and validated the sICA denoising approach and successfully piloted the storytelling paradigm in a child, providing strong feasibility data. The design's primary limits are its modest sample size of 60 children, which may have insufficient power for subtle recovery effects, and its cross-sectional nature, which can only infer developmental trajectories rather than directly observe them. These are acknowledged characteristics of an exploratory R21 study and are mitigated by pragmatic operational definitions and contingency plans.

The proposal's considerable strengths—a compelling scientific question, an established and expert team, an innovative and validated methodology, and a well-defined participant pool—outweigh its primary limitations, which are inherent to its exploratory R21 structure. The methodological breakthrough of sICA denoising credibly overcomes a major historical barrier, enabling the collection of ecologically valid data that can directly test mechanistic hypotheses for the first time. While the cross-sectional design and modest sample are genuine constraints, they do not defeat the project's stated purpose of identifying and characterizing neural markers. The proposal is therefore judged a well-conceived, high-value plan with a sufficient route to its primary goals.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

The scientific value of this project is high. The question it addresses—what patterns of brain activity during continuous speech are associated with persistent stuttering versus recovery in childhood—is both well-motivated and currently unanswered. No published fMRI study has directly compared neural activity during overt, connected speech in children with persistent stuttering, children who have recovered, and typically fluent peers. The knowledge the project would produce is therefore genuinely new and carries important basic and translational implications [1].

The proposal’s theoretical framework provides a coherent structure for the anticipated contribution. It distinguishes a left frontotemporal pathway supporting speech monitoring, a right‑frontal compensatory pathway, and motor‑execution circuitry involving the supplementary motor area, basal ganglia, and motor cortex [1]. Recovery, on this account, could involve either normalization of left‑hemisphere processing or recruitment of an alternative right‑hemisphere route. By testing these possibilities in a single experimental design, the study would deliver functional evidence that can discriminate between competing mechanistic accounts. That evidence would in turn inform hypotheses about why some children recover naturally while others do not, a distinction that existing structural work has begun to delineate but that functional data have not yet captured [1].

**A design capable of separating sustained and event‑related activity.** The attainable knowledge extends beyond a simple catalogue of regional differences. The mixed block/event design explicitly separates sustained neural activity during fluent speech from transient activity time‑locked to individual stuttering moments [1]. This separation matters because it allows the study to determine whether group differences observed during continuous speech reflect a general characteristic of speech production in children who stutter or are driven primarily by the presence of stuttering events themselves. The inclusion of a recovered group further permits an inference about which neural features track clinical status rather than simply historical diagnosis. Collectively, these comparisons can reveal whether the brain activity associated with recovery resembles that of typically fluent children, represents a distinct compensatory organization, or falls somewhere between the two—an ambiguity that the extant childhood diffusion imaging data, with their intermediate recovered‑group phenotypes, already anticipate [1].

**Descriptive and practical contributions.** By applying spatial independent component analysis denoising to fMRI data acquired during continuous speech in young children, the project will generate a whole‑brain functional characterization of speech‑motor networks that has previously been difficult to obtain without radiation exposure [1]. These data would establish a reference point for future longitudinal tracking from onset through persistence or recovery, as the proposal itself envisions [1].

While the proposal’s longer‑term objective is to inform interventions, the study is not designed to test a treatment. Its nearer‑term utility lies in identifying candidate neural targets that could eventually be monitored as markers of treatment response or used to stratify children by likely trajectory. In an R21 context, that level of translational promise is both realistic and significant. The combination of a well‑specified question, a design capable of answering it, and a clear path from descriptive findings to future hypothesis‑driven work makes the scientific value of the project strong.

## Innovation

The project is highly innovative for its field, not because it introduces a fundamentally new technique, but because it applies a recently validated methodological advance to a population and question where it can overcome a long-standing barrier and thereby produce the first evidence of its kind.

**A novel combination of comparison groups and task context.** The central novelty lies in the direct, within-study comparison of neural activity during ecologically valid, overt continuous speech in three carefully defined groups of children: those with persistent stuttering, those who have recovered, and typically fluent peers [1]. While the team’s own prior work has provided structural MRI and diffusion tensor imaging evidence distinguishing these groups during childhood [1], and has characterized functional activity during continuous speech in adults who stutter [1], no published study has combined the developmental, clinical, and task-based dimensions in a single functional imaging experiment. The proposed project would fill precisely that gap, replacing inference from adult data or from childhood rest and simple production tasks with direct functional observation in the relevant population and behavioral context.

**A validated denoising method applied to a new population.** The methodological innovation is the application of sICA denoising to continuous speech fMRI in young children. The core challenge that has limited previous work is that overt speech movements contaminate the BOLD signal, and children are especially prone to head motion [1]. The sICA approach, developed and validated by members of this team, uses six noise templates and three component-intensity measures—out-of-brain ratio, scattering degree, and slice-wise variation—to separate movement- and speech-related artifacts from neuronal signals based on their distinct spatial patterns [1]. The preliminary data demonstrate its effectiveness: in an analysis of over 45,000 volumes from 281 scans of 50 children who stutter and 45 controls aged 3–13, the strong relationship between movement and BOLD volume-to-volume variation diminished substantially after denoising, and DVARS remained below approximately 1% for movements up to 9 mm [1]. Validation against PET in 18 adults yielded similar continuous-speech activity patterns, and an eight-year-old child successfully completed the proposed storytelling paradigm, showing expected perisylvian activation [1]. These feasibility results make the method credible for the proposed application, and the application itself is a clear advance beyond resting-state or sparse-sampling designs previously used with this age group.

**A mixed block/event design that isolates stuttering-related activity.** A further innovative element is the mixed block/event general linear model design, which explicitly separates sustained neural activity during fluent speech blocks from transient activity time-locked to individual stuttering events [1]. This analytical strategy has been used in adult language research but has not been applied to characterize childhood stuttering. It allows the project to address a question that simpler blocked designs cannot: whether the neural differences between persistent and recovered children arise from a tonic alteration in speech-motor networks or are driven primarily by the occurrence of stuttering moments themselves. The combination of this design with the sICA denoising pipeline represents a genuinely new capability for developmental stuttering research.

**A productive incremental advance.** The contribution to the specific research field is substantial precisely because it is incremental in the most productive sense. The project does not claim to discover a new brain region or propose a novel theory of stuttering; rather, it takes established theoretical distinctions—left frontotemporal speech-monitoring pathways, right-frontal compensation, and motor-execution circuitry involving SMA, basal ganglia, and motor cortex [1]—and submits them to a test that previous methods could not perform in children. The prior structural diffusion findings, which showed no corresponding reduction in left inferior-frontal white-matter coherence in the recovered group but greater right inferior-frontal coherence, provide strong motivation for the functional hypotheses and illustrate the pattern of intermediate phenotypes that the functional data may clarify [1]. The innovation thus lies in the convergence of a validated method, a well-defined developmental question, and an experimental paradigm that together can deliver functional evidence that discriminates competing mechanistic accounts of persistence and recovery.

## Rigor

The research design demonstrates good rigor for an exploratory R21 study, with genuine methodological strengths that are appropriately tempered by acknowledged limitations inherent to its scope. The project’s ability to support its intended inferences rests on a well-integrated chain of design choices: a validated denoising pipeline that addresses the primary technical barrier, a carefully operationalized participant classification system, and an analytical strategy capable of separating the neural correlates of fluent speech from those of stuttering events.

**Data quality: the sICA denoising pipeline.** The most critical element underpinning the design’s internal validity is the sICA denoising approach. Without it, the inference from continuous speech fMRI in young children would be severely compromised by motion artifact [1]. As noted earlier, the preliminary evidence for its effectiveness is substantial: the analysis of over 45,000 volumes from 281 scans demonstrated markedly reduced movement–BOLD coupling after denoising, with DVARS below approximately 1% for movements under 9 mm [1]. The cross-modal PET validation in adults and the successful child pilot provide convergent support. The proposal also specifies a contingency for volumes with abrupt motion exceeding the 9 mm working range, namely image scrubbing, further strengthening the data quality plan [1].

**Analytical separation of fluent speech and stuttering events.** The mixed block/event general linear model is essential for the core inferences. It explicitly models sustained neural activity during fluent speech blocks separately from transient activity time-locked to individual stuttering events [1]. Aim 1a’s comparison of fluent speech between pCWS and CWNS could otherwise be confounded by stuttering events present in the pCWS group, and Aim 1b’s characterization of stuttering-event activity requires isolating those instances from the ongoing speech context. The use of two independent speech-language pathologists to identify and code stuttering events in CLAN/CHAT format, along with the extraction of linguistic covariates—speech rate, utterance length, semantic diversity (TTR, VocD), and syntactic complexity (DSS, IPSYN)—whose principal components will be included in the imaging model, adds important control for potential confounds [1]. Analyst blinding to diagnosis and permutation-based multiple-comparison correction are standard and appropriate safeguards [1].

**Participant ascertainment.** The classification of persistent versus recovered stuttering uses serial SSI-4 assessments with a threshold of 10, supplemented by clinician and parent reports, and requires persistence scores above 10 at years 3 and 4 for the pCWS group [1]. A higher SSI-4 threshold has been proposed in the classification literature for optimal predictive validity, and some children classified as persistent might therefore eventually recover. However, the temporal safeguard of requiring elevated scores across multiple years mitigates this concern, and the risk primarily affects the interpretation of group differences as stable traits rather than as markers of current clinical status—an ambiguity the cross-sectional design already carries. The additional requirement that pCWS show greater than 4.4% stuttering during quiet speech assessment, and the plan to exclude children with fewer than ten in-scanner stuttering instances only from Aim 1b’s event analysis, are pragmatic steps that protect the planned comparisons without wasting data [1].

**Recognised limitations: sample size and cross‑sectional design.** The primary limitations affecting rigor are the modest sample size and the cross-sectional design. The power calculation, extrapolated from adult data (between-group effect size 0.7 with SD 0.65), estimates 15 participants per group as sufficient and inflates recruitment to 20 to accommodate exclusion and potentially smaller recovery effects [1]. Adult effect sizes may not transfer to children; previous childhood analyses found significant differences concentrated in left premotor cortex, and recovery-related effects may be subtler. The cross-sectional design means the study can identify current group differences but cannot establish the developmental trajectory that produced them—a point the proposal acknowledges honestly, noting that future longitudinal work would be needed [1]. Both limitations are characteristic of the R21 exploratory mechanism and are mitigated rather than eliminated by the proposal’s contingency plans. Importantly, neither defeats the project’s stated ability to characterize neural markers of persistence and recovery; they constrain the precision and developmental interpretation of those markers rather than the feasibility of identifying them.

## Feasibility

Feasibility is high, grounded in a validated method that directly addresses the principal technical obstacle to the project’s goals and in an accessible, well-characterized participant population.

**A validated technical solution to the core barrier.** The critical dependence of the entire study is on obtaining interpretable BOLD fMRI data during overt, continuous speech from young children. The sICA denoising approach credibly resolves the historical barrier of speech-related and motion artifacts contaminating the signal [1]. As detailed above, the preliminary data provide strong evidence that this method works under conditions directly relevant to the proposed study: the large childhood dataset demonstrated effective denoising, the PET validation confirmed correspondence with an independent modality, and the eight-year-old pilot child produced the expected activation pattern using the proposed storytelling paradigm [1]. These feasibility results substantially reduce the risk that the core method will fail in the target population and provide an empirical basis for the movement thresholds and scrubbing contingency described in the plan [1].

**Participant infrastructure.** The study will recruit from an existing longitudinal cohort that has already undergone annual assessments, meaning that the classification of children as pCWS, rCWS, or CWNS relies on serial data rather than on a single cross-sectional snapshot [1]. This existing infrastructure reduces the uncertainty and timeline pressure associated with de novo recruitment, screening, and diagnosis. The children’s prior experience with scanning and storytelling tasks from the longitudinal study is an additional practical advantage likely to improve compliance and reduce attrition [1].

**Task and equipment readiness.** The storytelling paradigm using the textless picture book *Frog, Where Are You?* has been successfully used in this team’s childhood cohort [1]. The imaging will be performed on a GE MR750 3T scanner with an eight-channel head coil, and the necessary ancillary equipment—an optical noise-cancellation microphone, an MR-compatible camera, and E-Prime for synchronization—are all specified as available [1]. The use of an overlearned speech condition such as alphabet recitation as a control provides a within-subject baseline that does not require additional equipment or task training beyond the familiarization session [1].

**Managed uncertainties.** Several uncertainties are appropriately acknowledged and mitigated. The power calculation extrapolates effect sizes from adult data, and the actual effects in children may differ; the plan addresses this by inflating the recruited sample from the estimated minimum of 15 per group to 20, providing a buffer against smaller effects and subject exclusion [1]. The potential reduction of stuttering frequency inside the scanner is managed by a pre-scan eligibility threshold (\>4.4% stuttering during quiet speech) and by the provision that children with fewer than ten in-scanner stuttering instances will be excluded only from the event-related analysis in Aim 1b, preserving their data for the fluent-speech comparisons [1]. The 36-month schedule, with 18 months allocated to recruitment and imaging and the remaining time for analysis and dissemination, is realistic given the existing cohort infrastructure and the integrated expertise of the Chow–Chang–Ratner team [1]. No critical dependency, whether technical, logistical, or related to participant availability, appears unaddressed or carries a high probability of preventing the project from achieving its primary aims.

## Ethics and safety

The ethics and safety provisions are comprehensive and proportionate to the activities proposed. The study involves no invasive procedures or experimental interventions beyond an MRI scan and behavioural speech tasks, and the risks it does carry—discomfort, anxiety, fatigue, and potential breaches of privacy—are addressed with specific, age-appropriate mitigations. The plan meets the standards expected for non-invasive neuroimaging research with a paediatric clinical population.

**Consent, assent, and participant welfare.** Parents or guardians will provide written informed consent, and children will give verbal or written assent, with the process adapted to their age and comprehension [1]. This dual-permission structure respects both parental authority and the developing autonomy of the child participants. The age range of 5–11 years and the team’s extensive experience with this longitudinal cohort make it reasonable to expect that assent discussions will be tailored individually.

The physical and psychological risks of the MRI session are managed through multiple layers of precaution. Standard MRI safety screening will be conducted before scanning, and hearing will be protected with headphones or earplugs [1]. The protocol includes familiarization with the scanner environment and task procedures before the actual imaging session, scheduled breaks during scanning, and an explicit option for the child to stop at any time without penalty [1]. Parents may remain nearby and can be present with a child who becomes distressed, an arrangement that is particularly important for younger children in this age range. If a child cannot complete the task, the option to reschedule with additional training is specified, which protects against the pressure to continue an uncomfortable session [1].

The storytelling paradigm itself, based on the textless picture book *Frog, Where Are You?*, is non-threatening and developmentally appropriate; it has been used successfully in the team’s childhood cohort, which provides empirical reassurance that it is acceptable to children in this age group [1]. The control condition of reciting overlearned speech is similarly low-burden. Neither task introduces content that would be expected to cause distress.

**Data privacy and sharing.** The study will collect audio and video recordings of speech and visible articulatory movements alongside structural and functional MRI data [1]. All data will be stored with coded identifiers rather than directly identifying information, and access will be restricted through locked or password-protected storage with controlled collaborator access [1]. The plan provides for data sharing after analysis and publication, limited to de-identified relevant data under a formal data-use agreement, with release anticipated within one year of study closeout [1]. This balances the scientific value of data sharing against the privacy interests of a paediatric population whose speech recordings could, in principle, be re-identified. The use of a data-use agreement rather than open-access deposition is a proportionate restriction given the nature of the data.

No material risks to the broader community, to the environment, or to the research staff are evident from the described procedures. The study does not involve drug administration, radiation exposure beyond the MRI’s non-ionizing fields, deception, or experimental manipulation of stuttering frequency. The ethical framework is appropriate for an observational neuroimaging study with a vulnerable paediatric population, and the proposed safeguards are sufficient to manage the identified risks.

## Management

The management plan is well-conceived and draws on a team whose combined expertise directly maps onto the project's three operational components: fMRI acquisition and denoising, longitudinal childhood cohort infrastructure, and rigorous speech analysis. The integration of these capabilities into a single investigative team is a genuine strength, as it bundles the distinct competencies that the experimental design demands and that would be difficult for any single laboratory to assemble independently [1].

**Team expertise and role alignment.** The principal investigator, Ho Ming Chow, brings the core methodological expertise in sICA denoising and continuous-speech fMRI. The method was developed and validated by his group, and the preliminary data—spanning over 45,000 volumes from 281 childhood scans and a cross-modal PET validation in 18 adults—demonstrate direct PI-level command of the technique on which the project's data quality depends [1]. Soo-Eun Chang contributes the longitudinal childhood stuttering cohort from which participants will be drawn, and her structural and diffusion imaging work in this same population provides the empirical foundation for the functional hypotheses [1]. Nan Bernstein Ratner leads the speech-analysis component, bringing the expertise required for the CLAN/CHAT transcription, stuttering-event coding by two independent speech-language pathologists, and extraction of linguistic covariates [1]. Each of these roles corresponds to a critical dependency in the research design, and the named investigators have published extensively in their respective domains, including as collaborators on the preliminary studies cited in the proposal.

**Schedule and resources.** The schedule allocates 18 months to recruitment and imaging, with analysis preparation running in parallel, followed by 12 months for speech and fMRI analysis and preliminary presentations, and a final six months for manuscript preparation, submission, and dissemination [1]. This 36-month timeline is realistic for an R21 mechanism given that participants are drawn from an existing, longitudinally characterized cohort rather than recruited de novo. The annual assessment cycles already maintained by the cohort provide a natural mechanism for confirming group classification, scheduling scan visits, and collecting current SSI-4 and treatment-history data at the point of imaging [1]. The children's prior experience with scanning and storytelling tasks should reduce the need for repeated familiarization sessions and lower the risk of task non-compliance, which is explicitly identified as a potential problem and addressed with a rescheduling option [1].

Resource availability is well documented at the level expected for a scientific proposal. The GE MR750 3T scanner, eight-channel head coil, optical noise-cancellation microphone, MR-compatible camera, and E-Prime synchronization software are all specified as accessible, and no capital equipment requests or facility-access contingencies are identified [1]. The proposal does not include a detailed budget or itemized personnel effort allocation, but an R21 narrative is not expected to supply a full administrative application, and the absence of that detail does not, by itself, indicate a resource gap or management weakness. The division of labor implied by the investigator roles—Chow for fMRI methods, Chang for cohort access and clinical characterization, Ratner for speech coding—is coherent, and the plan to blind imaging analysts to diagnosis supports the independence of the analytical stream within this collaborative structure [1].

**Future directions and continuity.** The proposal also identifies a credible path beyond the funded period, noting future directions that include functional connectivity during continuous speech, time-resolved analyses of transitions into stuttering, and longitudinal tracking from onset through persistence or recovery [1]. While these extensions are not part of the current management plan, their mention signals that the team has considered how the R21 data and infrastructure could support subsequent applications, and the longitudinal cohort already in place provides the continuity needed for such follow-up work. No management-related dependency appears unaddressed or carries a substantial risk of preventing the project from delivering its stated aims.

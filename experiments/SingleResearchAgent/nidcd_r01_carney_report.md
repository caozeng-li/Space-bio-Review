# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project aims to develop and test a mechanistic, computational model of auditory masking and spectral coding based on neural fluctuations rather than the stimulus energy used in standard models. The planned work combines computational modeling with human psychophysics and neurophysiological recordings from the inferior colliculus of awake rabbits. Its most valuable expected contribution is an individualized modeling framework that predicts a listener's hearing-in-noise performance from their peripheral physiology, rather than their audiogram alone. By explaining robust human detection of tones in noise when energy cues are unreliable, and by accounting for differences between simultaneous and forward-masking estimates of cochlear tuning, the project could fundamentally shift understanding of how the brain uses peripheral neural codes. Extending this account to the steep spectral slopes in pinna cues and fricative consonants further promises insights into why simple amplification is insufficient to restore normal hearing in listeners with sensorineural hearing loss.

The proposal is supported by a coherent theoretical framework that links peripheral nonlinear processing, auditory-nerve fluctuations, and modulation tuning in the midbrain. Preliminary computational models have already replicated key human psychophysical results that are difficult for energy-based models, and initial neural recordings from awake rabbits show responses consistent with the proposed fluctuation cues. The experimental design incorporates strong tests, such as predicting later-task performance from an initial characterization without further fitting. The main substantive limitation is an inherent cross-species validation gap: the project does not include rabbit behavioral psychophysics, so the comparison is between rabbit neural thresholds and human behavioral thresholds, which limits the directness of the physiological evidence for active human cue use. Other material limitations include the ambition of recording 200 neurons per stimulus condition across many conditions, and a forward-masking model that is less mechanistically developed than the simultaneous-masking account.

Weighing the evidence, the proposal's significant scientific value and strong innovation, anchored by a coherent theoretical framework and robust preliminary data, support a judgment of scientific merit. The most substantive limitation is the cross-species, cross-level validation gap, which constrains the interpretation of the physiological data as direct evidence of human perceptual mechanisms. The ambitious neural recording targets and the less fully specified forward-masking account further temper confidence. However, the project's core strength—building and testing individualized, predictive models of hearing across multiple tasks—provides a powerful internal validation strategy. The theoretical framework is sufficiently developed, and the discriminating psychophysical manipulations are sufficiently well-chosen, that the project is likely to yield important knowledge and useful models even if every recording target is not met. Therefore, the current proposal is sufficient for support.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**The proposal addresses a fundamental problem in auditory science with high attainable value: understanding how the brain detects sounds in noise when simple energy cues fail, and why sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) impairs this ability beyond a loss of audibility.** The dominant power-spectrum model cannot explain robust human detection of tones in notched noise under roving-level conditions, nor discrepancies between filter-bandwidth estimates from simultaneous and forward masking [1]. The project's central contribution is a mechanistic, individualized framework that explains these phenomena through neural fluctuation cues shaped by peripheral nonlinearities and read out by modulation-tuned neurons in the inferior colliculus (IC). Demonstrating that individual listeners' performance across multiple tasks can be predicted from their peripheral physiology—rather than inferred from the audiogram alone—would constitute a conceptual advance with direct implications for hearing-device design and diagnostics [1].

The knowledge the project can deliver is both descriptive and practically useful. It promises to characterize how inner- and outer-hair-cell dysfunction alters fluctuation cues at each frequency, identify whether listeners shift from envelope-based to energy-based detection where hearing is poorest, and test whether forward-masking filter estimates reflect the same cochlear tuning as simultaneous-masking estimates or are shaped by central modulation processing [1]. The individualized modeling strategy is itself a methodological contribution: Aim 1 behavioral characterization jointly with audiometry and distortion-product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAEs) will constrain each listener's model, which then predicts Aim 2 and Aim 3 performance without further fitting [1]. This within-listener prediction across tasks provides a stronger test than group-level model fits and would, if successful, establish a reusable framework for evaluating individual hearing-in-noise deficits.

The preliminary basis strengthens the claim that these contributions are attainable. Published work from the laboratory shows that a single physiological model based on neural fluctuation cues in model IC neurons can account for fixed- and roving-level notched-noise thresholds as well as a profile-analysis task—capturing results the power-spectrum model cannot explain [2]. Recordings from awake rabbit IC demonstrate that the majority of neurons show rate changes consistent with fluctuation-based predictions during tone-in-noise detection, with neural thresholds broadly matching behavioral data [3]. A pilot study of listeners with SNHL found that rove effects were largest at 4 kHz, where thresholds were most elevated, suggesting a frequency-dependent shift toward energy-cue reliance [4]. In a small pilot, four of five listeners showed frequency-dependent cue use, with envelope cues more useful in frequency regions with better hearing, while one listener with a relatively flat audiogram was better predicted by energy cues throughout the tested range—directly motivating individualized characterization rather than assigning a cue from the audiogram alone [1]. Model responses to fricatives and pinna-related spectra change with simulated impairment in ways that simple amplification does not restore, indicating that the framework can address why hearing aids do not fully recover normal hearing [1].

The practical implications extend beyond basic science. Understanding that SNHL alters the neural representation of spectral slopes—critical for consonant identity and sound localization via pinna cues—would reframe the challenge for hearing devices from restoring audibility to restoring specific neural population codes [1]. The project's data and individualized models, to be shared through the laboratory website as MATLAB or C components with documentation, would provide a resource for other laboratories and potentially inform clinical assessment strategies [1]. These contributions are sufficiently valuable and well-grounded in preliminary evidence to merit the investment proposed, even though the forward-masking extension is less mechanistically developed and the cross-species neural-to-behavioral comparison imposes an inherent limitation on direct validation of active human cue use.

## Innovation

**The project offers significant innovation by extending a coherent neural-fluctuation account well beyond its current normal-hearing, group-level basis to individualized SNHL modeling across three connected domains: simultaneous and forward masking, and spectral-slope coding.** This is not incremental curve-fitting to existing datasets; it proposes a mechanistic re-interpretation of classic masking phenomena, anchored in peripheral physiology and midbrain processing, that directly challenges the long-dominant energy-based power-spectrum model.

The principal conceptual advance is the shift from one-size-fits-all models to individualized predictions. Prior work from this laboratory established that neural fluctuation cues in model IC neurons can account for group-level notched-noise and profile-analysis performance [2], and that awake-rabbit IC neurons exhibit rate changes broadly consistent with those cues [3]. The current proposal moves decisively beyond that foundation. Aim 1 builds a listener-specific model by combining each individual's audiogram, DPOAEs, and behavioral cue-use estimates from fixed- versus roving-level detection tracks, then uses that model to predict the same listener's performance in Aims 2 and 3—without independent fitting to those later tasks [1]. This within-subject, cross-task predictive strategy represents a more stringent and informative test than fitting separate models to each experiment, and it directly addresses the observed variability among listeners with similar audiograms [1]. No other group currently links awake-rabbit IC recordings to listener-specific SNHL models across this range of tasks.

The extension to forward masking (Aim 2b) is a further innovative step, albeit one with a less fully specified mechanistic basis. The proposal argues that neural-fluctuation profiles at gap-probe transitions, shaped by the same peripheral nonlinearities and IC modulation tuning that account for simultaneous masking, may explain the persistent puzzle of why forward-masking filter-bandwidth estimates differ from simultaneous-masking estimates [1]. Published work from the laboratory on inherent envelope fluctuations in forward masking [5] provides a starting point, and the planned discriminating manipulations—such as adding controlled modulation to the masker gap—are well chosen to distinguish fluctuation-based from temporal-integration accounts. However, the proposal acknowledges that this component is more exploratory, treating the consequences for estimates of peripheral compression and tuning as "hypotheses to test" [1].

Aim 3 extends the framework to a qualitatively new domain: the steep spectral slopes of pinna-related notches and fricative consonants. Applying the same fluctuation-based population code to explain both elevation perception from pinna cues and consonant identification represents a theoretically economical and original move. The preliminary model already shows that simulated outer-hair-cell impairment disrupts IC population responses to fricatives and pinna spectra in ways that simple amplification does not recover [1]. If successful, this extension would demonstrate that a single coding principle—neural fluctuation contrasts across frequency channels—unifies phenomena traditionally studied in separate literatures on masking, spatial hearing, and speech perception, which is a genuinely novel integrative contribution.

The methodological innovation of combining awake-rabbit IC recordings with individualized computational models also deserves emphasis. The awake preparation preserves spontaneous activity and modulation tuning that are substantially altered under anesthesia [6], making the physiological data more relevant to human perceptual mechanisms than data from anesthetized preparations.

## Rigor

**The proposal's design supports its intended inferences for the mature simultaneous-masking account and individualized-prediction framework, but the cross-species neural-to-behavioral comparison and the less fully specified forward-masking mechanism limit the directness of the physiological evidence for active human cue use.**

### The integrated predictive design

The strongest element of the design is the integrated, multi-level strategy for distinguishing neural-fluctuation accounts from energy-based alternatives, which is then used to build and test individualized models. The fixed- versus roving-level paradigm in Aim 1 is an established and direct behavioral assay: thresholds that remain stable under a 20-dB overall-level rove implicate level-independent (envelope or fluctuation) cues, whereas elevated roving-level thresholds point to energy-cue reliance [1]. Leong et al. (2020) used this paradigm in SNHL listeners and found the largest rove effects at 4 kHz, where hearing thresholds were most elevated [4]; the proposal's extension across five test frequencies with individualized model fitting is a logical and well-powered next step [1].

Critically, the design uses Aim 1 behavioral estimates jointly with audiometry and DPOAEs to constrain each listener's peripheral model parameters; that model then generates quantitative predictions for the notched-noise, forward-masking, and consonant-identification tasks in Aims 2 and 3 without further free parameters [1]. This within-listener, cross-task prediction provides a strong, falsifiable test that directly addresses the project's core claim—that individual cue-use strategies can be captured by a single physiologically grounded model rather than inferred post hoc from task performance.

### Discriminating manipulations and published benchmarks

The discriminating stimulus manipulations proposed in Aim 2a are well chosen to isolate fluctuation-based from energy-based cues. Replacing a tonal target with an equal-energy narrowband-noise target changes the fluctuation profile while equating the energy in the masker notch, and the preliminary model already shows distinct IC population responses to this manipulation [1]. Varying flanking-band slopes and filling the notch with low-level modulated sounds offer convergent tests that do not rely on a single critical stimulus. The incorporation of published benchmark datasets—Glasberg and Moore (1990); Baker and Rosen (2002, 2006); Lentz et al. (1999); Oxenham and Simonson (2006)—as targets for model reconstruction adds an external validation layer that complements the new data collection [1].

The signal-detection-theory framework for translating model sensitivity into predicted thresholds includes explicit consideration of Poisson variance or variance proportional to mean rate, independence or covariance among neurons, and combinations of modulation-transfer-function classes; it provides a principled quantitative bridge between model and behavior [1]. The criterion that "a model whose trends cannot be reconciled with observed thresholds can be rejected even if a single threshold is matched" [1] establishes a conservative, falsification-oriented standard well suited to the stage of theory development.

### Cross-species, cross-level comparison

The most material limitation is the inherent cross-species, cross-level comparison: rabbit IC neural thresholds will be compared with human behavioral thresholds without rabbit psychophysics as a bridging measure. The proposal explicitly acknowledges that "rabbit behavioral experiments are not proposed" [1].

The awake-rabbit preparation used by the PI offers a meaningful advantage over anesthetized models because anesthesia markedly alters spontaneous activity, sustained responses, and upper limits of temporal coding in the IC [6]. However, previous work from this laboratory noted that "rabbits, for example, do not use available temporal information as efficiently as humans" [2], underscoring that rabbit behavioral performance cannot be assumed to match human performance on the same stimuli. The IC recordings can therefore establish that neural fluctuation information is *available* in the rabbit midbrain and that its magnitude changes systematically with simulated hearing loss, but they do not directly validate the claim that human listeners *actively use* those cues for perceptual decisions.

The human individualized-modeling strategy partially mitigates this gap: the model predictions for human listeners are tested against human behavioral data, and the rabbit physiology serves to confirm that the neural representations posited by the model are physiologically plausible rather than merely computational constructs. Nevertheless, the absence of within-species behavioral validation means the physiological component provides corroborative rather than confirmatory evidence for the central claim.

### Forward-masking specification

The forward-masking extension (Aim 2b) is the least mechanistically specified component. The proposal links forward masking to modulation tuning primarily through a planned analysis "relating forward-masked responses to each neuron's modulation tuning" [1]. While the Brennan et al. (2023) study on inherent envelope fluctuations in forward masking provides groundwork [5], the specific computational mechanism by which neural fluctuations at the gap–probe transition generate the psychophysical threshold patterns is not articulated at the level of detail given for simultaneous masking. The proposal treats the consequences for estimates of peripheral compression and tuning as "hypotheses to test" [1], which is appropriate framing given the exploratory nature of this aim, but it also means the forward-masking predictions rest on a less complete chain of inference than those for simultaneous masking.

### Participant numbers

A minor inconsistency in the human-participant numbers—the shared methods describing 15 age-matched controls plus 30 SNHL listeners, alongside a reference to "a total recruitment of 40 listeners" [1]—does not alter the overall assessment of the sample size, which at 30 SNHL listeners is adequate for the individual-analyses approach emphasized throughout.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility is good overall, supported by established infrastructure, a well-demonstrated awake-rabbit preparation, and detailed psychophysical methods; the primary risks are the ambition of the physiological recording targets and participant retention given the testing burden.**

The project rests on three integrated workstreams—computational modeling, human psychophysics, and IC recordings from awake rabbits—all of which the PI's laboratory has demonstrated successfully in prior studies that directly prefigure the proposed experiments. The computational core uses the published Zilany et al. (2014) auditory-nerve model with inner- and outer-hair-cell parameters adjustable from individual audiometry and DPOAE data [1], so the modeling infrastructure is mature and does not require fundamental development. The awake-rabbit preparation with tetrode recordings in the IC, including daily probe-tube calibration, has yielded extensive published datasets on tone-in-noise detection and formant-frequency encoding [3] [7]. The human psychophysical protocols—adaptive two-down/one-up tracks, interleaved fixed- and roving-level conditions, notched-noise paradigms at multiple frequencies—are standard in the field and refined by the PI's prior work [1], reducing the risk of procedural failure.

The laboratory resources are adequate and accessible. The proposal documents sound-attenuated booths, auditory equipment including insert earphones and probe-tube microphones, local computational facilities, and access to university high-performance computing [1]. Consultants cover audiology, hearing-loss psychophysics, phonetics, and theoretical psychoacoustics, filling expertise gaps without requiring the PI to develop new technical skills de novo [1]. No unusual equipment acquisition, facility construction, or external-access dependency is identified that could threaten feasibility.

### Physiological recording yields

The feasibility concern that carries the most weight is the ambitious physiological recording target. The proposal aims for "responses from approximately 200 neurons for each stimulus condition" [1] across the multiple conditions spanning Aim 2 (several notch widths, frequencies, levels, masker types for simultaneous masking; multiple forward-masking delays). For context, the Fan et al. (2021) study recorded 163 neurons for a single masker type across an unspecified number of animals [3], and Agarwalla & Carney (2026) obtained 150 neurons from two rabbits [7]. The current proposal plans four rabbits, each to be used for 12–24 months of recording [1].

Sustained tetrode recordings can remain productive over many months, which partially offsets the concern. However, the multiplicity of conditions means that achieving the 200-neuron target for *each* condition would require a substantially higher total yield than any single study from this laboratory has reported. The proposal does not specify how conditions will be prioritized if yields fall short, though the linked nature of the aims means that conditions discriminating between energy and fluctuation accounts would naturally take precedence. This is not an infeasible target, but it is an optimistic one whose partial achievement would still support the core model tests, just with less statistical precision for condition-by-condition physiological comparisons.

### Participant retention

Participant retention in the SNHL group represents a second material risk. The testing burden is approximately 12–15 hours per listener over 6–8 weeks, with all participants completing Aim 1 characterization before progressing to Aim 2 or 3 [1]. The SNHL eligibility criteria allow participants up to age 80 with thresholds up to 60 dB HL [1]. Attrition in older populations undertaking multi-session laboratory studies is a recognized challenge, and the targeted 30 SNHL listeners—adequate for the individual-analyses approach if fully retained—leaves modest margin for drop-out. The proposal's emphasis on within-listener predictions across tasks means that partial datasets from attrited participants retain some value, but the strongest tests of the individualized framework require complete Aim 1 → Aim 2 / 3 trajectories.

### Cross-species strategy

The absence of rabbit psychophysics means that the neural recordings cannot independently confirm that fluctuation cues actually drive behavior in the same species. However, this does not prevent the project from achieving its stated aims. The human component—individualized model predictions tested against human behavioral data—provides the primary validation of the framework, and the rabbit physiology serves to verify that the posited neural representations exist in a mammalian IC. This division of labor is feasible and scientifically coherent, even though it limits the directness of the physiological evidence.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethical provisions for both human participants and animals are appropriately specified and proportionate to the planned activities, and data-sharing practices are responsibly described; no material ethical concerns are identified in the proposal as submitted.**

**Human participants.** The human-subjects plan follows established institutional review procedures, with written consent, sound-delivery safeguards to limit loudness exposure, and provision for frequent breaks during testing sessions [1]. Use of arbitrary participant identifiers in reports protects confidentiality. The testing burden—approximately 12–15 hours per listener spread over 6–8 weeks [1]—is substantial but is distributed across multiple sessions, and the use of standard adaptive psychophysical procedures at moderate presentation levels (no presentation above 100 dB SPL [1]) keeps the acoustic exposure within well-established safe limits for auditory research. The inclusion of participants aged 18–80, including those with sensorineural hearing loss up to 60 dB HL, is appropriate for the scientific aims and does not introduce disproportionate risks; the screening battery (audiometry, bone conduction, tympanometry, DPOAEs, speech-in-noise testing [1]) provides a thorough baseline characterization that also serves to identify any contraindications to participation.

**Animal work.** The animal work is described with appropriate detail for a research proposal at this stage. Dutch-belted rabbits will undergo surgery for electrode implantation under anesthesia, receive postoperative analgesia, and be gradually habituated to the recording chair before data collection begins [1]. Veterinary supervision is specified for surgical procedures and ongoing health monitoring. The physiological task is passive listening—animals are not required to perform behavioral responses—which minimizes the burden relative to behavioral training paradigms. Electrode locations may be verified histologically at the end of the study, which is standard practice for confirming recording sites. The awake preparation avoids the confounds of anesthesia that alter auditory neural processing [6] while raising welfare considerations that the proposal addresses through the habituation protocol and monitoring of health and behavior. The use of four rabbits over 12–24 months each [1] represents a moderate animal-use commitment that is justified by the need to sample neurons with diverse acoustic tuning, modulation-transfer-function types, and binaural properties across numerous stimulus conditions.

**Data sharing.** Data and model sharing practices are specified: new models and collected data will be shared through the laboratory website, with software supplied as MATLAB or C components accompanied by compiler instructions, readme files, and executable examples [1]. Associated publications will describe parameter determination and validation, supporting reproducibility. Human data will be shared under arbitrary identifiers, consistent with the confidentiality protections described. These practices meet current expectations for open and responsible research. No environmental or operational hazards beyond routine laboratory activities are identified.

## Management

**The management plan is well-structured and draws on demonstrated institutional resources and consultant expertise; its main vulnerabilities are the absence of explicit condition prioritization for physiological recordings and modest margin for participant attrition, neither of which threatens delivery of the core aims.**

The five-year timeline integrates modeling, physiology, and psychophysics within each aim, which is a more coherent organizational strategy than sequential aim-by-aim execution [1]. Aim 1 listener characterization feeds directly into model construction, and the resulting individualized models then generate predictions for Aim 2 and Aim 3 tasks without further fitting—a logical workflow that prevents the psychophysical data collection from becoming disconnected from the modeling cycle [1]. Human participants must complete Aim 1 before progressing to later aims [1], ensuring that the predictive framework can be tested on the same individuals whose peripheral parameters were used to build the model.

The resource base is well documented. The laboratory maintains sound-attenuated booths, insert earphones, probe-tube microphones, local computational facilities, and access to university high-performance computing [1]. The awake-rabbit preparation with tetrode recording, daily probe-tube calibration, and two-hour recording sessions is an established workflow in this laboratory, with published output demonstrating sustained productivity over the planned recording durations [1] [3] [7]. No unusual equipment acquisition, facility construction, or external-access dependency is identified that could delay project initiation or create a single-point failure risk.

The consultant team fills gaps that the PI need not develop through new hiring. Expertise in audiology, hearing-loss psychophysics, phonetics, and theoretical psychoacoustics is available [1], which strengthens the project's capacity to address the full range of stimuli and listener populations.

Two aspects of the management plan warrant attention at the implementation stage. First, the proposal does not specify how stimulus conditions will be prioritized within the physiological recording plan if neuron yields fall below the ambitious 200-neurons-per-condition target [1]. Given the history of the preparation—Fan et al. (2021) recorded 163 neurons for a single masker type [3], and Agarwalla & Carney (2026) obtained 150 neurons from two rabbits [7]—the multiplicity of conditions across simultaneous masking, forward masking, and spectral-slope stimuli calls for a transparent triage strategy. In practice, conditions designed to discriminate fluctuation-based from energy-based accounts (e.g., equal-energy noise targets, specific notch widths, controlled masker-gap modulation) would logically receive priority, and even partial coverage would support the core model tests, albeit with reduced precision for less central comparisons.

Second, participant attrition is a manageable but non-trivial concern. The testing burden of 12–15 hours per listener over 6–8 weeks [1], combined with an eligibility range extending to age 80 and SNHL thresholds up to 60 dB HL [1], means that the targeted 30 SNHL listeners represent a sample without large contingency for drop-out. The minor numerical inconsistency between "15 age-matched controls and 30 SNHL listeners" and "a total recruitment of 40 listeners" [1] does not independently alter the management assessment but indicates that recruitment planning would benefit from reconciliation during project start-up.

Data and model sharing commitments—software as MATLAB or C components with documentation, data on the laboratory website—are specified at the output level [1] and align with the five-year schedule, with publications timed to follow parameter determination and validation. The animal-use plan of four rabbits over 12–24 months each provides the sustained recording access that the physiological aims require, and the laboratory's published success with this preparation supports confidence that the planned durations are realistic.

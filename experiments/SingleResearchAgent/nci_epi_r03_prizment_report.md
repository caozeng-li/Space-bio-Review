# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project aims to determine whether prediagnostic circulating levels and changes of six MHC class I-like proteins (MICA, MICB, ULBP1-4) are associated with subsequent colorectal cancer (CRC) risk in the ARIC cohort. It will also identify genetic and lifestyle determinants of these proteins and explore causal relationships through Mendelian randomization. The study's most valuable expected knowledge is a prospective description of ULBP protein trajectories and their determinants, a domain with little prior epidemiological evidence, and the analysis of protein changes over time, which could offer insights into dynamic immune surveillance before a CRC diagnosis.

The study's main supports are its foundation in the well-characterized, prospective ARIC cohort with existing proteomic, genomic, and long-term cancer follow-up data, and a research team with relevant expertise. The analytical plan includes appropriate methods and sensitivity analyses. The decisive limitation is the proposal's failure to engage with a prior publication from the same team (Wang et al., 2023), which already tested and found null associations for sMICA and sMICB with CRC in the same ARIC cohort using the same SOMAscan platform. This unacknowledged prior work substantially reduces the expected scientific contribution of the principal aim. The study's novelty relies heavily on the inclusion of ULBP proteins and a factor score, but the rationale for this approach is weakened by the null findings for its main components, and the Mendelian randomization component lacks evidence of adequate instrument strength.

**Conditional support.** The project asks a relevant question and has a feasible analytical home in the ARIC cohort, but the expected knowledge contribution is substantially constrained by prior results that directly challenge the central hypothesis. The proposed study's value therefore rests on its secondary aims and the unproven assumption that the ULBP proteins and longitudinal changes will yield different signals than the null findings for MICA and MICB. Support is contingent on a revised plan that clearly distinguishes the new work from the published ARIC null result and provides a compelling rationale for why the proposed analyses would yield different, informative conclusions. The current plan, while executable, does not justify this on its own.

## Scientific value

**Judgement: The central research question is biologically important, but the attainable knowledge is substantially constrained by prior published work that already tested and did not support the principal hypothesis in the same cohort.** The proposal asks whether prediagnostic circulating MHC class I-like proteins signal future CRC risk—a question grounded in the biology of NKG2D-mediated immune surveillance and tumor-cell recognition [1]. Understanding whether these immune-regulating proteins precede clinically detected CRC could, in principle, inform risk stratification or reveal early immune-escape mechanisms. The project would provide prospective epidemiological evidence for six proteins using repeated measurements, genotypes, and long-term cancer follow-up in the well-characterized ARIC cohort.

The decisive constraint is that the central hypothesis has already been addressed—and not supported—for the two best-characterized proteins, MICA and MICB, in the same ARIC cohort using the same SOMAscan platform. Wang et al. (2023), with Prizment as senior author, examined sMICA and sMICB measured at ARIC Visit 2 in 10,834 cancer-free participants and found "no associations of sMICA and sMICB levels (modeled as continuous variables per 1 RFU decrease, or in quartiles) with CRC risk" [2]. A contemporaneous hypothesis-free screen of 4,712 proteins against seven cancers in ARIC (Wang et al., 2026 preprint, including Prizment as co-author) similarly identified no FDR-significant associations for any MHC class I-like protein with CRC [3]. The proposal under review does not cite or distinguish itself from these prior null findings. As a result, the knowledge contribution expected from Aim 1a—associations of baseline MICA and MICB with subsequent CRC—is largely already reported, and that prior evidence weighs against the stated hypothesis.

**What the proposal adds beyond the 2023 paper are three elements:** the inclusion of four ULBP proteins (ULBP1–4), longitudinal change analyses using three time points spanning approximately 21 years (Aim 1b), and a factor-score approach combining all six proteins. The ULBP proteins have essentially no prospective epidemiological evidence linking circulating levels to cancer risk; establishing their prediagnostic trajectories and determinants would contribute descriptive knowledge to a sparse literature. The repeated-measures design offers a genuine opportunity to describe protein changes over time and to relate short-term and long-term trajectories to CRC, a dimension absent from the single-time-point 2023 analysis.

These additions must be weighed against their limitations. A factor score dominated by MICA and MICB—the proteins with established null associations—may dilute any signal present for the ULBP proteins. The proposal's anticipated inter-protein correlations of 0.4–0.55 [1] mean the factor will reflect shared variance, making attribution to specific biological mechanisms difficult. The long-term change analysis with only 56 CRC cases at Visit 5 [1] is explicitly exploratory and will yield imprecise estimates at best. The Mendelian randomization component is appropriately labeled exploratory but lacks the instrument validation and power evidence needed to support causal claims. The follow-on validation using standard methods such as ELISA is deferred to future work [1]; the current study will produce relative fluorescence units from the aptamer-based platform, which, although reproducible (median CV \<5%, inter-run Spearman ρ 0.87 [4]), do not represent absolute protein concentrations and have no published specificity validation for the ULBP family against reference methods.

Taken together, the attainable scientific contribution is incremental and largely descriptive. The project may characterize ULBP protein distributions and their genetic and lifestyle determinants, and the change analyses may generate hypotheses about dynamic immune surveillance that could be tested in independent cohorts. It is unlikely, however, to meaningfully advance CRC risk prediction or mechanistic understanding beyond the null findings already in the public domain. The biological importance of the question is therefore not matched by the new knowledge this specific study can reasonably be expected to produce.

**Rating: C (moderate). Confidence: B (high).**

## Innovation

**Judgement: The proposal adds modest increments to a question whose principal component has already been answered by prior work from the same team in the same cohort; the novelty relies on four under-studied proteins, a longitudinal design, and a factor-score approach, none of which is individually transformative.**

The proposal's most original conceptual feature is the shift from a single-time-point analysis of soluble MICA and MICB—already reported as null for CRC risk in ARIC by Wang et al. (2023) [2]—to a six-protein panel that additionally includes ULBP1–4, combined with repeated measurements spanning approximately 21 years. In principle, examining protein *changes* before diagnosis (Aim 1b) could reveal dynamic immune-escape signals that a single baseline measurement misses, and this temporal dimension represents the study's clearest methodological advance over the prior publication. The use of marginal structural Cox models with inverse-probability-of-treatment weights to address time-dependent confounding by BMI and smoking [1] applies a rigorous causal-inference framework to this longitudinal question, which is a thoughtful analytical choice for an observational repeated-measures design.

However, the incremental character of this novelty must be assessed against what is already known. The proposal does not engage with the Wang et al. findings, leaving the reader to infer what new knowledge the expanded protein set and change analyses would add beyond an already-reported null. The ULBP proteins enter the study without population-level evidence linking circulating concentrations to CRC risk; their inclusion is therefore better characterized as hypothesis-generating exploration than as a conceptually novel mechanistic advance grounded in prior epidemiological observation. The factor score that combines all six proteins is intended to capture a common signal, but with anticipated inter-protein correlations of 0.4–0.55 [1], the factor will be dominated by shared variance—likely reflecting the two proteins for which null associations are already established—making it difficult to attribute any observed association to a specific biological pathway or protein family member.

The Mendelian randomization component is described as exploratory and secondary, which is appropriate, but it does not itself constitute a substantial innovation. Single-sample instrumental-variable probit models within ARIC using genetic instruments yet to be identified [1] is a standard approach; the proposal does not introduce novel instrumental-variable methods, external pQTL resources, or a multi-cohort replication strategy that would distinguish it from conventional MR practice.

Compared with relevant independent work, the study adds a prospective, population-based dimension to a literature that has largely examined MHC class I-like proteins in the context of advanced disease or prognosis [1]. That shift from clinical to prediagnostic settings is a meaningful conceptual reorientation, but it is not unique to this proposal: the 2023 ARIC paper already made that shift for MICA and MICB. The remaining novelty therefore rests on the ULBP proteins and the longitudinal framework, both of which are reasonable but limited extensions rather than a departure from established lines of inquiry.

**Rating: C (moderate). Confidence: B (high).**

## Rigor

**Judgement: The analytical design is generally appropriate for the stated aims, but several features weaken the strength of inference, and the proposal's failure to address prior null findings from the same platform and cohort undermines the rationale for its central analysis strategy.**

The proposal employs a prospective nested case–control structure within the ARIC cohort, using plasma samples collected years before cancer diagnosis. Cox proportional-hazards models with adjustment for established CRC risk factors are appropriate for Aim 1a, and the planned sensitivity analyses—excluding early cases to address reverse causation, testing proportional-hazards assumptions, and handling missing data with inverse-probability weighting—reflect careful epidemiological practice [1]. The marginal structural model approach proposed for Aim 1b is a methodologically sound choice for repeated-measures data where time-varying covariates such as BMI and smoking may both confound and mediate protein–cancer associations [1]. The use of inverse-probability-of-treatment weights to address this time-dependent confounding is consistent with current best practice for observational longitudinal analyses.

**The factor-score strategy introduces inferential difficulties that the proposal does not adequately resolve.** Combining MICA, MICB, and four ULBP proteins into a single score assumes that the six proteins share a common signal relevant to CRC risk. Yet prior work from the same team in the same cohort already demonstrated null associations for MICA and MICB individually with CRC [2]. The proposal offers no explanation of how a composite of proteins with established null individual associations is expected to yield a different result, nor does it discuss whether a factor dominated by these two proteins would be interpretable if the ULBP proteins alone carried a signal. With anticipated inter-protein correlations of 0.4–0.55, the first factor will capture shared variance that may simply reproduce the null finding at a composite level, without discriminating among the biological roles of individual family members.

**The power calculations and case numbers impose additional constraints on inference.** The categorical power example for Aim 1a uses tertiles and a hazard ratio of 1.58, whereas the analysis description specifies quartiles [1]. This discrepancy is minor in isolation but suggests the submitted power estimates may not correspond precisely to the planned comparisons. More consequential is the limited power for the long-term change analysis in Aim 1b, where only 56 incident CRC cases remain at Visit 5 [1]. The proposal acknowledges this as exploratory, but the expectation of stable estimates from a model with time-varying weights and 56 events is unrealistic; any finding from this analysis will be too imprecise to support firm conclusions.

**The Mendelian randomization component is appropriately labeled exploratory but lacks critical feasibility evidence.** The proposal states that instruments are being identified within ARIC and replicated in external datasets, which means the instruments are not yet established [1]. No first-stage F-statistics or power calculations are provided for the instrumental-variable probit analysis. Given the MICA and MICB genes reside in the HLA region with its complex linkage disequilibrium structure, selecting valid instruments that satisfy the exclusion restriction is nontrivial, and the conventional F \> 10 threshold may be difficult to achieve with 360 CRC cases. The proposal acknowledges the use of F \> 10 as a criterion [1] but provides no evidence that this threshold is attainable.

**The SOMAscan platform offers reasonable reproducibility** for relative quantification, and the described QC procedures—hybridization-control normalization, pooled plasma controls in triplicate, split-specimen duplicates—are appropriate [1]. The investigators' own pilot work in 42 ARIC participants using SOMAscan version 3 yielded a median CV of 5%, Spearman correlation of 0.89, and intraclass correlation of 0.96 in blinded split samples [1]. The measurements remain in relative fluorescence units and await future validation against absolute-concentration methods [1]; the absence of published specificity data for the four ULBP aptamers against reference immunoassays is a measurement limitation, though one that is acknowledged and deferred to later work rather than a fatal design flaw at this hypothesis-generating stage.

**The most consequential limitation for rigor is the proposal's silence regarding the Wang et al. (2023) null findings.** Scientifically, the burden is on the investigators to explain why the same assay platform applied to the same cohort should now yield informative results when the two best-characterized proteins in the panel have already shown no association. Without this justification, the analytical plan—however well-structured in its individual components—rests on an unexamined premise that weakens the inferential chain from measurements to conclusions.

**Rating: C (moderate). Confidence: B (high).**

## Feasibility

**Judgement: The study is principally an analysis of existing data and specimens, and the core elements needed to complete the primary and secondary aims are already in place or underway. The project is therefore feasible within the proposed 24-month timeline, with the main uncertainty confined to the exploratory Mendelian randomization component, which lacks evidence of adequate instrument strength.**

The proposal describes a secondary analysis of the ARIC cohort that requires no new participant contact, specimen collection, or assay development. Plasma SOMAscan measurements for the six prespecified MHC class I-like proteins are already completed for Visit 3 (1993–1995) and Visit 5 (2011–2013), and Visit 2 (1990–1992) assays are described as underway [1]. Genotyping from GWAS and deep whole-genome sequencing through TOPMed and the Centers for Common Disease Genomics is available for approximately 12,000 participants [1]. Cancer ascertainment through 2015 yields 360 incident CRC cases at Visit 2, 306 at Visit 3, and 56 at Visit 5 [1]. Lifestyle, anthropometric, and clinical covariate data were collected at each visit as part of the original ARIC protocol. The project therefore inherits the considerable infrastructure investment already made in ARIC and the SOMAscan platform; no critical data collection step remains to be initiated or funded by this R03.

**The analytical tools are standard and well matched to the tasks.** The proposal specifies SAS and Stata for descriptive analyses, Cox regression, mixed models, and instrumental-variable probit estimation [1]. The marginal structural model approach for Aim 1b and the random-intercept, random-linear-time mixed models for Aim 2 are established methods for which documented implementations exist. The team includes expertise in molecular cancer epidemiology, computational genetics, and biostatistics [1], and the prior publications from this group—including the Wang et al. (2023) paper [2]—demonstrate hands-on capability with these specific datasets and analytical workflows.

**The 24-month schedule** allocates months 1–3 to IRB arrangements and data extraction, months 4–12 to Aim 1 analyses, months 13–18 to Aim 2, months 19–24 to the secondary Mendelian randomization aim, with manuscript preparation overlapping months 13–24 [1]. This sequencing is logical and allows the determinant analyses in Aim 2 to inform the genetic instrument selection needed for Aim 3. The principal dependency is the completion of Visit 2 SOMAscan assays, which are already in progress and for which the described QC procedures—including pooled plasma controls, split-specimen duplicates, and normalization against global reference standards [1]—provide a clear path to analysis-ready data.

**The SOMAscan platform has been extensively characterized.** The version 4 assay demonstrates a median total CV below 5% for plasma, median inter-run Spearman ρ of 0.87, and 90th-percentile CV of 10% [4]. The investigators' own pilot work in 42 ARIC participants using SOMAscan version 3 yielded a median CV of 5%, Spearman correlation of 0.89, and intraclass correlation of 0.96 in blinded split samples, with only one of the six proteins showing significant change over six to eight weeks [1]. These metrics indicate adequate reproducibility for the relative-quantification comparisons the study design requires.

**The one feasibility concern that cannot be fully resolved** concerns the Mendelian randomization component. The proposal states that genetic instruments are being identified within ARIC and replicated in external CRC datasets from dbGaP [1], which means they are not yet validated. The MICA and MICB genes reside in the HLA region on chromosome 6, where complex linkage disequilibrium patterns complicate instrument selection. With only 360 CRC cases for the baseline analysis and the conventional F \> 10 threshold as the stated criterion [1], instrument strength may prove inadequate. This aim is, however, explicitly secondary and exploratory; its uncertain feasibility does not threaten the primary aims. The power calculations for Aims 1a and 1b indicate adequate sensitivity to detect moderate effect sizes (HR 1.24–1.26 per standard deviation) for the factor-score analyses [1], and the much larger sample available for Aim 2 (thousands of cancer-free participants) provides ample precision for the determinant analyses.

**Rating: B (good). Confidence: B (high).**

## Ethics and safety

**Judgement: The project involves secondary analysis of existing, de-identified data and specimens with no new participant contact, presenting negligible risk to human subjects. The described data protection arrangements are proportionate and consistent with standard practice for this type of research.**

The proposal is exclusively a secondary analysis of data and stored plasma specimens already collected by the ARIC study. No new participant recruitment, contact, intervention, or specimen collection is planned [1]. The original ARIC enrolment and follow-up protocols obtained informed consent, and this project does not alter the consent framework under which those materials were gathered. Because the research does not involve living individuals directly, the ethical considerations centre on privacy, data security, and appropriate secondary use of existing biospecimens and linked health information.

The data-handling procedures described are fit for purpose. Investigators receive coded research identifiers; the linkage between these study IDs and direct personal identifiers is held exclusively by the ARIC Coordinating Center and is not shared with the analytical team [1]. This arrangement maintains a separation between the research data and re-identifying information that reduces the risk of inadvertent disclosure. The proposal further describes privacy protections covering data transmission, storage, and handling, and notes that descriptive and aggregate association results will be shared under NIH data-sharing policies while access to the underlying study dataset follows ARIC leadership and publication-committee policies [1]. These measures align with the data governance framework expected for a long-running cohort study whose materials are made available to collaborating investigators under institutional data-use agreements.

No additional biosafety, environmental, or animal-subject considerations arise from the proposed work. The laboratory assays—SOMAscan proteomic measurements—have already been performed or are underway on previously collected frozen plasma [1]; the current project undertakes statistical analysis of the resulting data. No hazardous materials, gene-editing technologies, or environmental releases are involved.

The proposal does not explicitly discuss whether the original ARIC consent documents anticipated secondary proteomic and genetic analyses of the type planned here, nor does it detail the specific IRB status of the current protocol beyond a general reference to IRB arrangements in months 1–3 [1]. For a secondary-use study relying on an established cohort with a documented governance structure, this level of detail is not unusual in a scientific narrative, and the absence of itemized consent-language review does not itself indicate an ethical deficiency. The key protections—de-identification, restricted access to linkage keys, and institutional oversight through the ARIC Coordinating Center and publication committee—are in place, and the activities fall within the scope of secondary health research for which these governance mechanisms were designed. The ethical risk profile is therefore low, and the described safeguards are commensurate.

**Rating: A (excellent). Confidence: A (very high).**

## Management

**Judgement: The team combines appropriate expertise, a realistic schedule, and established infrastructure, giving good confidence that the planned analyses can be completed as described, though the proposal provides limited detail on specific responsibilities and no formal risk-mitigation plan for its one material dependency.**

The project is led by Anna Prizment at the University of Minnesota, with co-investigators contributing molecular cancer epidemiology, computational genetics, and biostatistics expertise [1]. The team's recent publication record—including the Wang et al. (2023) analysis of sMICA and sMICB in ARIC using the same SOMAscan platform [2] and the hypothesis-free proteomic screen across seven cancers in the same cohort [3]—demonstrates direct, hands-on experience with the exact data resources and analytical workflows that this R03 proposes to use. No steep learning curve or unfamiliar methodological domain is evident for the primary analyses.

The 24-month schedule is appropriately sequenced. Months 1–3 cover IRB arrangements and data extraction; Aim 1 analyses occupy months 4–12; Aim 2, months 13–18; and the secondary Mendelian randomization aim, months 19–24, with manuscript preparation overlapping months 13–24 [1]. This phasing allows the determinant analyses in Aim 2 to inform the genetic instrument selection needed for Aim 3, and manuscript drafting runs concurrently with later analyses rather than being deferred to a post-hoc writing phase. The timeline assumes that all core data—SOMAscan measurements from Visits 2, 3, and 5; genotyping and sequencing; cancer ascertainment; and covariate data—are either already available or nearing completion [1].

The single material dependency is the ongoing Visit 2 SOMAscan assay, which uses an established commercial platform with documented QC procedures including pooled plasma controls, split-specimen duplicates, and normalization against global reference standards [1]. A delay in assay completion would cascade through the schedule, yet the proposal does not identify this as a risk or describe a contingency. The proposal names software platforms (SAS and Stata) appropriate to the analytical tasks [1] and describes the statistical methods at a level of detail sufficient to confirm a concrete implementation plan. The ARIC Coordinating Center provides the data management infrastructure, coded research identifiers, and publication-committee governance that support multi-investigator secondary analyses of this type [1].

What the proposal does not provide is an allocation of named investigators to specific aims, a description of team meeting or coordination mechanisms, or a formal risk register. For an R03 secondary analysis with a small investigator group and a 24-month timeline, the absence of a detailed project-management plan is not itself a material weakness—the scope is modest, the dependencies are few, and the team has a track record of delivering related work using the same resources. The combination of appropriate expertise, a realistic schedule, and established ARIC infrastructure supports the expectation that the work will be completed.

**Rating: B (good). Confidence: B (high).**

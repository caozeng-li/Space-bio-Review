# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project investigates why school-age children who use hearing aids continue to have difficulty understanding speech in noise, even when amplification provides good physical access to the speech signal. Through cross-sectional experiments and repeated assessments over 24 months, the research will examine how spectral resolution, individual frequency-weighting strategies, and susceptibility to competing talkers depend on selective attention, working memory, language, and aided audibility. The most valuable expected knowledge is a developmental account of these relationships, built on a single well-characterized cohort, that can guide more individualized amplification fitting and habilitation for children with hearing loss.

The strongest supporting evidence comes from the investigative team's established expertise in pediatric amplification and cognition, a productive track record, and extensive pilot data that demonstrate feasible measurement protocols and meaningful variability in the target constructs. The coherent theoretical framework, preregistration plans, and careful audibility quantification further strengthen the design. The main limit is the proximity of the proposed cross-sectional experiments to recently published or in-press work from the same team, which already reports several of the planned comparisons. Notably, Bosen et al. (2025) found no cognitive prediction of band-importance weights after controlling for age, which directly undercuts key hypotheses in Aim 2. The longitudinal follow-up remains valuable, but the knowledge increment of the immediate experiments is more modest than the proposal conveys.

The proposal is scientifically and clinically well-motivated, and its integrated longitudinal design is a genuine step beyond the team's prior cross-sectional studies. The main asset is the prospect of a rich multi-domain dataset that could clarify how sensory, cognitive, and linguistic development jointly shape real-world listening. Weakened novelty in the cross-sectional arms and partial reliance on age-confounded preliminary correlations do not eliminate the value of the work, particularly the longitudinal extensions, but they do reduce the likelihood that the proposed analyses will confirm the strongest versions of the stated hypotheses. The judgment of the current plan therefore rests on the longitudinal contribution: the approach is credible and the team is capable, yet the framing overstates the novelty of the immediate experiments.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

**Scientific value is high (B), moderated by a published finding that narrows the expected knowledge gain from Aim 2.** The project addresses a clinically and educationally important question: why children who wear hearing aids, despite early identification and fitting, continue to experience substantial difficulty understanding speech in the noisy environments where they learn, socialize, and develop [1]. The persistent SNR disadvantage documented in the team’s own pilot data — strong sentence recognition in quiet but markedly poorer performance in speech-shaped noise at +6 dB SNR for children with hearing loss (CHL) — motivates the work persuasively [1]. The central proposal to separate sensory, cognitive, and linguistic contributions to complex listening, and to model how these contributions change with development, is coherent and would, if realized, provide actionable knowledge for individualized fitting and habilitation [1], [1].

**The expected contributions are multi-faceted.** A successful project would deliver purified estimates of spectral resolution (Aim 1), individual perceptual weighting functions for speech (Aim 2), and susceptibility to informational masking (Aim 3) within a single well-characterized cohort, together with longitudinal evidence on how these measures co-develop with attention, working memory, language, and aided audibility across a two-year window. Such a dataset does not currently exist. The direct clinical implications — for example, understanding whether a child’s poor speech-in-noise performance reflects sensory limitation, attentional difficulty, or underdeveloped language knowledge — could guide the selection of hearing-aid signal-processing strategies, remote-microphone use, or targeted auditory training [1].

The framework’s separation of immediate audibility effects from cumulative developmental effects operating through language [1] is a conceptually useful distinction that goes beyond correlational descriptions of cognitive–sensory associations. The inclusion of an adult comparison group for the perceptual-weighting experiment (Experiment 2a, [1]) strengthens the developmental framing by anchoring mature performance, and the CHL–CNH comparisons across all aims provide the clinical contrast that gives the work its translational relevance.

**The principal limitation on scientific value is the challenge that recent published work from the same team poses to Aim 2’s central hypotheses.** Bosen et al. (2025) applied essentially the same filtered-word band-importance method proposed here to 42 children with normal hearing (CNH), ages 6–13, and reported developmental changes in weights at 2 and 4 kHz — a finding that supports the descriptive contribution of Aim 2. Critically, however, that study found that *none* of the vocabulary and cognitive tasks predicted frequency-importance weights after controlling for age [2]. This null result directly contradicts the proposal’s prediction that selective attention and language drive weighting differences, and it suggests that the proposal’s preliminary language-weight correlation (r = .41 at 2 kHz, [1]) may have been confounded by age rather than reflecting the hypothesized cognitive mechanism. The knowledge that Aim 2 can therefore deliver is more modest than the proposal envisions: it can replicate and extend the developmental weighting changes in a larger sample, characterize weighting in CHL for the first time (Experiment 2b, [1]), and track longitudinal stability or change (Experiment 2c, [1]), but it is unlikely to confirm the stated cognitive-mediation account.

This limitation is specific to Aim 2. The remaining aims retain genuinely valuable knowledge targets. Aim 1 will provide a cleaner spectral-ripple measure than the team’s prior spectro-temporal task, with explicit tests of cognitive mediation in speech recognition and of longitudinal prediction [1], [1]. Aim 3 will supply the longitudinal replication of masker-specific attention and audibility effects that Lalonde et al. (2023) explicitly called for [3], extending beyond their cross-sectional sample of 31 CNH and 41 CHL [3]. The overall package therefore remains scientifically valuable, but the evidence base indicates that the cognitive-weighting component — a central element of the unifying framework — is less likely to yield the relationships the proposal foregrounds.

## Innovation

**The innovation is moderate (C): the project systematically extends the team’s prior cross-sectional work with longitudinal designs and improved measurement, but recent publications from the team substantially overlap with the proposed cross-sectional aims and, in one case, directly contradict a central hypothesis.**

The proposal’s ambition to integrate spectral resolution, perceptual weighting, and informational masking within a single developmental framework is appropriate and the longitudinal follow-up in each aim represents a genuine advance beyond the team’s published cross-sectional studies. The specific methodological refinements — a pure spectral-ripple task with narrowband carriers and level roving to replace the prior spectro-temporal measure [1], a rapid individual weighting procedure that reduces assessment time from four hours to under 30 minutes [1], and a within-subjects interstimulus-interval manipulation to probe working-memory demands [1] — are well-justified and would produce data of higher specificity than the team’s earlier work. These improvements would not resolve the underlying overlap with published findings, however, and the relationship between the proposed cross-sectional experiments and the team’s recent publications weakens the novelty claim most acutely for Aim 2.

**Aim 1 (spectral resolution) is an incremental methodological advance on an established finding.** Kirby et al. (2019) tested 24 CHL aged 6–13 and showed that nonverbal intelligence and visual working memory were associated with spectro-temporal ripple thresholds after controlling for age [4], [4]. The proposal acknowledges the limitations of that prior task — it could not separate spectral from temporal information, used a broadband carrier without level roving, and lacked a CNH comparison — and addresses each with the new spectral-ripple design [1]. The core question of whether cognitive abilities contribute to spectral discrimination has, however, already been answered affirmatively. The novel contribution lies in the planned mediation analysis (does spectral resolution mediate cognitive effects on speech recognition?), the CNH comparison, and the longitudinal prediction of speech-in-noise outcomes. These are worthwhile incremental advances, but the central empirical demonstration is established.

**Aim 2 (perceptual weighting) presents the most acute novelty concern.** Bosen et al. (2025), co-authored by the PI, used the same filtered-word band-importance method proposed here in 42 CNH aged 6–13 and reported developmental changes in weights at 2 and 4 kHz [2]. That paper explicitly states: “None of the vocabulary and cognitive tasks predicted frequency importance weights after controlling for age” [2]. This finding directly contradicts the proposal’s hypothesis that selective attention and language predict band-importance weighting [1]. The proposal’s preliminary correlation between language and 2-kHz weight (r = .41, [1]) did not control for age, and the Bosen et al. result demonstrates that age confounded that relationship. The proposed Experiment 2a — which compares 80 CNH with 25 adults on the same task and tests the same cognitive predictors — therefore largely replicates the Bosen et al. design and analysis, but with a hypothesis that the published evidence already challenges. The extension to CHL (Experiment 2b) and the longitudinal tracking of weights (Experiment 2c) remain contributions that have not been published elsewhere, but the rationale for expecting cognitive predictors of weighting — a central element of the Aim’s motivation — is weakened.

**Aim 3 (informational masking) overlaps substantially with published work but responds to an explicit call for longitudinal data.** Lalonde et al. (2023) compared 31 CNH and 41 CHL on speech recognition in speech-shaped noise versus a two-talker masker, and tested vocabulary, working memory, attention, and aided audibility as predictors [3], [3], [3]. That study showed that attention predicted two-talker performance for CNH but not CHL, while vocabulary and aided audibility predicted CHL performance, and noted that “larger samples of children with hearing loss and/or longitudinal data are needed” [3]. The proposal’s Aim 3 directly responds to that call with a larger sample (80 CNH, 80 CHL) and a 24-month longitudinal follow-up. The cross-sectional Experiments 3a and 3b are therefore primarily replication with increased power; the incremental contribution is methodological (interleaved adaptive tracks for within-subject comparison of masker conditions, [1]) and longitudinal (Experiment 3c). The replication itself has scientific value given the modest size of the original samples, but it does not constitute a novel research question.

In summary, each aim contains a longitudinal component that extends beyond published work and a cross-sectional component that overlaps with the team’s existing publications. The balance between replication, incremental improvement, and genuinely new investigation is reasonable for a coherent programme, but the framing overstates the novelty of the immediate experiments. The Bosen et al. null result for cognitive prediction of weighting is the most consequential external finding: it challenges a central hypothesis in Aim 2 and suggests that the proposal’s preliminary evidence for that hypothesis reflects an age confound rather than the posited attention-language mechanism. This does not eliminate the value of the longitudinal weighting data or the CHL characterization, but it does reduce the likelihood that the proposed cognitive-mediation account will be confirmed and narrows the knowledge increment of the cross-sectional component of Aim 2.

## Rigor

**Rigor is high (B), supported by careful measurement and analytical planning, but the 24-month longitudinal window with three time-points constrains developmental inference, and key cross-sectional mediation claims rely on temporal ordering that the design does not establish.**

The proposal incorporates several design features that strengthen the credibility of its intended inferences. Participant groups are defined by explicit audiological criteria, with CNH and CHL matched on age, sex, and maternal education [1]. Exclusion of cochlear-implant users and children with unilateral hearing loss reduces mechanistic heterogeneity, and the nonverbal-cognition and word-recognition floors ensure that participants can engage with the experimental tasks [1]. Aided audibility — a central construct in the CHL analyses — is directly quantified through electroacoustic verification at each visit, yielding the aided Speech Intelligibility Index (SII) and deviation from prescriptive targets, rather than relying on assumed fitting quality [1]. This is a methodologically important choice given the team’s prior finding that nearly half of studied children did not have optimally audible fittings [1].

**The measurement battery addresses the construct overlap inherent in the proposal’s own framework.** The framework acknowledges that ostensibly sensory tasks (spectral ripple, band-weighting, masked sentence recognition) recruit attention, working memory, and language to varying degrees [1]. The proposal responds by including independent cognitive and linguistic measures — PPVT-IV and WASI Vocabulary for language, Flanker and Switcher tasks for selective attention, AWMA subtests for verbal and visuospatial working memory — and by selecting visual or nonverbal tasks where possible to reduce confounding by audibility and language [1]. This multi-domain assessment is necessary for the planned mediation and path models, and the choice to combine highly correlated measures into construct Z-scores [1] is a defensible approach to managing collinearity before hypothesis testing [1].

**The analytical strategy is thoughtfully specified.** Linear mixed models with participant intercepts will accommodate repeated conditions and visits, and the proposal allows for random slopes and alternative covariance structures selected by information criteria [1]. Path models will distinguish predictors, mediators, outcomes, and controls, with scientific reasoning guiding variable reduction [1]. Power calculations based on 1,000 simulated datasets with alpha .05 and 80% power are reported for each experiment, and the detectable effect sizes — e.g., d \> .2 for group differences in Experiment 1a, r \> .3 for three-predictor models across multiple experiments — are transparent and appropriately modest for the planned sample sizes [1], [1], [1]. Preregistration of experimental designs and analyses, blinded reliability scoring of speech responses, and planned release of de-identified data, experimental software, and analysis code through OSF and GitHub further support reproducibility [1], [1].

**Three inferential limitations warrant attention, and their combined effect is to constrain what the design can confidently conclude.**

*Developmental inference from three time-points.* The longitudinal arms collect data at baseline, 12 months, and 24 months [1], [1], [1]. With only three waves, growth models are restricted to linear trajectories. The constructs under study — selective attention, working memory, spectral weighting — develop gradually across the 6–14 year age range, and their trajectories may include periods of acceleration, plateau, or non-linear change. The proposal acknowledges this implicitly by noting that older children may approach asymptote [1], but does not discuss whether three time-points provide sufficient resolution to distinguish linear from non-linear developmental patterns. The cross-sectional age range in each aim provides between-subject developmental information, but the longitudinal component — which is the proposal’s main advance beyond the team’s published work — may detect only the linear component of what could be more complex trajectories.

*Temporal ordering in cross-sectional mediation models.* Several central hypotheses are framed as mediation claims: that spectral resolution mediates cognitive effects on speech recognition (Aim 1), that attention and language predict weighting patterns (Aim 2), and that audibility has indirect effects on masked recognition through language (Aim 3). Path analysis on cross-sectional data cannot establish temporal precedence — it cannot distinguish whether spectral resolution mediates cognitive effects on speech recognition, or whether speech recognition experience shapes both spectral resolution and cognitive performance, or whether a third variable drives all three. The longitudinal arms partially address this by allowing baseline measures to predict later outcomes, but the cross-sectional mediation analyses, which form a substantial part of the proposed hypothesis tests, will yield associational rather than causal evidence regardless of the path-model specification.

*Construct validity of the interstimulus-interval manipulation.* Experiment 1b manipulates the retention interval in the spectral-ripple task (0.4, 1.2, and 3 seconds) to probe working-memory demands, predicting that longer intervals will strengthen associations with attention and working memory [1]. However, the proposal itself describes a plausible alternative: children may judge each interval independently as containing or lacking a feature, rather than comparing retained representations across intervals. If children adopt this strategy, the interval manipulation does not engage working memory in the intended way. The proposal treats a null interval effect as informative — “Weak interval effects would therefore help identify a task less dependent on cognition” [1] — which is scientifically reasonable, but the manipulation lacks an independent validation that the longer intervals actually increase memory demands as hypothesized. A null result could indicate either that spectral discrimination is not memory-dependent or that the manipulation failed to vary memory load; the design cannot distinguish these interpretations.

These limitations do not undermine the proposal’s capacity to generate useful associational evidence and descriptive developmental data. The longitudinal tracking of spectral resolution, perceptual weighting, and informational masking within a single cohort, with careful audibility and cognitive measurement, will produce findings that the field currently lacks. The limitations do, however, mean that the strongest causal claims in the proposal — particularly those framed as mediation — should be interpreted as associational patterns that are consistent or inconsistent with causal hypotheses, rather than as confirmatory tests of causal pathways.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility is good (B): the recruitment plan builds on established participant databases at two experienced sites, shared measures and a staggered schedule make efficient use of resources, and the team has a strong track record in the proposed methods. The main risks are an optimistic attrition allowance and the cumulative testing burden on the longitudinal cohort, neither of which appears prohibitive given the team’s experience.**

The overall recruitment target — approximately 150 children with normal hearing (CNH), 200 children with hearing loss (CHL), and 25 normal-hearing adults — is to be met across five years from Boys Town National Research Hospital and the University of Iowa [1], [1]. Both sites maintain existing participant databases and have extensive experience in pediatric audiology, cognition, language testing, and longitudinal data collection [1]. The PI and team have published multiple studies drawing on these populations, including the pilot and prior work described in the proposal [1], [1], [1], [1], and the independent evidence confirms a productive track record of recruitment and retention in this clinical group [3], [4], [2]. The numbers are substantial but not unrealistic for two well-established research centers over five years.

**The design shares participants across experiments to enhance efficiency.** For example, the 80 CNH and 80 CHL in Aim 1’s cross-sectional experiments also supply the baseline for the longitudinal Experiment 1c, and the CHL cohort in Aim 3 is reused for the longitudinal Experiment 3c [1], [1], [1]. This reuse reduces the total number of distinct children who must be recruited, lowering the recruitment pressure relative to what independent samples for each experiment would require. The staggered schedule — Aim 1 data collection in years 1–3, Aim 2 in years 2–4, Aim 3 in years 3–5 — prevents all aims from competing for participants simultaneously and spreads the testing load across the grant period [1], [1].

The shared assessment battery further supports feasibility. Standardized measures of vocabulary (PPVT-IV, WASI Vocabulary), selective attention (Flanker, Switcher), and working memory (AWMA subtests) are administered across aims, and audiometric thresholds, electroacoustic verification, and aided SII are collected at each visit for CHL [1], [1]. This common core means that a child recruited into one aim can contribute data to others without extensive additional testing, and the longitudinal follow-ups repeat these measures rather than introducing entirely new protocols [1], [1], [1].

**Two practical risks moderate the feasibility assessment.** First, the proposal assumes approximately 10% attrition [1]. In pediatric longitudinal studies involving clinical populations — children with hearing loss who already attend audiology, medical, and sometimes educational support appointments — attrition of 15–25% per year is common even in well-resourced studies. Over a two-year follow-up with three visits, cumulative dropout could exceed 30%. The recruitment totals of 150 CNH and 200 CHL provide some buffer above the per-experiment targets of 80 per group, and the proposal explicitly notes allowance for attrition and aging out [1], but the buffer is modest relative to realistic loss rates.

Second, the longitudinal CHL cohort participates across all three aims. A child entering at age 6 and completing the full protocol would experience the spectral-ripple tasks of Aim 1 (Experiments 1a and 1b), the filtered-word weighting procedure of Aim 2 (Experiment 2b), and the masked sentence-recognition tasks of Aim 3 (Experiment 3b), plus repeated cognitive and language testing, at each of three time-points [1]. The total burden table reports approximately 2,100 data-collection hours across 955 subject visits [1]. The Pediatric AZ-Bio sentences appear in both Aims 1 and 3, raising the possibility of material-specific practice effects that could reduce sensitivity to developmental change. The proposal does not fully address how fatigue, practice, or selective attrition of the most heavily tested children will be monitored and managed across the longitudinal sequence.

Neither of these concerns is decisive. The team’s demonstrated ability to recruit and retain pediatric hearing-aid users, the shared infrastructure across two sites, and the staggered timeline provide a credible operational basis. The feasibility rating reflects a plan that is essentially sound but faces realistic execution challenges that the proposal’s attrition assumptions and cumulative-burden discussion do not fully acknowledge.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety are excellent (A): the ethical framework is clearly specified, proportionate to the described minimal-risk activities, and supported by institutional review arrangements, explicit risk-mitigation measures, and a responsible data-management plan.**

The proposal addresses ethical requirements across four domains: participant welfare during experimental procedures, informed consent and clinical communication, data protection and sharing, and institutional oversight. Each is handled with a level of detail and precaution that matches the study's classification as minimal risk.

**Participant protection during auditory testing** is addressed through hardware and software safeguards that limit signal levels below potentially damaging thresholds [1]. This is a concrete, verifiable control rather than a general statement of intent, and it is appropriate for a study in which children with hearing loss will listen to speech and noise stimuli at controlled presentation levels (typically 65 dB SPL, [1], [1], [1]) through headphones or in sound field. The proposal also anticipates the specific discomfort that can arise from probe-microphone measurements during electroacoustic verification, noting that the flexible tube can be removed and repositioned if a child reports discomfort [1]. This attention to a routine but potentially aversive procedure reflects practical experience with pediatric testing and a proportionate approach to minimizing distress.

**Informed consent and clinical feedback** procedures are described. Children and caregivers will complete applicable consent and assent documentation, with questions addressed at study visits [1]. The inclusion criterion requiring at least one hearing parent who uses spoken English at home [1] ensures that consent discussions can proceed in the family's primary language. The proposal goes further by offering, with caregiver permission, to share clinically relevant audiological, language, or cognitive findings with the child's care or education team [1]. This is an ethically commendable provision: it acknowledges that the study's comprehensive assessments may uncover information with direct clinical or educational relevance, and it creates a structured pathway for that information to reach professionals who can act on it, without compelling disclosure.

**Data management and privacy protections** are appropriately specified. Research records and audio-video recordings will be protected by access controls and password-protected storage, and published reports will not identify individual children [1]. De-identified data and documentation will be shared through the Open Science Framework under data-use agreements that restrict re-identification, require secure use, and mandate return or destruction of data after analysis [1]. Experimental and analysis code will be released through the Boys Town GitHub repository [1]. These arrangements balance the scientific commitment to reproducibility and data sharing — which is strengthened by the proposal's preregistration plans [1] — with the need to protect the confidentiality of a potentially identifiable clinical pediatric sample. The data-use agreements add a contractual layer to the technical de-identification measures, which is appropriate given that the dataset will combine audiological, cognitive, linguistic, and demographic variables from a relatively small clinical population.

**Institutional oversight** will follow a single-IRB arrangement for the two participating sites (Boys Town National Research Hospital and the University of Iowa, [1]). A single IRB is efficient for multi-site research and ensures consistent ethical review across locations. The proposal characterizes the study as minimal risk, a designation that is consistent with the nature of the procedures: behavioral listening tasks, standardized cognitive and language assessments, non-invasive electroacoustic hearing-aid measurements, and routine audiometry. No investigational devices, drugs, or interventions are involved. The study does not alter children's clinical management; CHL participants use their personal hearing aids at their ordinary settings for most testing [1], with the hearing-aid simulator in Experiment 2b being the specified exception [1].

No material ethical or safety concern is identified. The combination of technical safeguards for auditory exposure, explicit plans for managing procedural discomfort, structured consent and clinical-feedback pathways, de-identification with contractual data-use controls, and coordinated institutional review provides a thorough and proportionate ethical framework for the proposed work.

## Management

**Management is good (B): the investigative team possesses the requisite expertise and track record, shared measures and a two-site structure support coordination, and the five-year schedule distributes work sensibly across aims, though the interdependence of longitudinal follow-ups creates coordination demands that the proposal acknowledges only in outline.**

The project is led by an established principal investigator with deep expertise in pediatric amplification, speech perception, and the cognitive and linguistic factors that shape listening outcomes in children with hearing loss. The team's published record — including the Kirby et al. (2019) spectro-temporal discrimination study, the Lalonde et al. (2023) masking study, and the Bosen et al. (2025) frequency-weighting study — demonstrates sustained productivity in the specific experimental paradigms and clinical populations proposed here [4], [3], [2]. This record provides confidence that the team can execute the planned spectral-ripple, band-importance, and masked sentence-recognition protocols, and that the analytical approaches (linear mixed models, path analysis, growth modeling) are within the group's established methodological repertoire.

**The operational design incorporates features that support efficient delivery.** A shared core assessment battery — including PPVT-IV, WASI Vocabulary, Flanker and Switcher attention tasks, AWMA working-memory subtests, and standardized audiometric and electroacoustic verification for CHL — is administered across all three aims [1], [1]. This common core reduces duplication, ensures that data collected for one experiment can contribute to others, and simplifies staff training and quality-control procedures across the two participating sites. Participant reuse across experiments further reduces the total recruitment load: for example, the CHL cohort recruited for Aim 1's cross-sectional experiments supplies the baseline for the longitudinal Experiment 1c, and the CHL cohort in Aim 3 is followed longitudinally in Experiment 3c [1], [1]. These efficiencies are well-judged for a multi-experiment package with substantial sample-size requirements.

**The five-year schedule distributes data collection and manuscript preparation in a logical staggered sequence** that prevents all aims from competing for participants and staff attention simultaneously [1], [1]:

| Activity | Years 1–2 | Year 3 | Years 4–5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aim 1 cross-sectional (1a, 1b) | Data collection | Manuscripts | — |
| Aim 1 longitudinal (1c) | Data collection begins | Data collection completes | Manuscripts |
| Aim 2 cross-sectional (2a, 2b) | — | Data collection | Manuscripts (Year 4) |
| Aim 2 longitudinal (2c) | — | Data collection begins | Data collection → manuscript (Year 5) |
| Aim 3 cross-sectional (3a, 3b) | — | Data collection begins | Manuscripts (Year 5) |
| Aim 3 longitudinal (3c) | — | — | Data collection |

The cascade from Aim 1 through Aim 3 is logical: spectral-resolution and cognitive measures (Aim 1) are collected first and can inform the subsequent weighting (Aim 2) and masking (Aim 3) experiments, while the later aims avoid competing for the same longitudinal participants during their early-visit windows. This sequencing is appropriate for the proposed scope.

The proposal also documents commitments to measurement standardization and reproducibility infrastructure that support management quality: preregistration of experimental designs and analyses, blinded reliability scoring of speech responses, electroacoustic verification at each CHL visit, and planned release of de-identified data, experimental software, and analysis code through OSF and GitHub [1], [1]. These provisions establish clear quality benchmarks and make the research outputs auditable.

**The main coordination challenge is the interdependence of the longitudinal arms.** The CHL participants who enter Aim 1's longitudinal Experiment 1c also contribute to Aim 2 and Aim 3 follow-ups, meaning that retention failures or scheduling delays in one aim propagate across the others. The proposal's staggered schedule manages this temporal overlap, but the coordination across two sites, multiple experimental protocols, and repeated visits for the same children over a 24-month window requires attentive project management that the proposal describes only at the level of the broad timeline. This is not a deficiency that threatens feasibility — the team's experience with multi-year pediatric studies argues against it — but it is an operational dependency that the management documentation addresses at a higher level of generality than the technical methods.

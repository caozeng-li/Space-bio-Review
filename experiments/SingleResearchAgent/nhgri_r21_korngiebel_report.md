# Project review

## Overall assessment

This proposal addresses the timely and practical challenge of electronically returning heterogeneous genetic test results to patients through portals. The core research objects are the informational, emotional, and logistical needs of patients when receiving pharmacogenomic, hereditary condition, and cancer-risk findings, as well as negative results, via an EHR portal. The most valuable expected knowledge consists of empirically grounded, patient-driven electronic report prototypes and a refined “Points to Consider” ethical framework. This work will provide a crucial, systematic evidence base to guide the design of future portal-based genetic result communication, moving beyond a one-size-fits-all approach.

Key strengths include a well-motivated research design that systematically compares genetic versus non-genetic results, samples across a continuum of result complexity, and crucially includes the perspectives of portal non-users recruited from a safety-net clinic. The investigative team is highly qualified, the ethical framework is robust, and institutional resources are strong. Material limitations that affect the judgement are the compressed two-year timeline with little contingency, the thin within-category sampling for Aim 2 which limits its exploratory power, and the restriction to English-speaking participants, a gap that particularly constrains the equity-focused insights the study otherwise promises.

The incremental but genuine contributions of patient-driven prototypes, a refined ethical framework, and empirical comparisons across result types and user populations are well-served by a generally sound qualitative UCD methodology. The limitations in sampling breadth, schedule tightness, and the simulated rather than real-world testing environment constrain the strength and equity dimensions of the conclusions. These trade-offs are acknowledged and largely intrinsic to an exploratory R21-scale project. When weighed together, the study’s value and methodological core are sufficient to meet its stated formative goals, making it a worthwhile investment.

## Scientific value

The project addresses a timely and practically important gap with contributions that are incremental but genuine. The central question—what patients need and prefer when heterogeneous genetic test results are returned electronically, and how portal report templates should be designed accordingly—sits squarely at the intersection of the 21st Century Cures Act’s electronic-access mandate and the interpretive complexity of genomic medicine. The proposal’s intended products are patient-driven electronic report prototypes for the Epic-based eCare portal, a refined ethical Points to Consider framework, and empirical evidence comparing patient experiences across genetic and non-genetic results, across four genetic-result categories, and across portal users and non-users. These outputs are explicitly positioned as formative material for subsequent clinical and implementation studies [1].

The attainable knowledge is bounded but valuable. Korngiebel’s prior published work at the same institution, drawing on 59 interviews, has already established that patients want summary coversheets, jargon-free language, clinician notes, and smartphone-compatible access [2]. The present proposal extends that foundation to domains the prior work did not cover and that a recent scoping review by Petrovskaya et al. identified as among the least-studied areas—only two studies focused on patient preferences for mode and timing of result release [3].

The three aims advance knowledge on distinct fronts. Aim 1’s direct within-subject comparison of genetic and non-genetic portal-result experiences provides evidence about what, if anything, distinguishes the experience and support needs for genetic information—a question the prior scoping review found barely addressed [3]. Aim 2’s systematic sampling across pharmacogenomic, hemochromatosis, colorectal-cancer-risk, and negative findings creates an empirical platform for understanding how result characteristics shape concern thresholds and acceptable delivery arrangements [1]. Aim 3’s inclusion of portal non-users recruited through Harborview Medical Center’s safety-net clinic directly addresses a limitation Korngiebel acknowledged in her 2022 paper: that prior work sampled only English-speaking portal users at a single academic center [2].

The continuum-of-arrangements framework that the study will investigate—ranging from fully electronic return to personal delivery with supplementary portal access—provides practical guidance that moves beyond a simplistic electronic-versus-in-person dichotomy [1]. By asking which results fit which arrangements from patients’ perspectives, the study can inform institutional decisions that currently rest on clinician intuition (where prior work showed clinicians themselves disagree about which results should count as sensitive) [1]. The prototypes, including both Epic-style designs for immediate practical utility and blue-sky versions for platform-agnostic guidance, offer concrete outputs that health-information-technology professionals can evaluate, rather than only a set of general written recommendations [1].

The ethical Points to Consider framework, organized around beneficence/nonmaleficence, respect for autonomy, and justice/equity, provides a defensible, iteratively refinable structure [1]. This is proportionate to the exploratory, developmental character of the project: it does not promise a validated instrument but a structured tool to guide portal design decisions that the study evidence directly informs.

The study’s value is consequently specific and real, not speculative. It will produce knowledge that neither Korngiebel’s earlier work nor the more recent MyCancerGene digital portal development by Trieu et al. (which developed a cancer-genetics portal without comparing result types or including non-users) [4] has supplied. A workshop or committee asked to decide whether to invest in electronic genetic-result templates would be substantially better informed by this work than by current evidence.

## Innovation

The proposal’s innovations are well-justified increments rather than radical departures, and the framing is appropriately modest for an exploratory R21 project. The advance lies in the combination of three features that the prior literature—including the PI’s own work—has not yet brought together: a direct within-subject comparison of genetic and non-genetic portal result experiences (Aim 1), systematic sampling across four genetic-result categories stratified by anticipated concern level (Aim 2), and the deliberate inclusion of portal non-users alongside experienced users in usability testing (Aim 3).

The genetic-versus-non-genetic comparison addresses a genuine gap. A recent scoping review by Petrovskaya et al. found that patient preferences for mode and timing of result release were among the least-discussed areas in the reviewed studies [3]. Asking the same participants to reflect on both types of results permits evidence about whether the informational and emotional needs that patients identify are specific to genetic information or reflect generic portal usability concerns. The earlier Korngiebel interview study established patient preferences for summary coversheets and jargon-free language [2], but that work did not separate the genetic from the non-genetic experience within individuals. The MyCancerGene digital portal development by Trieu et al. focused exclusively on cancer genetics and did not compare result types or include non-users [4]. Aim 1 therefore supplies a direct empirical comparison that has been called for but not yet conducted.

The Aim 2 sampling across pharmacogenomic, hemochromatosis, colorectal-cancer-risk, and negative findings operationalizes the heterogeneity that the background section rightly emphasizes [1]. The four categories provide systematic variation in anticipated concern, family implications, and treatability, moving beyond the binary classification of “sensitive” versus “routine” that prior clinician-focused work found to be contested [1]. This design allows the study to test whether concern thresholds, information needs, and acceptable delivery arrangements differ by result type, rather than importing an a priori classification. The proposal explicitly treats these categories as starting points to be refined through the data [1], which is an appropriate epistemological stance for exploratory work.

The inclusion of portal non-users, recruited through Harborview Medical Center’s Family Medicine Clinic—a safety-net setting with relatively low eCare enrollment and substantial minority representation—is the third incremental but important contribution [1]. In her 2022 paper, Korngiebel acknowledged that the sample was limited to English-speaking portal users at a single academic center and observed that underrepresented groups might disproportionately be non-users [2]. Aim 3 directly addresses that limitation by building non-user perspectives into the usability testing design.

The prototypes themselves contribute methodological as well as practical innovation. Producing both Epic-style and blue-sky designs provides platform-specific guidance for the widely used Epic environment while also generating transferable design principles applicable to other EHR settings [1]. The continuum-of-arrangements framework, which explicitly allows findings to indicate that particular results should be delivered personally with the portal serving a supplementary function, avoids the simplistic “electronic versus in-person” dichotomy that has limited prior guidance [1]. Taken together, these innovations do not represent a radical conceptual leap, but they form a coherent package of extensions that the prior evidence, including the PI’s own work, has identified as needed.

## Rigor

The qualitative and UCD methods are generally sound and well-matched to the project’s exploratory, formative goals, but three material limitations constrain the strength of the inferences the study can support. The overall design—qualitative interviews followed by iterative usability testing—appropriately sequences needs-discovery before prototype refinement and reflects established user-centered design practice [1].

**Qualitative methods (Aims 1 and 2).** The qualitative core employs semistructured interviews with directed content and thematic analysis, dual coding with consensus resolution, and Atlas.ti support [1]. These are standard, defensible procedures for exploratory interview studies. The recruitment strategy seeks variation by age, gender, and race/ethnicity, with explicit targets (e.g., at least 13 non-White or Hispanic participants in Aims 1 and 2, and at least 28 in Aim 3) [1]. Saturation monitoring is built into the design: recruitment continues until diversity targets are met and major themes reach idea saturation, up to the planned ceilings [1]. The interview protocols will be piloted and reviewed by the advisory board before data collection, and Aim 2 protocols will be informed by Aim 1 findings [1].

**Usability testing design (Aim 3).** The usability testing design has several commendable features. Three rounds of 28 participants each (total 84), equally divided between portal users and non-users, with no participant returning in a later round, avoids contamination between design iterations [1]. Counterbalanced presentation order addresses learning effects. The problem-discovery sample-size rationale draws on established usability engineering methods and estimates a minimum discovery rate above 98% for each participant type [1]. Think-aloud cognitive interviews capture both task performance and experiential dimensions [1]. External consultant heuristic walkthroughs between rounds 2 and 3 provide an independent quality check [1]. This is a well-structured iterative design that should produce meaningful prototype refinements.

**Limitation 1: Thin within-category sampling for Aim 2.** Aim 2’s ten participants per result category is thin, even for qualitative work aimed at identifying themes rather than measuring prevalence. The proposal further subdivides these small cells by age, gender, and race/ethnicity [1]. For a study that aims to distinguish concern thresholds, information needs, and acceptable delivery arrangements across pharmacogenomic, hemochromatosis, colorectal-cancer-risk, and negative findings [1], ten interviews per category may not reach saturation within each subgroup, particularly where participant experience is varied. The proposal acknowledges that these categories are starting points to be tested and refined [1], but limited within-category data constrains how confidently between-result-type distinctions can be drawn from the data rather than from the a priori schema. This limitation weakens Aim 2’s stated contribution of providing evidence about which results fit which delivery arrangements.

**Limitation 2: English-only restriction.** The English-only restriction, while pragmatically justified by eCare’s current language availability [1], limits the justice and equity dimension of the ethical framework. Aim 3 recruits non-users through Harborview’s Family Medicine Clinic, a safety-net setting with substantial minority representation [1], yet the very patients with limited English proficiency who are most likely to face access barriers are excluded. This attenuates the study’s ability to speak to the justice-and-equity questions the Points to Consider framework explicitly asks about accommodating limited technology literacy and health-literacy levels [1]. The proposal recognizes this as future work, but the restriction diminishes one of the study’s novel contributions relative to the PI’s prior work [2].

**Limitation 3: Prototype testing in simulated conditions.** The prototypes are mockups evaluated through cognitive walkthroughs where participants react to hypothetical scenarios, not functional portal integrations delivering real results [1]. This is acknowledged and appropriate for a formative R21: the proposal explicitly positions implementation studies as subsequent steps [1]. Nevertheless, the gap between simulated prototype review and actual result receipt bounds what the study can conclude about emotional responses, information-seeking behavior, and downstream actions. This is not a flaw in the design but a scope limitation intrinsic to the project’s stage.

## Feasibility

The expertise and institutional resources are strong, but the sequential design, compressed two-year timeline, and thin within-category sampling create material execution risks that require active management.

The institutional context is a genuine asset. UW Medicine's Epic-based eCare infrastructure, the Clinical and Translational Informatics Services (CTIS) for identifying eligible patients, and the Harborview Family Medicine Clinic for recruiting non-users provide credible access to the target populations [1]. The investigative team combines the necessary expertise: Korngiebel has direct experience with formative research, electronic prototyping, and usability testing from the prior 59-interview study [2]; Robins contributes extensive qualitative-methods experience; Fullerton provides genomic bioethics expertise; and Amendola adds genetic counseling and clinical-sequencing experience [1]. The ten-member advisory structure—spanning clinical genetics, laboratory-medicine informatics, ambulatory IT, safety-net primary care, biomedical informatics, and external prototype consultants from Vanderbilt and Geisinger—provides appropriate oversight and practical guidance [1]. Availability of Epic-style and blue-sky prototype development capability is supported by both internal UW ambulatory IT representation and external consultants with demonstrated experience in genomic-medicine informatics [1]. The stratified recruitment targets (at least 13 non-White or Hispanic participants in Aims 1 and 2, at least 28 in Aim 3) are achievable within the UW Medicine and Harborview catchments [1].

Three dependencies create material execution risk. First, the sequential design means Aim 2 depends on Aim 1 completion, and prototype development in Aim 3 depends on both Aims 1 and 2. The schedule allocates Aim 1 interviews to quarters 1–2 with analysis overlapping in Q2–Q3, Aim 2 interviews to Q3–Q4 with analysis extending to Q5, and prototype creation plus round 1 testing to Q5–Q6 [1]. Each handoff requires completed analysis before the next phase can begin. Recruitment difficulties, coding delays, or the need to refine protocols based on Aim 1 findings before finalizing Aim 2 encounter very little buffer.

Second, the Aim 2 recruitment of ten participants in each of four mutually exclusive result categories while simultaneously meeting demographic strata [1] depends on sufficient eligible patients who received the relevant results within the preceding 12 months and agree to participate. Pharmacogenomic testing volumes and hemochromatosis testing at UW Medicine will determine whether these cells can be filled within the allocated window. The advisory board, with laboratory-medicine informatics and medical genetics representation [1], can help assess viability, but the proposal does not provide utilization data confirming the available pool for each category.

Third, the two-year R21 budget and schedule leave little room for the iterative revision cycles between usability rounds. The plan allocates revision, round 2 testing, and consultant review to Q6–Q7, and further revision with round 3 testing to Q7–Q8 [1]. Each revision requires analysis of the preceding round's think-aloud interviews, prototype modification, and resolution of conflicting feedback [1]. With three or four prototypes per participant and 28 participants per round [1], the volume of usability data is substantial. The contingency plan to prioritize underrepresented groups if they prove disproportionately represented among non-users [1] adds further recruitment effort to an already full timeline.

These dependencies are manageable but require active monitoring. The advisory board's twice-yearly meetings provide a mechanism for schedule review, and the experienced qualitative team can anticipate common recruitment and coding challenges. A compressed schedule with limited contingency does not mean the project cannot succeed; it means execution requires consistently favorable conditions that an exploratory R21 cannot guarantee.

## Ethics and safety

Ethics and data practices are thoroughly and appropriately planned, with participant protections proportionate to the low-risk interview and usability-testing design. The study merits an A rating with high confidence because the proposal anticipates the relevant ethical dimensions—privacy, consent, participant distress, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and data management—and addresses each with specific, defensible procedures.

The research involves two principal activities: semistructured interviews about participants’ experiences receiving genetic and non-genetic results through the eCare portal, and cognitive usability interviews in which participants interact with prototype report templates and think aloud about their experience. Neither activity involves a clinical intervention, experimental treatment, or return of new genetic findings. The proposal correctly identifies the absence of incidental clinical findings and does not enroll children [1]. These characteristics place the study squarely in the low-risk category.

The privacy and consent arrangements are proportionate and well-justified. The project will seek a HIPAA waiver for access to protected health information used solely to identify eligible patients, and an IRB waiver of written consent documentation to reduce unnecessary identifying links between participants and study records [1]. The rationale for these waivers is sound: written consent documentation would itself create the kind of identifying trail that the study design aims to minimize. Recruitment materials and consent discussions will explain procedures, confidentiality safeguards, voluntary participation, risks, and the absence of direct clinical benefit [1].

Participant distress is the most material risk, and the proposal addresses it with proportionate safeguards. Interviews may elicit discomfort when discussing inherited cancer risk or the implications of genetic findings for family members; participants might also feel unease about audio recording or about recognition by others during clinic-based recruitment [1]. The protocol specifies that participants may stop at any time, will be reminded not to provide identifying information about third parties, and will be directed to assistance if they experience distress [1]. Any inadvertently supplied third-party identifying information will be removed from recordings and transcripts. These measures reflect standard practice in qualitative health research exploring sensitive topics.

Data management follows a defensible lifecycle. Recordings will be transcribed, de-identified, and checked for accuracy [1]. Information needed only for incentive administration will be stored separately from study data. Data will reside on secure servers or password-protected computers, with physical materials locked and access restricted to the research team. A clear data-destruction provision applies at study close [1].

The English-only restriction, discussed under Rigor as a scientific limitation, also carries an ethical dimension: it excludes patients with limited English proficiency who are likely to face the access barriers the ethical framework’s justice and equity domain explicitly addresses [1]. The proposal acknowledges this as a limitation and identifies multilingual report development as future work [1]. The restriction does not render the study unethical—the enrolled participants receive appropriate protections, and the exclusion is based on a practical constraint of the current portal environment. The scientific consequence of this restriction (diminished equity evidence) properly belongs under Rigor. No animal research, environmental hazards, or operational safety risks outside the clinical interview context are present.

## Management

The team composition and advisory infrastructure are strong, but the compressed sequential schedule with limited contingency and the unspecified division of labor for key prototype-development tasks create execution risk that warrants close project management attention.

The investigative team combines the necessary expertise. Korngiebel brings direct experience in formative research, electronic prototyping, and usability testing from the prior 59-interview study at the same institution [2]. Robins contributes extensive qualitative-methods experience, Fullerton adds genomic bioethics depth, and Amendola provides genetic counseling and clinical-sequencing knowledge [1]. This configuration covers the study's three methodological domains—qualitative interviewing, ethical analysis, and user-centered design—without obvious gaps. A graduate assistant will support prototype revision between usability rounds [1], providing dedicated effort for the iterative design work.

The advisory and consulting structure provides substantial external guidance and practical access, summarized below.

| Group | Relevant expertise | Operational role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Advisory board | Family medicine, ambulatory IT, medical genetics, laboratory-medicine informatics, biomedical informatics (Vanderbilt) | Twice-yearly meetings; protocol review; recruitment and compatibility advice |
| Prototype consultants | Genomic-medicine informatics (MUSC, Geisinger) | Heuristic walkthroughs between usability rounds 2 and 3; platform-specific and platform-agnostic design review |
| Mentoring committee | Bioethics, biomedical informatics, medical education | PI career development and scientific guidance |

Board members covering Harborview Family Medicine and UW ambulatory IT [1] directly support the Aim 3 non-user recruitment through the safety-net clinic and the assessment of Epic-style prototype compatibility [1]. The external prototype consultants from MUSC and Geisinger bring experience with genomic-medicine informatics in settings that differ from UW Medicine, strengthening the generalizability assessment that the proposal identifies as an advisory role [1].

Two management concerns remain. First, the schedule allocates Aim 1 interviews and analysis to quarters 1–3, Aim 2 interviews and analysis to Q3–Q5, and prototype creation with three iterative usability rounds to Q5–Q8, with revision, consultant walkthroughs, and further testing compressed into the final four quarters [1]. Each phase depends on completed analysis from the preceding phase, but the plan embeds no explicit buffer for recruitment delays, coding backlogs, or the resolution of conflicting usability feedback between rounds. The contingency plan to prioritize underrepresented recruitment if these groups appear disproportionately among non-users [1] adds recruitment effort that the current timeline already absorbs without slack.

Second, the proposal specifies that Korngiebel will lead the usability analysis and identify redesign priorities, and that the team will review proposed changes for accuracy and resolve conflicting feedback [1], but the exact division of responsibility for prototype creation—the step that translates Aim 1 and 2 findings into the six to eight Epic-style and blue-sky mockups—is described at the level of team capability rather than assigned roles. The advisory board includes ambulatory IT expertise and the external consultants have relevant design experience [1], yet the plan does not specify who will build the interactive prototypes that participants evaluate on laptop computers during cognitive interviews [1]. If prototype construction falls to the PI and a graduate assistant without dedicated design or informatics programming support, the volume of work between rounds 1, 2, and 3—analysis of 28 think-aloud interviews, redesign of multiple prototypes, and preparation for the next round—could strain the allocated two quarters for two full revision cycles.

These are not deficiencies that prevent the project from succeeding. The team's demonstrated experience with similar work, the advisory board's scheduled oversight, and the clear sequencing of the three aims provide adequate structure. Active monitoring of recruitment against diversity targets and early confirmation of prototype-development responsibilities during the project's first quarter would substantially reduce the management risk identified here.

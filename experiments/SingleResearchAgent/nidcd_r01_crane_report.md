# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project investigates how humans combine visual and inertial cues to perceive self-motion direction, determine whether the two signals share a common cause, and adapt when they are persistently inconsistent. The work will systematically map the range over which explicit common-cause perception and implicit cross-modal influence operate across the full horizontal plane, in both healthy individuals and people with unilateral vestibular loss. Its most valuable expected contributions are a rigorous descriptive framework for causal inference in heading perception and the first systematic evidence on whether perceptual adaptation can durably correct the heading errors that persist after vestibular damage. Beyond its basic mechanistic goals, the project will provide the methods and effect-size estimates necessary to design a subsequent clinical rehabilitation trial.

The proposal is grounded in the laboratory's validated psychophysical methods and strong preliminary data, which already show a dissociation between unity judgments and inertial heading bias. The experimental design appropriately measures both explicit and implicit responses in the same participants, enabling the core inferential step. The principal limitation is that several important comparisons—particularly the generalisation of heading adaptation across the response plane and the comparison between adaptation methods—are not supported with the same measurement rigour as the primary causal-inference experiments. Recent evidence from the group that across-session heading reliability is moderate, with minimum detectable change comparable to the predicted adaptation effect, further tempers the strength of conclusions that individual experiments can draw. Nonetheless, the project's logic remains sound and the limitations are ones of inferential strength rather than a failure of the design to address its aims.

Weighing the strengths against the limitations, this is a well-grounded project with strong scientific value. The core mechanistic experiments in Aims I and II are methodologically sound and likely to yield the intended descriptive and conceptual contributions. The primary risks—moderate sample sizes, test-retest reliability that is comparable to the expected adaptation effect, and a quasi-experimental design for comparing adaptation methods—do not undermine the project's fundamental goals, but they do mean that conclusions about the durability of heading correction and differential efficacy of methods should be treated as preliminary. The project's value as a necessary foundation for a later rehabilitation trial is sufficient justification for support.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**This project has strong scientific value, addressing a clear and consequential gap in the understanding of multisensory self-motion perception.** The central question—whether explicit judgments of common causation and implicit cross-modal influence operate over different discrepancy ranges—is important for models of sensory integration and directly motivated by clinical phenomena.

Dizziness, vertigo and motion sickness are perceptual symptoms, yet vestibular evaluation has historically emphasized reflexes such as the vestibulo-ocular reflex and vestibular-evoked myopotentials [1]. These reflex measures do not fully account for patients' perceptual experience, and experiments on isolated vestibular thresholds do not reproduce the multisensory conditions of everyday movement. A clinically useful account must explain how visual and inertial information are combined and when visual motion is attributed to environmental movement rather than self-motion [1].

The proposal builds on a mature conceptual framework in which the brain must solve a causal inference problem: visual motion is ambiguous because it could represent observer movement through a stationary world, environmental movement around a stationary observer, or a combination of both [1]. The project will provide the first systematic mapping of this inference across the full 360° horizontal plane, distinguishing the direction perceived in each sensory modality from the judgment that the two modalities describe the same movement [1].

Independent evidence confirms the inferential requirement that this dual-measurement approach targets. Acerbi et al. (2018) demonstrated that the heading discrimination task alone could not rule out forced fusion—a strategy that integrates cues regardless of discrepancy—and that only combined evidence from explicit and implicit tasks distinguished causal inference from forced fusion [2]. The proposal's design, which obtains both unity judgments and independent heading estimates in the same session, is thus not merely convenient but necessary for the intended conclusion.

The attainable descriptive contribution extends beyond healthy perception to the clinically important question of how heading perception changes after unilateral vestibular loss [1]. The longitudinal study of acute lesions (Experiment IIIA) will characterize the time course of heading bias and test competing mechanistic accounts—whether lesion-side heading errors reflect otolith asymmetry, rotational compensation, or both [1]. Preliminary data from five participants with chronic unilateral hypofunction showed a mean heading deviation of 7.5 ± 3.4 degrees, while visual headings were not systematically displaced, establishing that a measurable perceptual asymmetry exists [1].

The adaptation experiments in Aims II and III then test whether these persistent errors can be corrected, providing the effect-size estimates and methodological foundation necessary for a subsequent randomized rehabilitation trial [1]. Even if the mechanism proves heterogeneous and durable correction is limited, the longitudinal characterization of heading bias and its relationship to reflex recovery represents a worthwhile descriptive contribution in its own right.

## Innovation

**The proposal makes a well-defined incremental contribution to an active research area, with its strongest innovation residing in the systematic, within-participant dissociation of explicit causal inference and implicit cross-modal influence across the full 360° horizontal plane and in both healthy and vestibular-lesioned populations.**

The finding that inertial heading estimates can be biased toward the visual heading even when participants explicitly report that the two signals do not share a common cause has been established in published work from this laboratory. Rodriguez and Crane (2020) demonstrated in eight healthy participants that inertial heading was biased 10–20° toward the visual heading for offsets of 30–120°, including 11–15° on trials where common causation was not perceived [3].

The present proposal innovates by vastly expanding the parameter space and adding the clinical comparison that the literature currently lacks. Specifically, the project will:

- Densely sample headings across the full 360° plane rather than 30° increments alone [1].
- Systematically manipulate temporal offset, motion duration, acceleration, and visual-field size as factors that alter the evidence for common causation [1].
- Obtain both explicit unity judgments and independent heading estimates in the same session for every condition, enabling formal model comparison within individual participants [1].
- Examine whether reduced vestibular reliability in unilateral lesions shifts the operating characteristics of both explicit causal inference and implicit cross-modal influence [1].

This within-participant design, spanning healthy and clinical groups under parametrically varied stimulus conditions, adds a level of descriptive and computational specificity that the field does not yet possess. A further contribution is the planned integration of the causal-inference results into a unified computational model within the a priori five-year schedule [1]. This modeling ambition, while dependent on the empirical data yet to be collected, provides a natural endpoint for the descriptive work and a framework for generating testable predictions beyond the current experimental programme.

**The adaptation experiments represent distinct methodological extensions rather than conceptual breakthroughs, though their clinical application adds worthwhile novelty.** The heading-offset method builds directly on Zaidel et al. (2011), who demonstrated fixed-ratio vestibular-to-visual calibration of approximately 1.75:1 in humans exposed to a 10° heading discrepancy [4]. The proposal's use of a 20° offset and a rotating virtual environment with reduced coherence are incremental variations on established adaptation paradigms [1].

Where the proposal adds novelty is in testing two adaptation methods within the same vestibular-lesioned participants, measuring both visual and inertial heading separately to determine whether each method produces the intended inertial correction without introducing unwanted visual bias, and systematically assessing whether any correction generalizes beyond the trained heading range and persists across weekly intervals [1]. These translational design features extend the method to a patient population for which no systematic adaptation evidence currently exists. The preliminary data from five chronic-lesion participants undergoing rotational adaptation showed strong first-session correction with some retention at one week in four of five, but negligible change in Dizziness Handicap Inventory scores [1]. The proposed work will determine whether these early signals of perceptual correction can be sustained and whether the heading-offset method produces comparable or different effects—questions that directly determine whether a subsequent randomized clinical trial is justified.

Taken together, the proposal does not claim or require a new mechanism or a world-leading breakthrough. Its innovation is the careful, systematic parameterization of a known phenomenon in a clinically relevant comparison, combined with the first attempt at durable perceptual correction after vestibular damage. That constitutes a worthwhile incremental contribution.

## Rigor

**The psychophysical methods are firmly grounded in the laboratory's validated protocols, and the core design—measuring both explicit unity judgments and independent heading estimates in the same participants—is well suited to the central inferential goal. Material limitations in the measurement of adaptation generalization, in the comparison between adaptation methods, and in the statistical power available for patient subgroup analyses mean that conclusions about durable heading correction and differential efficacy should be treated as preliminary, though the project remains capable of delivering its primary descriptive and methodological contributions.**

### Strengths of the measurement framework

Visual and inertial stimuli are delivered via a six-degree-of-freedom hexapod platform synchronized with head-tracking and visual displays, and participants report perceived heading using both forced-choice psychophysics with interleaved staircases and continuous mechanical-dial estimates [1]. The systematic separation of visual and inertial report blocks addresses the practical finding that reporting both modalities in the same block proved confusing [1]. Forced-choice responses are fitted with cumulative Gaussian functions using Monte Carlo maximum-likelihood procedures, with resampling to generate confidence intervals and support individual-level inference [1]. These methods reproduce the approach used in the laboratory's published work and are appropriate for the stated descriptive goals.

The design's most important rigour is the within-participant comparison that distinguishes explicit causal inference from implicit cross-modal influence. Experiment IA presents all 144 combinations of 12 visual and 12 inertial headings in 30° increments, obtaining both a unity judgment and an estimate of either the visual or inertial direction on every trial, with six unity judgments and three estimates of each modality per combination across repeated blocks [1]. This permits formal comparison of the discrepancy range that supports explicit common-cause perception against the range over which one heading estimate is attracted toward the other—the dissociation that the preliminary data from four participants already suggest and that Acerbi et al. (2018) showed requires combined evidence from both task types to establish [1] [2].

### Limitations in the adaptation experiments

The primary rigour limitations concern the adaptation experiments, where the central question of generalization beyond trained headings is assessed with a measurement strategy that is not equally developed. Forced-choice testing near straight ahead is the primary outcome for Experiments IIA, IIB, and IIIB, with continuous estimates deployed in "selected experiments" to test generalization across the horizontal plane [1]. As worded, generalization measurement is a secondary outcome, not systematically powered or guaranteed across every adaptation experiment; yet clinical usefulness of any heading correction depends substantially on whether it transfers beyond the trained direction. This does not prevent the experiments from establishing whether adaptation occurs at all, but it weakens the strength of any conclusion about generalization.

The comparison between adaptation methods in Experiment IIIB introduces a further design concern. The proposal states that early enrollees receive the rotation method first and later enrollees the heading-offset method first [1]. This creates a confound between cohort and method sequence: any difference between methods could reflect differences in the patient samples recruited at different calendar periods rather than a genuine differential effect. Randomized crossover or counterbalanced allocation would be a conventional solution that the current design does not adopt.

### Statistical power and measurement reliability

Sample sizes are calculated from pilot data using plausible effect sizes, but external evidence from the same research group tempers confidence. Geno et al. (2024) reported that across-session test-retest reliability for heading direction point of subjective equality was moderate (ICC = 0.59), with a minimum detectable change of 6–9° [5]. The expected adaptation effect in Aim II is approximately 8° [1]—directly comparable to the measurement variability. With the proposed N of 15, a mean shift of this magnitude may be detectable at the group level, but individual-level conclusions about whether a given participant has adapted meaningfully will be unreliable. The proposal's use of resampling-based confidence intervals and the stated p ≤ .01 criterion are appropriate analytical precautions, but they do not increase the underlying signal-to-noise ratio.

For the patient experiments, the tension between the plan to analyze etiologies separately and the anticipated sample sizes is a structural limitation. Experiment IC plans approximately ten participants with chronic unilateral lesions, Experiment IIIA estimates 16 participants before approximately 50% attrition, and Experiment IIIB seeks 20 or more to examine etiologies separately [1]. If the sample in any experiment divides into three etiology groups (e.g., surgical, neuritis, gentamicin) with further attrition from the longitudinal arm, cell sizes become descriptive rather than comparative. The proposal acknowledges that clinical variability and slower recruitment are expected, but the inferential ambition of separate etiology analyses exceeds the statistical design that supports them.

**These limitations do not invalidate the project.** The core causal-inference experiments in Aim I are methodologically robust and likely to produce the intended descriptive framework. The adaptation experiments in Aim II will provide effect-size estimates and feasibility data even if generalization and individual-level reliability are not definitively established. The patient studies will generate the first systematic longitudinal characterization of heading bias after vestibular loss and the first evidence on whether perceptual adaptation can be induced in this population—contributions that are valuable at a descriptive level regardless of whether durable correction is demonstrated. The rigour is sufficient for the primary descriptive and methodological goals, though appropriately cautious interpretation of the adaptation and patient-arm conclusions is warranted.

## Feasibility

**The core experimental programme is solidly feasible, resting on established laboratory infrastructure, validated protocols, and demonstrated pilot data. The principal feasibility risk is clinical recruitment for Aim III, where acknowledged attrition may reduce the effective sample available for etiology-stratified analyses, though this affects the strength of patient-arm inferences rather than the deliverability of the mechanistic experiments.**

### Equipment and methods

The laboratory possesses the necessary equipment and has demonstrated its operational readiness. A six-degree-of-freedom hexapod motion platform delivers controlled translations and rotations synchronized with a 98-degree azimuth visual display; a second platform facilitates parallel testing and clinical access. Complementary virtual-reality systems with head tracking and binocular disparity, a 110-degree headset, and a custom multi-axis sled/rotator offering unlimited yaw range provide the flexibility to accommodate conditions that the primary platform cannot [1]. Motion tracking, video eye tracking, accelerometers, and rate gyros characterize stimulus delivery and participant movement throughout testing [1]. This is a mature apparatus configuration, not a developmental one.

The psychophysical protocols are equally established. The laboratory has published extensively using forced-choice procedures with interleaved staircases, continuous mechanical-dial heading reports, and cumulative-Gaussian fitting with Monte Carlo maximum-likelihood estimation and resampling-based inference [1]. The pilot data from four participants in the causal-inference paradigm, from individual adaptation sessions using both heading-offset and rotating-environment methods, and from five chronic vestibular-loss participants undergoing repeated adaptation visits all demonstrate that the specific experiments can be executed and yield interpretable data [1]. The separation of visual and inertial report blocks resolved the practical confusion that arose when both modalities were reported in the same block, confirming that the measurement framework has been refined through direct experience [1].

### Recruitment and schedule

Recruitment pathways are specified and credible. The proposal draws on an institutional database, recurring healthy participants, and patients with unilateral vestibular lesions from otolaryngology clinics [1]. The combined target of approximately 120 healthy controls and 30 participants with vestibulopathy over five years is realistic for a laboratory with established clinical access and a full-time technician supporting recruitment and testing [1]. The five-year schedule sequences experiments to begin with normal-participant causal inference and adaptation work while patient recruitment proceeds in parallel, so that delays in one arm need not idle the entire programme [1].

### Clinical recruitment risks

The recognized feasibility challenge is the clinical recruitment for Aim III, where two factors compound. First, the longitudinal acute-lesion study (Experiment IIIA) requires enrolment within approximately the first week after lesion onset to capture the weekly measurement schedule, and the proposal anticipates approximately 50% attrition because serial visits over one year may be untenable for patients travelling long distances [1]. With a target N of 16 to detect a 7.5-degree difference, approximately 32 patients would need to be enrolled, and a portion of those will have incomplete lesions (e.g., neuritis, gentamicin) that must be analyzed separately [1]. Second, the chronic-lesion causal-inference experiment (IC) and the adaptation experiment (IIIB) each seek 10–20 or more participants, with the ambition of examining etiologies separately [1].

The aggregate requirement of approximately 30 patient participants is achievable, but the number of participants available per etiology group after attrition is likely to be small, constraining the comparative analyses the proposal envisages. This is a manageable rather than a prohibitive risk: the laboratory has clinical access through otolaryngology, the prevalence of unilateral vestibular lesions in the clinical population provides a sufficient referral base, and the five-year horizon allows for slower-than-expected recruitment without compromising the normal-participant experiments that constitute the larger portion of the project. The longitudinal arm's primary value—characterizing the time course and mechanistic origin of heading bias—remains attainable even if the final sample is smaller than planned, because the data will be the first systematic description of perceptual recovery after acute vestibular loss, and descriptive value does not depend on achieving a particular N for hypothesis testing.

## Ethics and safety

**The ethical framework and safety provisions are proportionate, clearly specified, and appropriate for a noninvasive psychophysical study with acknowledged risks of motion sickness and transient dizziness. No material ethical deficit is identified.**

### Risk identification and mitigation

The project involves three participant populations—healthy adults, adolescents aged 13–17, and patients with unilateral vestibular lesions—all undergoing noninvasive perceptual testing on a motion platform or in virtual-reality environments [1]. The principal foreseeable risks are motion sickness, worsening dizziness (particularly in the patient adaptation experiments where stimulus exposure may provoke symptoms), and loss of privacy [1]. These are correctly identified as the primary concerns, and the mitigation strategy addresses each.

For physical discomfort, the proposal specifies that participants receive standardized explanations and large, unambiguous practice stimuli before experimental trials, establishing comfort and comprehension without correctness feedback during data collection [1]. The adaptation experiments in Aim IIIB explicitly state that Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI) monitoring will be conducted during visits to detect worsening symptoms, and that if discomfort occurs, exposure duration or rotational speed will be reduced as an immediate response [1]. This is a practical, patient-facing safety measure that aligns the experimental protocol with clinical monitoring, rather than treating participant well-being as a matter for post hoc adverse event reporting alone. The preliminary adaptation data from five chronic-lesion participants showed negligible mean DHI change (−0.8, range −4 to 2), providing some empirical reassurance, though the small sample limits generalization [1].

### Consent, privacy, and oversight

Informed consent procedures are described with appropriate specificity for the populations involved. Adults provide standard consent; adolescents, if enrolled, require parental consent and an age-appropriate assent process [1]. Participants may discontinue at any time without penalty, and research participation is explicitly not permitted to interfere with other clinical care [1]. The intended recruitment age range of 13–89 is acknowledged, with the expectation that most participants will be adults aged 18–89 [1].

Data management provisions address the privacy risk through separation of identifying information from research measurements, with access limited to designated study personnel [1]. This is conventional but adequate for a study that does not collect particularly sensitive health data beyond the standard clinical characterization measures. Dissemination via scientific publications and presentations, with trial registration planned before data collection, further supports transparency [1].

Adverse event documentation is specified to include severity and relationship to participation, with review through institutional oversight [1]. While the proposal does not itemize a formal data and safety monitoring board, this is proportionate to the risk level: the procedures are noninvasive, the primary adverse events (motion sickness, transient dizziness) are self-limiting and familiar to the laboratory, and institutional review provides external oversight.

## Management

**The management arrangements are appropriate for the planned scope, with clear supervisory responsibility, a logical year-by-year sequence, and institutional resources sufficient to deliver the mechanistic and patient experiments in parallel. Contingent risks around clinical recruitment timing are acknowledged and do not destabilize the programme, though delays in the acute-lesion arm could compress the later-year analytical integration.**

### Staffing and resources

The principal investigator, Benjamin T. Crane, will oversee all experiments, supported by a full-time technician who will handle recruitment and testing [1]. This division of labour is sensible: the investigator's published expertise in vestibular heading psychophysics and the technician's dedicated operational role together provide capacity for the high-volume data collection that the factorial causal-inference designs and repeated-session adaptation protocols demand. The proposal does not describe additional graduate student or postdoctoral personnel, and the five-year timeline reflects an appropriately scaled programme for this staffing level.

Existing equipment and clinical access are the critical institutional resources. The hexapod motion platform, the second accessible platform, virtual-reality systems, and the custom multi-axis sled/rotator are operational rather than developmental assets [1]. Clinical recruitment pathways through otolaryngology are in place, and the laboratory maintains a screened participant database and recurring healthy volunteers [1]. Because the proposal does not depend on new equipment procurement, facility construction, or novel clinical referral relationships, the infrastructure risk is low—the primary resource uncertainties concern patient flow rather than technical readiness.

### Schedule and dependencies

The five-year schedule organizes experiments into a coherent sequence that respects dependencies while enabling parallel work [1]. Year 1 establishes the normal-participant causal-inference baseline (Experiment IA), begins heading-offset adaptation (IIA), and initiates both acute-lesion follow-up (IIIA) and chronic-lesion adaptation (IIIB). Year 2 extends causal inference to temporal and stimulus-parameter manipulations (IB), adds the lesion group (IC), and begins rotational adaptation (IIB). Years 3 and 4 complete parameter-variation experiments and the core patient adaptation work. Year 5 is reserved for integrating causal-inference results into a unified computational model and completing analysis and dissemination across aims.

This sequencing is prudent: the normal-participant experiments that require the largest sample sizes and most elaborate factorial designs begin early, establishing the methods and baseline data before the more constrained patient studies rely on them. The final-year modelling activity provides a natural endpoint that depends on the empirical data collected in earlier years, and the schedule explicitly allows that findings motivating further experiments may be pursued [1]—an appropriate acknowledgment that a five-year programme is a framework, not a rigid commitment.

The principal management risk is patient recruitment timing. Acute unilateral lesions must be identified and enrolled within approximately the first week to begin the weekly measurement schedule specified in Experiment IIIA [1]. If referrals arrive irregularly, the planned weekly follow-up could be disrupted for individual participants, and the overall recruitment target of 16 after attrition may take longer to reach than the schedule assumes. However, the schedule does not make downstream patient experiments strictly dependent on completing IIIA; the chronic-lesion causal-inference study (IC) and the adaptation experiment (IIIB) use participants with lesions present for at least one year and can proceed in parallel [1]. The risk of recruitment delays is therefore one of timeline compression for the final analytical synthesis, not a cascade that halts other experimental work. The Year 5 provision for model integration offers buffer, and the project retains substantial scientific value from the normal-participant experiments even if the patient arms deliver smaller or later samples than planned.

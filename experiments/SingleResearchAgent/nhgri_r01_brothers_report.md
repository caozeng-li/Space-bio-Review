# Project review

## Overall assessment

This four-year mixed-methods project addresses the question of how networked biorepositories reconcile divergent local ethical and regulatory commitments when pooling biospecimens, genotyping data, and health-related data across institutions. It will produce a systematic inventory of U.S. networked biorepositories, retrospective interviews and primary-document analysis on ethical and regulatory challenges and solutions, and longitudinal participant observation of governance-in-action in five contrasting networks at different stages of development. The most valuable expected knowledge is a structured descriptive account of how ethical problems—spanning informed consent, donor control, investigator access, privacy, return of results, and governance—are actually identified, deliberated, and resolved in everyday network operations, and how organizational context shapes those processes. This evidence, integrated with normative analysis and refined through focus groups and an expert symposium, is intended to yield practicable, domain-specific recommendations and adaptable governance models for networked biorepositories.

The proposal’s main supporting evidence rests on the investigative team’s prior national survey and case-study experience with U.S. biobanks, their direct access to five contrasting networks, and a well-documented mixed-methods design. The retrospective inventory and interview components are well specified and build directly on proven methods; protections for interview and focus-group participants are proportionate to the low-risk study population; and the leadership and advisory structure provides appropriate expertise and oversight. The principal limitations concern the participant observation component—the most novel element—where the analytical pathway from field notes to coded themes is less fully described than the interview coding logic, and the observational data will inevitably be asymmetric across networks that differ in which governance bodies are accessible. The normative integration (Aim 3) is the weakest methodological link: the proposal outlines a framework for evaluating candidate solutions against ethical and legal standards but does not specify which normative standards will be applied or how trade-offs among them will be adjudicated, so the resulting recommendations will be tested more for practical acceptability than for normative validity. Finally, the deliberate exclusion of donor interviews means the recommendations on consent, donor control, and return of results will lack direct evidence of donor preferences.

Weighing the trade-off, the proposal combines an important research object, strong methodological innovation in the participant observation approach, and a well-informed, realistic plan that builds on the investigators’ proven prior work. The normative integration pathway and the underspecified handling of asymmetric observational data temper confidence in the generalizability of the translational recommendations but do not undermine the prospective empirical contribution. On balance, the planned work is judged to be a valuable, feasible investigation that will yield substantial empirical knowledge and a reasoned basis for governance guidance, provided the observational access holds and the schedule permits incorporation of later-phase findings.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**The project addresses an important and timely gap in the empirical understanding of networked biorepository governance, and the knowledge it promises—a structured descriptive account of how ethical challenges are actually identified, deliberated, and resolved in everyday network operations—would substantially advance both scholarship and practice in bioethics and human-subjects protection.**

Precision medicine increasingly depends on assembling large, diverse cohorts that no single institution can assemble alone; networking existing repositories is the practical response, producing the most comprehensive empirical picture of networked biorepository governance yet attempted in the ELSI literature. The proposal defines a networked biorepository as a research collection providing centralized access to biospecimens, genotyping data, or health-related data collected at multiple institutions, with specimens potentially remaining at local sites [1]. Within that definition the study identifies six operational domains where tensions between local and network-level commitments manifest: informed consent, donor control over specimens and data, investigator access, privacy and data security, return of research results, and governance and oversight [1].

The background rationale articulates why those tensions are inherent: a donor may have limited sharing at one institution while another site offers different choices; client investigators may request broad access whereas donor institutions see themselves as stewards; returning a result found at a remote client site may require cooperation among multiple parties with divergent priorities [1]. These are concrete, recurring problems that the current governance system—designed for “one researcher, one project, one jurisdiction”—was not built to address [2].

The attainable knowledge is threefold. First, the retrospective inventory and interviews (Aim 1) will yield a systematic characterization of U.S. networked biorepositories, documenting their organizational configurations, the ethical and regulatory challenges they have encountered, and the solutions they have attempted. This builds directly on the team’s prior national survey of U.S. biobanks, which found that many operate as formal or informal networks but could be characterized only on organizational features, not ethical practices [1]. Second, the longitudinal participant observation (Aim 2) will produce the first sustained empirical record of how governance, deliberation, and problem-solving actually function in everyday network operations, across five contrasting networks at different stages of development [1]. Third, the translational aim promises domain-specific recommendations and adaptable governance models [1], refined through practitioner focus groups and an expert symposium [1]. This empirical grounding distinguishes the contribution from purely normative bioethics scholarship and positions the recommendations to inform the design of large collaborations, including but not limited to the Precision Medicine Initiative cohort [1].

The deliberate inclusion of developing networks and an abandoned networking effort alongside mature organizations [1] adds significant descriptive value by avoiding the survivor bias common in organizational research. The resulting evidence will capture not only successful solutions but also the conditions under which networking efforts struggle or fail—knowledge that is at least as important for governance design as success stories.

## Innovation

**The proposal’s most distinctive contribution is methodological: sustained participant observation of governance-in-action across five contrasting networks, combined with the deliberate sampling of developing and abandoned networking efforts alongside mature organizations. This combination has no obvious precedent in the published ELSI literature on biorepositories.**

The innovation resides in what the project adds beyond the methods and evidence already established in biobank ELSI research. Prior work—including the investigative team’s own national survey [1], IRB policy analyses at CTSA institutions [1], and the eMERGE consortium’s documentation of data-sharing challenges [3]—has relied principally on surveys, semi-structured interviews, and document analysis. These methods capture what participants report about their practices and what policies prescribe, but they cannot access the situated deliberation through which ethical challenges are actually identified, framed, and negotiated in real time. Qualitative studies of single biobank governance exist—for example, interview-based work on governance concepts in Japanese biobanks [4]—but they do not observe governance processes directly, and they do not compare across network configurations.

The proposal’s observational design fills this gap. Investigators will embed themselves in consortium meetings, advisory groups, conference calls, and relevant governance bodies [1]. Field notes taken immediately after sessions, biweekly team reflection on observer effects, and shared coding in the ATLAS.ti framework [1] follow established ethnographic practice. The approach is designed to capture both the deliberative process and the organizational context shaping solutions [1]—matters that retrospective interviews cannot access because participants may rationalize outcomes after the fact or be unaware of how institutional constraints shaped available options. The observation settings mapped in Table 4 [1] reveal genuine variation in which governance bodies exist and are accessible, from eMERGE’s community and ethics advisory boards to the Mayo Clinic Biobank’s inclusion of IRB meetings. This variation is itself an important source of comparative evidence about how governance structures differ and what consequences those differences carry.

The sampling strategy adds further originality. Rather than studying only successful, mature networks—the default in organizational research—the proposal deliberately includes networks at different stages: eMERGE and the Kentucky Cancer Registry as mature networks, the Newborn Screening Translational Research Network and Sage Bionetworks–Synapse as new networks, and the Mayo Clinic Biobank as a network in development [1]. The project also explicitly commits to examining “developing networks and an abandoned networking effort, rather than studying only successful mature organizations” [1]. Understanding why a networking effort was abandoned, and what ethical or governance obstacles contributed, may prove as informative for future network design as documenting what works in established collaborations.

A secondary innovation, more incremental but still valuable, is the integration of empirical and normative analysis through investigator-led domain reports, stakeholder focus groups, and an expert symposium [1]. The proposal’s commitment to testing draft recommendations for practical adequacy and implementability with practitioners, and revising those perceived as inadequate, distinguishes its translational ambition from purely theoretical normative scholarship. This iterative, empirically informed approach, while not wholly novel in ELSI research, is applied here to a domain—networked biorepository governance—where it has not previously been executed at this scale.

## Rigor

**The mixed-methods design is coherent and appropriate for the research questions, and the retrospective components—the systematic inventory, interviews, and primary-document analysis—are well specified and build on validated prior methods. The participant observation component, though the most innovative element, is less fully specified in its analytical pathway, and the normative integration (Aim 3) depends on evaluative standards that are outlined in principle rather than specified in detail. On balance the design supports the intended empirical inferences; the principal limitations concern the rigor of the translational step from descriptive findings to normative recommendations.**

### Retrospective inventory and interviews

The three-component architecture is logically structured to address distinct but complementary questions. The systematic inventory (Aim 1a) adapts the multi-faceted search strategy validated in the Henderson et al. national survey [5]—combining Boolean PubMed and NIH RePORTER searches, Google keyword searches, and AAMC and CTSA site searches—to identify networked biorepositories specifically [1]. Because terminology is inconsistent, the search will cast a wide net including small and informal arrangements and verify network existence through direct contact with key informants [1]. The resulting inventory will characterize contributing institutions, network scale and maturity, specimen and data locations, governance arrangements, and investigator access [1]. Rather than treating networks as a single uniform model, the inventory is designed to guide the selection and interpretation of qualitative cases by documenting the organizational variation against which interview and observational findings can be understood.

The interview component (Aim 1b) specifies a defensible sampling strategy: up to five stakeholders per network and up to 100 interviews in total, with snowball sampling across deliberately varied role types—scientific and administrative leaders, operations managers, informaticists, ethics advisers, IRB members, community advisers, donor-study investigators, and client researchers [1]. The commitment to “deliberately seek less powerful or dissenting stakeholders to reduce reliance on leadership-selected accounts” [1] is a methodologically important choice that addresses a recognized limitation of elite-interview designs in organizational research. The interview domains are clearly mapped to the project’s substantive concerns [1], and the prompts distinguish practical problems from ethical responsibilities—for example, logistical difficulty in sharing is different from a perceived duty of stewardship [1].

Framework Analysis with dual coding at the two lead sites, a shared codebook refined through independent coding of the same ten transcripts, and a 90% agreement threshold [1] follows accepted qualitative analysis standards. Primary documents—consent forms, data-use agreements, and IRB protocols—will be coded in the same framework, enabling systematic triangulation between stated policies, reported experiences, and observed deliberations [1].

### Participant observation

The participant observation component (Aim 2) is the most innovative element but also the least fully specified for analytical rigor. The mapping of planned observation settings [1] reveals genuine variation in accessible governance bodies: eMERGE has community and ethics advisory boards plus collaborator meetings; the Kentucky Cancer Registry has only collaborator meetings and conference calls; the Mayo Clinic Biobank uniquely includes IRB meetings. This variation is scientifically informative—it reflects real differences in network governance structures—but it also means the observational record will be asymmetric. The proposal acknowledges this implicitly by preserving the option to add or replace cases if Aim 1 findings justify it [1].

The biweekly team reflection process—in which investigators discuss observations, reflect on observer effects, and incorporate significant points into field notes before coding [1]—is well described and follows established ethnographic practice. What remains underspecified is *how* observations will be systematically compared across settings that differ in meeting frequency, formality, and the range of governance bodies accessible. The common coding framework in ATLAS.ti provides a structure for that comparison [1], but the proposal does not explain, for example, how an observed deliberation about return of results in an eMERGE advisory board meeting will be analytically related to the absence of such a body in the Kentucky Cancer Registry, or to the different deliberation that may occur in a Mayo Clinic IRB session.

### Normative integration

The normative integration (Aim 3) presents the most significant methodological challenge. The proposal states that the normative core will “examine conceptual and legal literature and evaluate candidate solutions according to their compatibility with ethical/legal standards, costs and practical trade-offs, and usefulness across different network configurations” [1]. This is a reasonable general framework, but it does not specify *which* normative standards will be applied, how trade-offs among competing standards will be adjudicated, or what criteria determine that a recommendation achieves “usefulness across different network configurations.”

The focus groups—up to ten, each devoted to one domain report, with purposive recruitment of stakeholders with practical experience [1]—and the expert symposium [1] provide empirical testing and refinement, which is a genuine strength. They are well suited to assessing whether draft recommendations are perceived as adequate and practically implementable by those who would use them. But they test practical acceptability rather than normative validity. The integration of normative and empirical analysis is a recognized challenge in ELSI research, and the proposal’s approach—domain reports led by individual investigators with team deliberation—is a defensible model whose success will depend substantially on the composition and working methods of the normative core, which are described in outline [1] but whose internal deliberative procedures are not specified.

### Donor perspectives

A further limitation affecting the completeness of the evidence base is the deliberate exclusion of specimen donors from the study population. The project “will not interview specimen donors directly,” relying instead on community advisory members “and others tasked with representing donor priorities” [1]. The proposal acknowledges this as a limitation reserved for subsequent work, but it means that recommendations on consent, donor control, and return of results will be formulated without direct evidence of donor preferences—a gap noted in the broader biobank governance literature, where incorporating stakeholder values and perspectives is recognized as essential to trustworthy governance [6] [7].

## Feasibility

**The project is well-grounded in prior work by the investigative team and the proposed networks show realistic variety, making the core empirical work feasible within the planned four years. The principal threats to feasibility are the tight integration of later-phase observational findings into the translational outputs, and the dependence on sustained, unrestricted access to all five geographically dispersed networks for the full observation period.**

The proposal rests on a demonstrated foundation. The team’s prior national survey successfully identified and recruited U.S. biobanks using the same multi-faceted search strategy now adapted to target networked biorepositories [1]. Their case studies of biobanks and IRB policy analyses at CTSA institutions [1] provide direct experience with the interview, document-analysis, and recruitment methods that Aim 1 will deploy at larger scale. The investigators’ embedded roles in eMERGE, the Newborn Screening Translational Research Network, and other genomics consortia [1] give them both the substantive familiarity and the professional relationships needed to negotiate observational access. The advisory committee includes named representatives from all five provisional case networks [1], which reinforces those relationships and provides a practical mechanism for troubleshooting access issues if they arise.

The five provisional cases in Aim 2 demonstrate variation across the dimensions that matter for the study’s comparative logic: maturity (mature eMERGE and Kentucky Cancer Registry versus new NBSTRN and Sage Bionetworks versus in-development Mayo Clinic Biobank), specimen and data architecture (study-based pooling, state registry linkage, virtual repository, cloud-based sharing, and system-wide cloud repository, respectively), and governance configuration [1]. The proposal explicitly reserves the option to add or replace cases if Aim 1 findings justify it within time and budget [1], which provides flexibility without over-committing to a fixed sample before the inventory results are known. The inventory data can confirm that the five cases adequately represent the organizational diversity identified, or guide substitution if they do not.

The four-year schedule [1] overlaps the work streams logically: the inventory and interviews begin early (Q1–Q10), participant observation runs for most of the project (Q3–Q14), and recommendation development and testing occupy the later phases (Q9–Q16). However, the schedule is tight at the translational end. Preliminary recommendations are prepared in Q9–Q12 while observation continues through Q14, meaning that observational findings emerging in the final year of fieldwork may not be fully incorporated into the domain reports before they enter focus-group testing. The same schedule constraint means the focus groups (Q11–Q14) and expert symposium (Q13–Q14) must run largely in parallel with the last phase of observation. This does not prevent the project from producing useful recommendations, but it does mean that the recommendations finalized in Q13–Q16 will draw more heavily on early- and mid-phase observational data than on the complete observational record. A minor adjustment to allow the symposium to follow the conclusion of fieldwork would strengthen the translational claim, though the current overlap is not a disqualifying weakness.

The most significant feasibility risk is the dependence on continued observational access across all five networks for three years. The proposal states that network leaders “have agreed to observation of relevant activities” [1], which is a necessary starting point, but it does not describe a contingency plan if a network withdraws, restricts access to particular governance bodies, or undergoes institutional reorganization. Given that one of the cases is explicitly described as an “in development” network and another as “new” [1], the risk of organizational instability over a four-year project is real. The option to replace cases using Aim 1 findings provides a partial safeguard [1], but it assumes that suitable replacements can be identified and negotiated into the study mid-course—a process that would consume time and may not reproduce the specific configuration that made the original case valuable.

On resources, the materials read do not include a budget, so the adequacy of funding for the substantial travel and coordination required by twice-annual in-person visits to each of five geographically dispersed sites [1] cannot be independently confirmed. The narrative’s description of the team’s collective expertise and institutional support is credible but rests on the investigators’ own account rather than independently verifiable documentation in the materials supplied.

## Ethics and safety

**The proposal addresses participant protection appropriately and proportionately for the low-risk study population of adult professionals and stakeholders, with robust data-protection measures and a pragmatic approach to consent in observational settings. The deliberate exclusion of specimen donors eliminates direct risk to the population most affected by the governance decisions under study.**

The study population consists entirely of adult professionals—scientific and administrative leaders, operations managers, informaticists, ethics advisers, IRB members, community advisers, donor-study investigators, and client researchers [1]—and does not involve specimen donors, patients, or vulnerable groups as research participants. This substantially limits the nature and magnitude of potential harms. The proposal identifies the relevant residual risks: discomfort from discussing sensitive organizational matters, professional repercussions if views are attributed to identifiable individuals, and the possibility of indirect identification of individuals or networks from published findings [1].

For interviews and focus groups, the consent procedures are clearly specified: participants receive information about the study, have opportunities to ask questions, and may decline individual questions, stop recording, or withdraw [1]. These are standard and adequate protections for the study population. The interview design—using open-ended prompts and nondirective follow-up, distinguishing practical problems from ethical responsibilities [1]—further reduces the likelihood that questioning itself causes distress, since it avoids interrogating participants about personal conduct and instead focuses on organizational processes and their experiences of them.

The observation component presents a more complex consent challenge because governance meetings, conference calls, and advisory sessions may involve transient or occasional participants for whom obtaining individual written consent before every session is impracticable. The proposal acknowledges this explicitly and describes a proportionate response: coordination with network leadership to establish transparency about the research presence, repeated reminders to meeting participants that observation is occurring, and “appropriate authorization” maintained with the study IRB [1]. This approach reflects the realities of embedded organizational research and follows accepted practice in ethnographic studies where the alternative—requiring signed consent from every person entering a meeting—would be both impracticable and disruptive to the very governance processes under observation.

Protections against deductive disclosure are thorough and proportionate. Transcripts and reports will use study identifiers or pseudonyms; network names will generally be withheld in publications unless explicit permission is obtained from the network [1]. Access to recordings, transcripts, and linkage information is restricted to the research team, with paper records locked and computer files password protected. Original recordings will be destroyed once transcription and coding are complete [1]. These measures address the most realistic risk—that a published finding about, for example, a conflict between a local IRB and a network governance body could be traced back to identifiable institutions or individuals. They also respect the reputational interests of networks that have granted observational access, which is important for maintaining the trust on which the study’s continued access depends.

The focus-group design includes a specific mitigation against power-related risks: groups will be “composed with attention to power differences so that participants can speak freely” [1]. This is particularly important given that focus groups will include stakeholders from different organizational levels and roles discussing potentially contentious governance issues. The measure is appropriate, though its effectiveness will depend on implementation details—such as whether junior staff are placed in groups separate from senior leaders—that the proposal does not fully specify.

The project does not involve hazardous materials, clinical interventions, animal subjects, or environmental risks. No participant incentives are offered for interviews [1], which removes any concern about undue inducement in a professional population where participation might otherwise carry career implications. The reliance on community advisory members as proxies for donor interests, while excluding donors themselves from the study [1], means the project gathers no direct evidence of donor perspectives on the governance questions it studies. This is not a safety concern—donors face no research risk because they are not enrolled—but it is an ethical limitation of the evidence base that affects the completeness of the normative recommendations, particularly on consent and donor control, as discussed under Rigor.

## Management

**The leadership and advisory arrangements are well structured for the proposed research, with a clear division of responsibilities matched to demonstrated expertise. The principal management weakness is the absence of documented contingency for the critical dependency on sustained observational access, though the built-in flexibility to replace cases provides a partial safeguard.**

The co-PI model distributes oversight sensibly across the two lead sites. Brothers and Goldenberg share overall responsibility, with Goldenberg leading the retrospective inventory, interviews, and focus groups, and Brothers leading primary-document analysis, participant observation, and policy development [1]. This division reflects their respective strengths: Goldenberg’s prior experience with the national biobank survey and interview methods [1] maps directly onto the Aim 1 work, while Brothers’ policy and observational experience aligns with Aims 2 and 3. The five co-investigators contribute complementary expertise that is matched to identifiable tasks: Cadigan provides ethnographic methods guidance for the observation component, Sharp contributes normative analysis and biobank experience, Rivera brings regulatory and IRB expertise, Rothstein supplies legal and privacy analysis, and Harrell takes primary responsibility for document analysis [1]. This is a coherent team in which each member’s role can be traced to a specific methodological or substantive need of the project.

The advisory committee is a particular strength [1]. It combines external ELSI and genomic research expertise (Gail Henderson, Eric Juengst, Jonathan Haines) with named representatives from all five provisional observation cases: Janet Olson from the Mayo Clinic Biobank, Rex Chisholm from eMERGE, Michael Watson from NBSTRN, Thomas Tucker from the Kentucky Cancer Registry, and Megan Dorr from Sage Bionetworks. This dual function—scientific oversight and practical facilitation of access—addresses both the intellectual quality of the work and the operational requirement of maintaining relationships with the networks under study. The advisory structure is well suited to a project where access depends on trust and reciprocal benefit: network representatives who participate in shaping the research are more likely to sustain their institutions’ commitment to the observation component over three years. The normative core—philosophical and legal/regulatory specialists who contribute throughout instrument development and interpretation [1]—provides the interdisciplinary capacity that the translational aim requires, though its internal deliberative procedures are not specified.

The four-year schedule coordinates overlapping work streams logically [1]. The inventory and interviews begin early (Q1–Q10), creating the descriptive foundation on which case selection and observational focus depend. Participant observation runs for most of the project (Q3–Q14), allowing sufficient time to capture governance processes that unfold over extended periods. The biweekly team reflection process [1] provides regular cross-site coordination and quality control across the two geographically separated lead institutions. The schedule overlaps recommendation development (Q9–Q12) with the final phase of observation (through Q14) and with focus groups (Q11–Q14), creating a tight integration that is efficient but leaves limited margin for incorporating late-observational findings or for delays in any single component. The structured milestones would benefit from explicit decision points—for example, a formal review of whether the provisional case set remains adequate after the Aim 1 inventory results are available in mid-project.

The most significant management risk is the dependence on continued observational access across all five geographically dispersed networks for three years, with twice-annual in-person visits per bank [1]. The proposal states that network leaders have agreed to observation [1], and the advisory committee representation reinforces that commitment, but a contingency for withdrawal or restricted access is not spelled out. The option to replace cases using Aim 1 findings [1] provides a partial safeguard, but it assumes suitable replacements can be identified and negotiated mid-course. This is a manageable risk for a project whose primary empirical contribution comes from the comparative richness of *any* well-observed cases, rather than from a fixed experimental design that would be invalidated by substitution. A brief contingency plan—specifying, for example, that the project would continue with a reduced case set and draw more heavily on the interview and documentary components—would strengthen confidence without altering the project’s design.

The materials read do not include CVs or an itemized budget, so individual qualifications and the adequacy of resources for the substantial travel and coordination required cannot be independently verified. The narrative’s description of team expertise and institutional support is credible given the investigators’ publication record and the institutional affiliations documented in the proposal references [1], but it rests on the investigators’ own account rather than independently reviewed documentation. The management arrangements described in the proposal are appropriate to the work planned; the confidence in this judgement is tempered by the absence of those supporting materials.

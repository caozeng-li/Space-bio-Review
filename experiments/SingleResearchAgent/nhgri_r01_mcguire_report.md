# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project investigates the critical and timely intersection of whole genome sequencing (WGS) clinical integration with existing regulatory, intellectual property, and reimbursement frameworks. The planned work combines a systematic, multi-source industry landscape analysis of technology developers, sequencing services, and interpretation providers with a structured, multi-stakeholder Policy Delphi process. Its most valuable expected knowledge contribution is a core analytical toolkit for policymakers: a publicly available map of the emerging WGS industry, a prioritized and clearly articulated agenda of policy questions generated by diverse stakeholders, and a set of defined alternative policy approaches—complete with a transparent account of the grounds for agreement and disagreement—for a selected set of tractable issues. This focus on clarifying options and recording dissensus is a significant and intellectually honest contribution in a complex, high-stakes domain.

The project is well-supported by a strong investigative team with complementary expertise in health policy, genetics, intellectual property, and reimbursement, and by a well-justified mixed-methods design. The explicit, multi-domain scope is sensible, and the integration of an independent advisory board and a multi-pronged dissemination strategy, including Capitol Hill briefings, substantially supports the prospect of impact. The main limitations are methodological: the decision rule for selecting the three policy questions for deep analysis is not fully specified, and no explicit strategy is provided for managing stakeholder attrition over the 20-month Delphi process. These are material considerations that could influence the final policy products, but they do not nullify the project's core descriptive and analytical value.

Weighing the strengths against the limitations, this is a well-conceived and timely policy research project that merits support. The core scientific value—providing a systematic landscape and clarifying, rather than prematurely closing, policy options on a critical issue—is clearly attainable with the proposed mixed-methods design. The methodological limitations, such as the under-specified issue-selection rule and Delphi attrition risk, are manageable and affect the precision rather than the fundamental validity of the results. The team's collective expertise and the robust dissemination plan further strengthen the case. The project's considerable potential to inform evidence-based policymaking during a period of rapid technological change firmly outweighs its manageable design limitations.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**Scientific value: B (Confidence: B).** The project addresses a question of genuine policy urgency—how the clinical integration of WGS interacts with regulatory, intellectual-property, and reimbursement frameworks—at a moment when existing policy architecture was demonstrably strained. The proposal correctly identifies that a regulatory and payment landscape built around single-analyte, single-condition genetic tests may not accommodate the multiplex, multi-actor nature of WGS, where equipment manufacturing, sequencing, interpretation, and delivery to clinicians or patients can involve different organizations governed by different oversight systems [1]. This diagnosis is specific and consequential, and it gives the project an importance beyond generic horizon-scanning.

The intended knowledge contributions are threefold. First, the project will produce a systematic, publicly available description of the WGS industry—covering organizations that develop sequencing technologies, provide sequencing services, and offer clinical annotation and interpretation—with structured data on technologies, business models, patent claims, health-related claims, CLIA certification, and third-party coverage [1]. At the time of the proposal, no such comprehensive landscape existed, and establishing this descriptive baseline is a genuinely useful capability for regulators, payers, professional bodies, and the research community. The initial landscape table listing 34 organizations provides a credible starting point [1], and the commitment to monitor new entrants and service changes throughout the study means the resource will remain current during the project period [1].

Second, the Delphi process will generate a stakeholder-informed, ranked list of policy questions spanning multiple domains, with accompanying ratings of importance, validity, and technical and political feasibility [1]. This prioritized agenda is valuable precisely because it surfaces the range of stakeholder concerns—including issues such as privacy, data ownership, workforce, and equitable access that are acknowledged but not treated in depth—rather than allowing the investigators' own interests to determine what counts as important [1]. Making these priorities publicly available enables other researchers and funders to direct attention to questions that the stakeholder community, rather than any single institution, identifies as pressing.

Third, the project will develop and present alternative policy approaches for one tractable issue in each of the three domains (analytical validity and testing quality; patents and licensing; reimbursement and payment), accompanied by a transparent account of where stakeholder positions converge, where they diverge, and what would require negotiation or compromise [1]. The emphasis on clarifying options and recording the grounds for agreement and disagreement—rather than manufacturing consensus—is an intellectually honest and practically useful contribution [1]. Policymakers need to understand not only what might be done but also who supports and opposes different approaches and why; the project will deliver that understanding for the selected issues.

The project's value is enhanced by its dissemination design. Aim 3 is integrated throughout the project rather than treated as an afterthought, with early engagement of policymakers, Year 2 and Year 3 Capitol Hill briefings with follow-up meetings, and a range of accessible products including white papers, issue briefs, webcasts, and electronic landscape materials [1]. This active dissemination strategy increases the likelihood that the descriptive and analytical products will reach the audiences who can use them.

The principal limitation affecting scientific value is the selectivity built into the design. The project will develop detailed policy alternatives for only three issues—one per domain—selected by the investigators and advisory board from the ranked list [1]. Issues outside these three domains, however highly ranked, will remain as published priorities without receiving detailed policy analysis. This is a defensible scoping decision given the resources and expertise available, but it means the project's direct impact on policy development is concentrated on a limited set of questions whose selection is not fully specified in advance. The value of the broader priority list and the landscape analysis is less affected by this limitation; these products retain substantial independent worth as descriptive and agenda-setting contributions.

## Innovation

**Innovation: B (Confidence: B).** The project is methodologically innovative in its deliberate integration of a systematic industry landscape analysis, qualitative interviews, and a structured Policy Delphi to address a domain where empirical evidence was limited and stakeholder positions were both diverse and entrenched. The innovation resides not in inventing new techniques, but in combining established methods in a configuration specifically suited to the problem—and in treating the outputs of that configuration as primary knowledge contributions rather than as preliminary steps toward a single recommendation.

**Landscape contribution.** The landscape analysis (Aim 1) applies a documentary and interview-based mapping approach, already proven in the Genetics and Public Policy Center's prior work on direct-to-consumer genetic testing, to a substantially new target: the emerging WGS industry [1]. The systematic collection of structured data across technology developers, sequencing providers, and interpretation services—capturing business models, patent claims, health-related claims, CLIA certification, and third-party coverage—goes beyond the ad hoc company profiles that were available at the time [1]. This descriptive contribution represents a qualitative advance in the level of detail and comparability available to the policy community.

**Policy Delphi as a core analytical engine.** The central innovation is the use of a Policy Delphi, following the Turoff model, as the project's core analytical engine rather than as a supplementary consensus exercise [1]. The Policy Delphi differs fundamentally from conventional Delphi applications in that it explicitly treats disagreement as informative output rather than as failure or noise. The design commits to surveying approximately 40 stakeholders from industry, healthcare, and regulatory/policy/patient constituencies across five rounds spanning approximately 20 months, with the explicit statement that "complete stakeholder consensus is not the intended endpoint" [1]. This is a conceptually important choice in a domain where technology developers, clinical laboratories, payers, regulators, and patient groups hold structurally different interests and where premature consensus would likely paper over genuine conflicts that policymakers must ultimately navigate.

The two-phase Delphi structure further strengthens the innovative character. The first phase (Surveys 1–2) generates and ranks policy questions using transparent composite scores—Priority (importance + validity), Tractability (technical + political feasibility), and Overall—with median values used for ranking [1]. The second phase (Surveys 3–5) develops and iteratively evaluates policy alternatives, with panelists ranking resolutions' desirability and feasibility and adding arguments for and against each option [1]. The final two-day meeting with anonymous real-time voting adds a deliberative dimension that allows the project to explore the complexity behind the numerical rankings, including implications for different stakeholder groups and for clinical WGS implementation [1].

**Methodological and dissemination reflexivity.** The project also makes the Delphi process itself an object of study: the final meeting will evaluate participants' perceptions of the method's usefulness for integrating perspectives, identifying priorities, and developing alternatives, generating methodological knowledge applicable to other complex health-technology policy settings [1]. The dissemination plan further extends the project's novelty by embedding active engagement with policy audiences throughout the research, including Capitol Hill briefings in Years 2 and 3 structured to gather feedback that informs the final report [1]. This iterative relationship between research and policy audiences is not yet standard in health-policy research and represents a worthwhile departure from the more common model of producing publications after the research is complete.

**Scope transparency.** The project is appropriately candid about domains it will not address in depth. Issues such as privacy, data ownership, workforce needs, and equitable access are acknowledged as consequential and will be captured in the published priority list, but detailed policy alternatives will concentrate on the three domains where the collaborating teams bring specific expertise [1]. This transparency about scope—treating important questions outside the study's focus as visible in the published priorities rather than as answered—is a responsible approach that increases the credibility of the contributions the project does make.

The innovation is incremental rather than transformative—the individual methods are established, and the Policy Delphi has been applied in other technology-assessment contexts—but the specific combination, the target domain, and the commitment to treating structured disagreement as a primary output together constitute a genuinely original contribution to the policy-research toolkit for genomic medicine.

## Rigor

**Rigor: B (Confidence: B).** The mixed-methods design is well-justified for the research questions and the state of evidence, and each component follows established practice in its respective tradition. The project's central inferential strength is a transparent, multi-stage process that connects a systematic documentary and interview-based landscape to a structured stakeholder deliberation, with explicit procedures for generating, ranking, and refining both policy questions and policy alternatives. Key limitations—the under-specification of the issue-selection decision rule and the absence of an attrition management plan—are material but do not undermine the fundamental validity of the descriptive and analytical contributions.

**Landscape and interviews (Aim 1).** The documentary search strategy is systematic, combining PubMed, internet searches, NEXIS media databases, the USPTO database, LexisNexis, and direct contact with companies when necessary [1]. The initial landscape table lists 34 organizations spanning technology developers, sequencing services, and interpretation providers, which provides a credible and appropriately broad starting point [1]. The structured data to be collected for each entity—technologies, platform capabilities, services, customer groups, patent claims, health-related claims, CLIA certification, and third-party coverage—are well-chosen to support subsequent policy analysis [1].

The semi-structured interviews with up to 20 senior industry leaders, purposively sampled from at least 40 eligible organizations and continuing to thematic saturation, follow established qualitative research practice [1]. The use of NVivo for content and thematic analysis, with a focus on emerging business models and pathways to clinical integration, is appropriate [1]. A limitation is that the proposal does not specify how thematic saturation will be determined operationally—a common gap in qualitative research that introduces discretion but does not invalidate the approach. The commitment to triangulating interview findings with documentary evidence provides a cross-check against relying solely on informant accounts.

**Delphi prioritization (Aim 2, phase 1).** The scoring system is clearly specified: four dimensions (importance, validity, technical feasibility, political feasibility) rated on five-point Likert scales, combined into Priority (importance + validity, range 0–8), Tractability (technical + political feasibility, range 0–8), and Overall (sum of all four, range 0–16), with median values used for ranking [1]. This is a reasonable and transparent approach. However, the equal weighting implied by simple summation is not justified, and the proposal does not mention sensitivity analyses or alternative weighting schemes. This is a manageable limitation—alternative analyses could be explored during implementation—but the project would be strengthened by an explicit plan to test whether the resulting rankings are robust to different weighting assumptions.

**Policy development and legal analysis (Aim 2, phase 2).** The iterative development of policy alternatives through Surveys 3–5 is well-structured: open-ended suggestion, expert-generated alternatives using the Patton and Sawicki framework, ranking of desirability and feasibility, feedback of group statistics, and final ranking with arguments for and against each option [1]. The legal analysis examines relevant federal and state laws, regulations, professional guidelines, and industry standards across the three domains, coordinated by Kaufman and McGuire [1]. However, the description of the legal analysis is less detailed than the Delphi component; the proposal does not specify how the legal analyses will be validated or cross-checked across the three contributing institutions (GPPC, Duke, CMTP), which introduces some uncertainty about consistency across the three domain white papers.

**Critical junctures and limitations.** Two limitations merit particular attention because they affect the project's central deliverables.

The selection of the three issues for detailed policy analysis is a pivotal moment that determines much of the project's direct policy impact. The proposal describes this as involving the investigators and advisory board consulting the ranked list to choose "the most important tractable issue" in each domain [1]. The criteria beyond the composite scores are not fully specified, and a more structured decision rule or explicit commitment to documenting the rationale for selection would strengthen transparency. The advisory board's involvement, discussed under Management, provides external scrutiny at this juncture but does not substitute for a specified decision framework.

Panel attrition over the approximately 20-month, five-survey process is a well-known challenge in Delphi studies. The proposal does not discuss retention strategies or how partial non-response will be treated analytically. Attrition that differentially affects stakeholder categories could shift the balance of perspectives represented in later rounds, potentially affecting the policy alternatives developed and the account of agreement and disagreement.

**Epistemological positioning.** The Policy Delphi produces informed stakeholder judgement rather than empirical evidence of effectiveness. The proposal is appropriately modest about this, and the final meeting with anonymous real-time voting provides a forum to verify whether staff have accurately represented options, agreement, and disagreement [1]. Including minority views in the resulting account is specifically committed [1], which aligns with the Policy Delphi's epistemological stance that structured dissensus is informative output. Taken together, the design provides a defensible basis for the descriptive and analytical contributions claimed, while the identified limitations affect the precision and transparency of the process rather than its fundamental validity.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility: B (Confidence: B).** The project is well-supported by a team whose collective expertise aligns directly with each major component of the proposed work, and the three-year phased schedule is realistic given the scope of activities. The proposal demonstrates that the investigators have the necessary access, experience, and institutional infrastructure to execute the plan as described. The principal uncertainty concerns the absence of detailed budget information in the provided narrative, which limits the evidence for resource adequacy without constituting evidence of insufficiency.

**Team expertise.** The core feasibility strength is the complementary expertise assembled across the collaborating institutions. The Genetics and Public Policy Center (GPPC) at Johns Hopkins has conducted prior landscape analyses of genetic testing companies and has experience using interviews, surveys, focus groups, and discrete-choice methods to investigate genetic testing and biobank participation [1]. This prior work provides a direct methodological precedent for the Aim 1 landscape and interview activities, reducing the risk that the systematic mapping of the WGS industry will encounter unanticipated difficulties. McGuire has published on the regulation of direct-to-consumer genetic testing and on ethical and policy questions in whole-genome research [1], providing the substantive policy expertise needed to lead the development of policy approaches. Cook-Deegan and colleagues at Duke have systematically examined DNA sequencing technologies, patents, business models, and intellectual-property issues in multi-allele testing [1], as confirmed by the 2010 Cook-Deegan and Heaney review documenting the evidence on patents in genomics and human genetics [2]. Tunis and Deverka bring directly relevant expertise in coverage and reimbursement for molecular diagnostics and pharmacogenomics [1], aligning with the reimbursement/payment domain. Messner's specific experience conducting qualitative stakeholder research, organizing technical panels, and implementing Delphi processes [1] addresses the most methodologically demanding component—the five-survey, 20-month Policy Delphi.

The team has also demonstrated its capacity to convene stakeholders who do not share a single institutional perspective; previous engagement included a national summit with more than 60 participants from regulatory agencies, professional organizations, industry, and genetic-testing laboratories [1]. This track record supports both the logistics of recruiting the approximately 40-member stakeholder panel and the proposed emphasis on managing disagreement constructively.

**Schedule.** The three-year schedule is appropriately phased. Year 1 concentrates on the landscape analysis, interviews, and initial engagement—activities that do not depend on the Delphi panel being fully constituted [1]. This sequencing is sensible because the landscape products, particularly the integrated report combining documentary and interview findings, are themselves inputs to the Delphi panel's work [1]. Year 2 initiates panel recruitment and preparation, conducts the two prioritization surveys, selects the three issues for detailed analysis, and delivers the first Capitol Hill briefing. Year 3 accommodates the three policy-development surveys, the two-day stakeholder meeting, final analysis, and the second briefing. The schedule builds in the iterative feedback loops that the Delphi method requires while maintaining a pace consistent with a three-year grant period.

**Dissemination capacity and adaptability.** The engagement of Burness Communications for stakeholder identification, communication strategy, events, and archiving [1] adds dedicated professional capacity for dissemination, reducing the burden on the research team. The plan for two Capitol Hill briefings with follow-up meetings with federal officials and congressional staff [1] is concrete and appears achievable given the project's Washington presence and the advisory board's connections. The commitment to monitoring WGS technologies, business models, regulations, and litigation throughout the study, with a mandate to reassess the research strategy and prioritize broader questions that cut across particular technologies [1], provides a responsible mechanism for maintaining relevance in a rapidly changing environment.

**Resource evidence.** The main limitation for the feasibility assessment is that the provided proposal narrative does not include an itemized budget, detailed staffing plan, or institutional commitment letters. The evaluation guidance specifies that this absence alone does not demonstrate incapacity or inadequate funding, and the documented expertise and institutional track record support the capacity to execute the plan. However, the confidence in this rating is tempered by the inability to verify that the personnel effort allocated to each activity—particularly the labor-intensive landscape analysis and the coordination of five Delphi surveys across multiple institutions—is sufficient relative to the demands of the work.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety: B (Confidence: B).** The ethics and data protection arrangements are thorough, proportionate to the activities proposed, and appropriately attentive to the specific vulnerabilities created by a multi-stakeholder policy deliberation that touches on commercially and legally sensitive information. The project involves two categories of human participation—semi-structured interviews with industry leaders and the Delphi surveys and summit—neither of which imposes physical risk. The ethical challenge is primarily informational: participants may disclose views about business strategies, regulatory approaches, or policy positions that, if attributed, could carry commercial, legal, or reputational consequences. The proposal addresses this challenge through a layered set of procedural protections that align with established research ethics practice.

**Consent and confidentiality protections.** The consent process is described for both the interviews and the summit: participants receive study information in advance and provide verbal consent [1]. The proposal acknowledges the small but real possibility that commercially or legally sensitive information could be disclosed, including information potentially subject to subpoena, and commits to requesting a Certificate of Confidentiality to provide an additional legal shield against compelled disclosure [1]. This acknowledgement is important because it demonstrates that the investigators have considered the specific risk profile of policy research involving industry participants, rather than applying a generic consent template.

Data protection measures are specified in concrete detail. Identifying information is held separately from numbered interview recordings and transcripts [1]. Electronic survey access uses individual links or passwords, and responses are encrypted and password-protected [1]. The summit is closed to outside participants and the press, creating a space where participants can speak candidly without concern about public attribution [1]. Transcripts are de-identified before analysis, and comments are reported in aggregate [1]. The meeting recording is destroyed after transcription [1]. These measures form a coherent protective architecture: separate storage reduces the risk of inadvertent identification, encryption protects against unauthorized access, de-identification and aggregate reporting prevent individual attribution in published outputs, and destruction of recordings eliminates a persistent source of identifiable data.

**Delphi-specific ethical design.** The Delphi design itself has ethical dimensions that the proposal addresses. The use of coded two-letter identifiers for surveys, combined with an initial group-building exercise that shares participants' names, affiliations, and expertise without linking them to specific survey responses, allows the panel to know the range of experience represented while preserving the anonymity of individual contributions during the iterative rounds [1]. This is a well-considered balance: the Policy Delphi's intellectual value depends on participants being able to challenge arguments without the inhibition that would accompany immediate public attribution, while the group's credibility depends on transparency about who is at the table.

**Inclusion and dissemination.** The proposal states that recruitment will not exclude people on the basis of sex, race, or ethnicity, while acknowledging that the demographic composition of the relevant professional populations is not sufficiently defined to specify enrollment targets [1]. This is a candid and defensible position for research that recruits institutional representatives based on expertise and stakeholder category rather than from a defined population. The dissemination plan includes a commitment to respecting the confidentiality of individual contributions when sharing study findings through publications, websites, public and policy briefings [1]. The public products—the landscape, priorities, policy alternatives, and methodological findings—are structural outputs of the Delphi process rather than individual-level data, which aligns with the protective measures described.

No material gaps in the ethics framework are evident. The arrangements are proportionate to the informational risks involved, and the investigators demonstrate an understanding of both the general principles and the context-specific challenges of conducting policy research with industry and stakeholder participants.

## Management

**Management: B (Confidence: B).** The project's management arrangements are well-conceived, with a clear division of labor, specified coordination mechanisms, and an independent advisory board providing governance at key decision points. The documented expertise and institutional track record support the capacity to execute the plan as described. The principal constraint on this assessment is the absence of an itemized budget or detailed staffing plan in the provided narrative; the evaluation guidance specifies that this alone does not demonstrate incapacity, but it limits the evidence available to confirm resource adequacy.

**Leadership and coordination.** The co-leadership structure assigns complementary responsibilities that align with the investigators' expertise. Kaufman and McGuire jointly lead the project and share responsibility for the landscape analysis, which benefits from both investigators' familiarity with genetic testing policy and the GPPC's prior landscape work [1]. Kaufman oversees implementation of the Delphi process and dissemination, while McGuire primarily oversees development of policy approaches [1]. This division separates the two most operationally demanding components—managing five survey rounds and the two-day meeting, and coordinating the legal and policy analyses across three institutions—while maintaining joint accountability for the integrated products.

Coordination across institutions is addressed through specified mechanisms rather than left to ad hoc communication. Weekly communication between the co-PIs and regular project meetings involving key personnel from all institutions are explicitly committed [1]. Given that the policy analyses are distributed across GPPC, Duke, and CMTP, each addressing one of the three domains, the role of Kaufman and McGuire in coordinating these analyses is specifically noted [1]. This is an appropriate arrangement, though the proposal does not detail how consistency across the three white papers will be assured—a point that also affects the Rigor assessment and tempers confidence in management of this particular deliverable.

**Advisory board governance.** The eight-member advisory board, distinct from the 40-person stakeholder panel, provides an independent governance layer. Its expertise encompasses clinical genetics, sequencing technology, regulation, economics, intellectual property, and consumer protection—covering the domains that the project must navigate [1]. The board's five video-conference meetings are scheduled to align with critical junctures: clarifying study goals, discussing the landscape, advising on the selection of the three policy issues, reviewing developing policy approaches, and guiding dissemination and future research [1]. The board's role in advising on issue selection partially addresses the concern, noted under Rigor, that the decision rule for choosing the three questions for detailed analysis is not fully specified; the board provides external scrutiny at precisely this juncture.

**Operational capacity.** The three-year phased schedule distributes activities sensibly across the project period, with the landscape and interviews concentrated in Year 1, panel recruitment and prioritization in Year 2, and policy development, the final meeting, and dissemination extending into Year 3 [1]. The engagement of Burness Communications for stakeholder identification, communication strategy, events, and archiving adds dedicated professional capacity for dissemination [1]. The team's track record in convening stakeholders—including a national summit with more than 60 participants from regulatory agencies, professional organizations, industry, and genetic-testing laboratories—supports the logistical feasibility of recruiting and managing the approximately 40-member Delphi panel across the 20-month process [1]. The commitment to monitoring technological and regulatory developments and adjusting the research strategy [1] provides a responsible mechanism for maintaining relevance, though it also implies a coordination demand that the specified weekly communication and regular project meetings are designed to meet.

# Project review

## Overall assessment

This R01 project proposes a prospective, longitudinal observational study to define trajectories of linguistic, cognitive-communicative, and quality-of-life recovery from stroke-related aphasia. The study will enroll 400 participants across three inpatient rehabilitation facilities, following them for 18 months with a comprehensive battery of performance-based and patient-reported outcomes. The work’s most valuable expected contribution is an integrated model of aphasia recovery that, for the first time, simultaneously describes how impairment, communicative participation, and quality of life evolve together or diverge, and how these trajectories are associated with detailed therapy content, genetic variants, and resting-state brain network connectivity. This will supplant the current fragmented understanding derived from isolated domains, providing a rich, multi-dimensional characterization of recovery.

The proposal is supported by a well-articulated rationale that identifies a genuine need to move beyond isolated outcome measurement and small, retrospective samples. The multi-site design, use of NINDS Common Data Elements, and sophisticated analytic plan with individual growth-curve models and rigorous internal validation—bootstrap stability assessment and Monte Carlo cross-validation—are significant strengths. A key supporting element is the innovative plan to measure therapy by modality using clinician logs, addressing a known blind spot in rehabilitation research. The primary material limitations are the observational design, which precludes causal inference about treatment effects, and an acknowledged but unresolved risk of overfitting in Aim 2 given the very wide range of predictors relative to the planned sample of 300. The genetic and imaging components, while integrated, remain exploratory due to limited statistical power, particularly in the 100-person imaging subsample.

The project’s ambitious scope is its greatest strength and its primary source of risk. The same integration of multiple predictor domains that makes the study scientifically valuable also creates challenges around model overfitting and the need for a large, well-characterized sample. However, the proposal’s careful attention to internal validation in Aim 3 provides a credible mechanism for evaluating and tempering these risks, demonstrating scientific maturity. The absence of a causal design for treatment effects is correctly framed as a feature of the study type, not a flaw. On balance, the potential to generate an unprecedented, integrated model of aphasia recovery—laying the foundation for a generation of more precise, holistic research—strongly outweighs the acknowledged limitations.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**The project addresses a question of substantial clinical and scientific importance: how do linguistic impairment, communicative participation, and quality of life recover after stroke-related aphasia, and what factors are associated with their distinct trajectories?** No existing prospective cohort simultaneously characterizes these three outcome domains alongside detailed therapy content, genetic markers, and resting-state connectivity across an 18-month window. The study will therefore produce the most comprehensive longitudinal characterization of aphasia recovery to date.

The proposal’s rationale correctly diagnoses a fragmented literature. Prior studies have emphasized linguistic outcomes, used small or retrospective samples, or measured only a few endpoints [1]. The most closely comparable recent work, by Levy et al. (2024), enrolled 217 patients and used support vector regression with lesion location and initial severity to predict approximately 60% of outcome variance [2]. That study—which its authors describe as an early step requiring “even more predictors planned for inclusion” [2]—retained only 74 patients at 12 months [2] and did not measure therapy exposure, genetic variants, resting-state connectivity, or participation and quality-of-life outcomes. The present proposal extends the time horizon to 18 months, adds five assessment points including inpatient discharge, and targets 300 retained participants across three sites [1]. These differences represent a material advance in scope and depth.

**Three features define the knowledge the study can deliver.** First, the selection of patient-reported communicative participation (Communicative Participation Item Bank) and aphasia-specific quality of life (Stroke and Aphasia Quality of Life Scale-39) as primary outcomes, alongside the Western Aphasia Battery–Revised Aphasia Quotient [1], enables the study to test the clinically important hypothesis that participation and quality of life may continue improving after linguistic recovery plateaus [1]. This moves the field beyond the assumption that impairment-level change adequately represents meaningful recovery.

Second, the therapy measurement approach addresses a recognized evidence gap. Billing records capture time and service codes but do not describe what language modality was treated [1]. The proposed tablet-based clinician logs with modality coding and reliability checks [1], combined with post-discharge patient and caregiver checklists verified by weekly research assistant calls [1], align with recommendations that therapeutic ingredients require direct measurement rather than reliance on session duration alone [3]. The resulting dataset will allow the field to examine, for the first time, how the content and timing of real-world therapy are associated with domain-specific outcomes.

Third, the integrated modeling framework—combining demographic, clinical, treatment, genetic, and imaging predictors within individual growth-curve models and subjecting them to internal validation [1]—will generate a systematically evaluated set of associations. The interactive trajectory tool planned as an output [1] offers a practical dissemination vehicle, provided it is interpreted as describing observed associations rather than causal predictions, consistent with the study’s observational design. Collectively, the project will deliver an unprecedented resource for hypothesis generation and a stronger empirical foundation for designing future intervention studies that target the outcomes patients care about.

## Innovation

**The study’s primary innovation is a deliberate, prospective integration of multiple outcome and predictor domains that prior research has examined in isolation.** The contribution is not a single novel measurement or predictor, but the construction of a unified observational framework that simultaneously tracks impairment, communicative participation, and quality of life while measuring detailed therapy content, candidate genetic markers, and resting-state brain connectivity. This integrated design—applied to a multi-site cohort followed through 18 months—represents a conceptual advance beyond the current literature.

Current evidence illustrates the fragmentation the proposal seeks to overcome. The largest recent prospective aphasia study, by Levy et al. (2024), applied support vector regression to lesion location and initial severity in 217 patients, yet the authors described the work as an early step requiring “even more predictors planned for inclusion” [2] and noted that the brief Quick Aphasia Battery cannot comprehensively account for all aspects of language [2]. That study retained 74 patients at 12 months [2] and did not incorporate therapy exposure, resting-state connectivity, or participation and quality-of-life measures. The present proposal extends the time horizon to 18 months, adds five assessment points including inpatient discharge, and targets 300 retained participants [1]—creating a substantially richer observational platform.

**Three design choices embody the project’s originality.** First, the joint designation of linguistic, communicative-participation, and quality-of-life outcomes as co-primary endpoints [1] allows the study to test whether participation and well-being continue improving after linguistic recovery slows, a hypothesis stated explicitly in Aim 1 [1]. This contrasts with the widespread practice of treating patient-reported outcomes as secondary complements to impairment tests.

Second, the therapy-exposure measurement moves beyond billing-record approximations. Clinician logs will code each treatment task by target modality—auditory comprehension, oral expression, reading comprehension, written expression—with reliability checks using video-recorded sessions [1]. Post-discharge therapy is captured through weekly patient or caregiver checklists supported by research-assistant calls [1]. This approach responds to calls for specifying therapeutic ingredients rather than relying on session duration alone [3], and it will generate the most granular description of real-world aphasia rehabilitation yet assembled in a longitudinal cohort.

Third, the analytical strategy places five predictor groups—demographic, clinical, therapy, genetic, and imaging—into a single individual-growth-curve framework and systematically compares their incremental contributions [1]. No existing aphasia study has combined these domains in a prospectively designed model with planned internal validation through bootstrap resampling and Monte Carlo cross-validation [1]. The interactive trajectory tool planned as a dissemination output [1] further translates the integrated model into a potentially useful clinical resource, provided its observational basis is clearly communicated.

## Rigor

**The study employs a design that is well-matched to its descriptive and associational aims, producing a credible, carefully specified route to the intended inferences while acknowledging the inherent limitations of an observational framework.** The multi-site cohort, comprehensive assessment battery, detailed therapy-exposure measurement, and individual-growth-curve analytic strategy are all appropriate to the questions posed. The project’s explicit validation steps—500 bootstrap resamples and Monte Carlo cross-validation—provide meaningful internal checks on model stability and prediction error, a stronger approach than is customary in aphasia outcome studies.

### Design structure and trajectory estimation

The design constructs three linked aims that build logically from description to explanation to internal validation [1]. Aim 1 estimates unconditional recovery curves for each primary and secondary outcome, testing the central hypothesis that linguistic improvement slows around six months while communicative participation and quality of life continue to improve toward 18 months. The choice of individual growth-curve models with participant random intercepts and slopes, and the planned comparison of linear, polynomial, and negative-exponential forms using residual diagnostics and information criteria [1], is appropriate for trajectory data with repeated measures. Categorical time models provide a sensitivity check against misspecified functional forms. The five-point schedule—admission, discharge, 6, 12, and 18 months—captures both the early inpatient phase and the longer community-based recovery period where participation and quality-of-life divergence is expected.

### Covariate modeling and internal validation

Aim 2 extends the selected time function with demographic, clinical, therapy, imaging, and genetic covariates, comparing models built from separate predictor groups and then combined [1]. Purposeful model building with clinically relevant interactions and appropriate transformations of continuous predictors is a sensible middle ground between atheoretical automated selection and a rigid pre-specified model. The explicit comparison of nested predictor sets—demographic plus clinical; demographic plus biomarkers; demographic, clinical and biomarkers; demographic, clinical and treatment; and all five groups together—provides a transparent decomposition of incremental predictive information.

The internal validation in Aim 3 is a notable strength. Five hundred bootstrap resamples will assess coefficient distributions and predictor-selection frequencies, flagging variables with low reproducibility for reconsideration [1]. Monte Carlo cross-validation with 500 independent training-validation splits will evaluate prediction of held-out outcomes, with performance judged against the relevant minimally clinically important difference [1]. This combination of stability and prediction checks is more rigorous than the single hold-out or leave-one-out approaches common in aphasia prediction studies, and provides a credible internal safeguard against overfitting.

### Assessment battery and therapy measurement

The assessment battery is comprehensive yet selected with clear justification. The primary linguistic outcome (WAB-R Aphasia Quotient), cognitive-communicative outcome (Communicative Participation Item Bank), and quality-of-life outcome (Stroke and Aphasia Quality of Life Scale-39) address distinct constructs [1]. Secondary measures capture attention, communication confidence, caregiver-rated effectiveness, mood, fatigue, and social participation [1], and the battery follows NINDS Common Data Elements where available [1], facilitating future harmonization. The therapy-exposure measurement is particularly rigorous: clinician tablet logs code each task by target modality with a \>90% reliability target, verified by video-recording 10% of sessions [1]; post-discharge services are tracked through weekly patient or caregiver checklists supported by research-assistant calls [1]. This granularity addresses known limitations of billing-record proxies [1].

### Principal limitations

**The principal limitations are correctly identified in the proposal and affect the strength of the inferences that can be drawn rather than the study’s internal validity.** Treatment is not randomized, and the proposal acknowledges that observed therapy–outcome associations will be associational: “treatment was not randomized” [1]. Patients with more severe deficits tend to receive more therapy [4], creating confounding by indication that statistical adjustment cannot fully resolve. The individual growth-curve models will describe how therapy exposure co-varies with recovery trajectories conditional on measured covariates, but they cannot establish that more therapy causes better outcomes. This is an inherent feature of the observational design, not a flaw, and the proposal does not claim otherwise; the interactive trajectory tool must be interpreted accordingly [1].

Statistical power for Aim 2 is an acknowledged uncertainty. The proposal states candidly that long-term aphasia parameters are unavailable for reliable simulation-based sample-size estimation [1]. With five predictor groups and multiple outcomes, the risk of overfitting and unstable coefficient estimates is real even at n=300. The bootstrap stability assessment in Aim 3 partially addresses this after model selection has occurred, but it cannot guarantee that all true associations are detected or that selected predictors are unbiased. For the imaging subsample of 100 participants, power to detect connectivity-based predictors with small-to-moderate effects is limited; Levy et al. acknowledged that even n=217 was insufficient for some language subdomains [2]. Resting-state fMRI is acquired only at admission [1], which captures baseline predictive information but cannot describe how connectivity changes relate to recovery—a limitation that constrains the imaging contribution to static prediction rather than dynamic biomarker development.

Missing data pose a further challenge. The expected 25% attrition (400 enrolled to retain 300 at 18 months) is realistic [1], and the models will use all available assessments under the missing-at-random assumption [1]. However, the proposal acknowledges that dropout may violate this assumption, and no sensitivity analysis for missing-not-at-random patterns is specified. The genetic analysis, with 10 SNPs across 6 genes examined within an epistasis framework, involves a large effective number of comparisons relative to the sample size, and the findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. These limitations are well understood and do not undermine the study’s core descriptive and associational value, but they temper the precision and generalizability of the factor-identification conclusions.

## Feasibility

**The project is highly feasible as planned, with a realistic schedule, demonstrated procedural readiness, adequate recruitment infrastructure, and well-specified contingency measures.** The five-year timeline, multi-site recruitment across three established inpatient rehabilitation facilities, and integration with routine clinical workflows provide a strong operational foundation. The proposal’s honest acknowledgment of key uncertainties—particularly around statistical power and retention—is accompanied by appropriate mitigation strategies that strengthen confidence in execution.

### Schedule and recruitment

The five-year schedule is detailed and practical [1]. Site approvals, REDCap database setup, and staff training occupy the first two quarters, followed by approximately 30 months of accrual (Year 1 Quarter 3 through Year 3 Quarter 3) with staggered 6-, 12-, and 18-month follow-up visits extending into Year 5. This sequencing creates substantial overlap between enrollment and follow-up, allowing the team to monitor accrual and retention rates while follow-up is still underway. Therapy extraction and data cleaning proceed in parallel with data collection, concentrating the computationally intensive growth-model development, hypothesis testing, bootstrap stability assessment, and cross-validation in the final period. The schedule explicitly allows extension or addition of a fourth site if accrual or retention proves insufficient [1], providing a concrete operational contingency.

Recruitment feasibility rests on realistic targets and demonstrated access to the clinical population. The three sites—Shirley Ryan AbilityLab in Chicago, Alexian Brothers Rehabilitation Hospital, and Mary Free Bed Rehabilitation Hospital—represent a mix of urban and regional rehabilitation settings, supporting generalizability while keeping coordination manageable. The expected attrition of 15% by six months and an additional 10% by 18 months is consistent with longitudinal stroke studies; Levy et al. (2024) retained 74 of 217 patients at 12 months [2]. Daily screening of admissions by dedicated research assistants, combined with aphasia-supportive consent procedures [1], addresses the practical challenge of identifying and enrolling patients with communication impairments during brief inpatient stays.

### Procedural readiness

Procedural feasibility is supported by pilot data and preparatory work. Extraction of billing records from 71 stroke admissions demonstrated that therapy time can be assembled from existing administrative data [1], and a survey of 56 speech-language pathologists at participating sites confirmed the feasibility of identifying targeted treatment modalities [1]. The tablet-based clinician logs with modality coding and reliability checks—training clinicians until they exceed 90% reliability, verifying 10% of sessions through video recording [1]—provide a practical mechanism for capturing therapy content without imposing an unsustainable documentation burden. Prior work by this team has applied individual growth-curve models in spinal cord injury and traumatic brain injury populations [1], demonstrating in-house expertise with the core analytic method.

The imaging subsample of 100 participants drawn from the Shirley Ryan AbilityLab [1] is feasible given that site’s volume and research infrastructure. The specialized MRI protocol includes two resting-state scans to estimate reliability, T1 anatomical, T2 FLAIR, and two-shell diffusion imaging [1], fitting within a single research session. Saliva collection for genetic analysis uses commercial Oragene Discover kits with standard extraction and genotyping protocols [1], requiring no specialized equipment beyond what is described. The REDCap platform supports common case-report forms, central data monitoring, and eventual de-identified data sharing [1], providing an established, low-risk data management solution.

The principal feasibility risk—uncertain statistical power for Aim 2—is acknowledged rather than ignored. The proposal states that suitable long-term aphasia parameters are unavailable for simulation-based sample-size estimation [1]. This is a scientific limitation, but it does not make the project infeasible; the descriptive trajectory estimation in Aim 1 is well-supported at n=300, and the internal validation in Aim 3 provides a mechanism for assessing which associational findings are robust. The team’s contingency approach—monitoring accrual, extending recruitment if necessary, or adding a site [1]—is pragmatic and preserves the study’s core value even if the full covariate model requires cautious interpretation.

## Ethics and safety

**The ethics and safety plan is excellent, with all material risks identified and managed through proportionate, well-specified procedures tailored to the study population and design.** The proposal demonstrates thorough attention to the distinctive ethical challenges of research involving people with aphasia, for whom communication impairments can threaten both the validity of consent and the burden of participation.

### Consent and participant protection

The consent process directly addresses the core ethical challenge of enrolling participants with communication disorders. The proposal specifies that consent will use supported conversation techniques to ensure that aphasia does not itself prevent understanding [1]. This goes beyond standard consent formalities by embedding communication support—a practice recommended for aphasia research but not universally implemented—into the recruitment workflow. Eligibility further requires sufficient capacity to consent and participate in testing, with aphasia-supportive communication used during both recruitment and assessment [1]. Participation is explicitly voluntary, and withdrawal will not affect clinical services [1], a necessary assurance given the study’s location within clinical rehabilitation settings where patients may perceive research participation as expected.

The risk mitigation strategy is comprehensive and proportionate. The principal risks—fatigue, frustration, MRI discomfort, and loss of confidentiality [1]—are characteristic of longitudinal behavioral research in a medically complex population. Frequent breaks and the option to split follow-up visits across consecutive days or conduct them at home [1] directly address fatigue, which is both an ethical concern and a data-quality concern. Trained communication support throughout assessment sessions reduces frustration and ensures that participants can express discomfort or a wish to stop. MRI eligibility is managed through standard screening for implants, claustrophobia, and pregnancy; only eligible participants undergo research scanning [1], and participants unable to undergo MRI can still contribute core observational data [1], avoiding coercion by preserving study inclusion regardless of imaging eligibility.

### Oversight and data protection

The oversight infrastructure is well-constructed and includes patient and family representation. A Data Monitoring and Oversight Committee will review readiness initially and then evaluate safety, burden, progress, and data integrity annually [1]. The committee’s composition—incorporating clinical, imaging, genetic, epidemiologic, and patient/family perspectives—is notable for including the very stakeholders whose welfare is at issue. This structure is particularly appropriate for a study collecting genetic samples and neuroimaging data alongside behavioral outcomes, where the aggregate burden may accumulate across domains. Adverse events will be communicated through site leadership to the co-PIs and institutional oversight according to a stated reporting schedule [1]. The use of Northwestern as the single IRB [1] streamlines ethical review across the three sites while maintaining centralized accountability.

Data protection measures are standard and adequate. Research records will be separated from medical records and identified by codes rather than names; electronic files will be password-protected, physical records secured, and access restricted [1]. The de-identified REDCap dataset will be available on request under an agreement that prohibits reidentification and unauthorized redistribution, defines reporting responsibilities, and requires destruction after analyses [1]. The shared data package—covering behavioral and patient-reported outcomes, treatment exposure, relevant imaging measures, and candidate-marker information—is substantial enough to support secondary analyses without exposing direct identifiers. The requirement for a data-sharing agreement rather than unconditional open access appropriately balances the benefits of scientific transparency with the risk of reidentification in a dataset combining genetic, imaging, and detailed clinical information from a geographically concentrated cohort.

## Management

**The management plan is sound and well-coordinated, with the necessary expertise, infrastructure, and oversight mechanisms to deliver the study’s aims.** Responsibilities are clearly distributed across a team with demonstrable, complementary experience, and the project’s procedural and data-management arrangements are practical and adequately resourced.

### Team expertise and operational coordination

The investigative team integrates the distinct expertise required for this multi-domain study. The co-PIs bring established track records in aphasia treatment and rehabilitation outcomes research, and their prior collaborative work—including the characterization of inpatient therapy use and the application of individual growth-curve models in spinal cord injury and traumatic brain injury populations—provides direct precedent for the proposed analytic approach [1]. The broader team adds specialized competency in genomics, neuroimaging, and growth modeling, covering the four predictor domains without over-reliance on any single investigator [1].

Operational coordination is built on a clear division of labor and standardized procedures across three sites. Dedicated research assistants will screen admissions daily, coordinate assessments, and maintain post-discharge contact [1]. Standardized training, NIHSS certification, and continuous speech-language pathology supervision support consistency in behavioral assessments across sites [1]. The therapist training and reliability procedures—targeting \>90% agreement on modality coding with 10% of sessions video-recorded for verification, plus provision for research-assistant observation or later coding when clinicians need additional practice [1]—are appropriately detailed and provide a mechanism for maintaining data quality without disrupting clinical workflows.

### Data management and quality control

Data management and quality control are well-specified and proportionate. REDCap provides a common case-report-form platform with central data checks [1], reducing site-to-site variability in data entry. Quality-control procedures include independent rescoring of 10% of videotaped assessment sessions, rechecking of 10% of case-report forms, and a separate 10% data-entry verification sample [1]. These overlapping checks cover the principal sources of measurement and transcription error. The Data Monitoring and Oversight Committee, which includes clinical, imaging, genetic, epidemiologic, and patient/family perspectives, provides external scrutiny of readiness, safety, burden, progress, and data integrity on an annual cycle [1], adding a governance layer appropriate to the study’s complexity and duration.

### The interactive trajectory tool

The interactive trajectory tool is the one deliverable whose management is insufficiently defined. The proposal mentions this tool as an output of the integrated models [1], but its development process, intended users, validation steps, and the personnel responsible for its design and testing are not described. An interactive tool based on associational models from an observational study carries a risk of misinterpretation as a causal prediction instrument, and its construction warrants explicit planning. This gap does not threaten the core scientific aims, but it leaves a dissemination deliverable without clear accountability or quality criteria. Addressing it during the project’s early phase—by assigning responsibility and specifying a user-testing plan—would strengthen an otherwise coherent management framework.

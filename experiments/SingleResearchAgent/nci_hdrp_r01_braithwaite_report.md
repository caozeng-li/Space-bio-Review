# Project review

## Overall assessment

The project aims to determine how co-existing chronic conditions, functional limitations, and impaired pulmonary function modify the benefits and harms of low-dose computed tomography lung cancer screening in routine clinical care. It combines an observational cohort of approximately 34,000 screened adults from three distinct health systems with the CISNET UM-LCS microsimulation model. The most valuable expected knowledge is quantitative evidence on which patient subgroups are likely to benefit from screening, which may experience net harm, and the clinical characteristics that distinguish them—directly informing individualized screening decisions beyond current qualitative guideline statements.

The proposal is supported by a well-motivated evidence gap (NLST participants were substantially healthier than community screening-eligible populations), a coherent conceptual framework, and a team with demonstrated expertise in comorbidity research, lung cancer screening, and microsimulation modeling. Preliminary work on latent comorbidity profiles, NLP nodule identification, and the Watch the Spot data infrastructure provides a credible basis. The main limitations are that the observational cohort lacks an unscreened comparison group and cannot establish causal net benefit; the FRI functional limitation data are unavailable for approximately 20% of the cohort at one site, with the imputation strategy only generically described; and the comorbidity measure relies on a count-based score rather than the richer profiles the team has previously developed.

The current proposal connects a well-justified research question to a feasible multi-site observational and modeling plan, and the team brings the necessary disciplinary expertise. The main limitations are design-inherent: the cohort cannot directly estimate net benefit, the comorbidity exposure is coarser than the team’s own prior work would support, and the handling of site-differential missing functional limitation data is not fully resolved. These issues do not negate the intended contribution, but they bound the strength of conclusions from the observational arms and create some dependency risk for the simulation work. Addressing the missing-data strategy and clarifying the iterative workflow between cohort estimates and model refinement would strengthen the plan without altering its scope.

## Scientific value

**A clearly important question with a well-defined evidence gap.** Current U.S. Preventive Services Task Force guidelines recommend lung cancer screening for adults aged 50–80 with a substantial smoking history, yet qualify this with the statement that screening should be offered only to persons who are “in reasonably good health” and capable of tolerating treatment. This qualitative language provides no transparent quantitative criteria for clinicians or patients, and masks the central physiological tension the proposal identifies: the same smoking exposure that elevates lung cancer risk also drives chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cardiovascular disease, and competing mortality, meaning that patients at highest cancer risk need not obtain the greatest net benefit [1].

The evidence gap is well documented. NLST participants were substantially healthier than community screening-eligible populations—estimated life expectancy was 21.2 years for trial participants versus 14 years for a comparable Medicare subgroup [1]—and the trial excluded adults older than 74. A comparison table in the proposal shows markedly higher prevalence of diabetes, heart disease, and stroke in the real-world eligible population [1]. Extrapolating screening recommendations from this trial to unselected clinical populations therefore carries genuine uncertainty about the benefit–harm balance.

**Three categories of attainable knowledge.** The project promises a detailed descriptive characterization of multimorbidity in approximately 34,000 adults undergoing LDCT screening across three geographically, socioeconomically, and racially distinct health systems: Kaiser Permanente Southern California (an integrated managed-care organization), the Medical University of South Carolina (a safety-net hospital), and VA Portland (a Veterans Affairs system) [1]. This will yield weighted prevalence estimates for co-existing chronic conditions measured by a combined Charlson/Elixhauser score, functional limitations captured by a 13-indicator claims-based FRI score, and spirometry-defined pulmonary impairment, with subgroup estimates by race/ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and age [1].

The project will also produce quantitative estimates of how screening-related harms—false-positive findings, invasive diagnostic procedures, and procedural complications—vary across multimorbidity categories in real-world practice [1]. Such empirical estimates, derived directly from screening cohorts rather than from trial populations or literature assumptions, represent a meaningful advance.

Third, the project will refine a validated CISNET microsimulation model with these cohort-derived inputs, enabling comparison of long-term screening strategies across comorbidity, functional, and pulmonary subgroups and identification of thresholds where benefits and harms resemble those of an average-health reference group [1].

**Practical contribution and its limits.** The contribution to individualized screening discussions and future guideline development is clear, but the observational cohort alone cannot establish causal net benefit because it lacks an unscreened comparison group. The causal question is appropriately shifted to the microsimulation in Aim 3. The project’s value therefore lies in refining the evidence base for personalized screening decisions rather than in resolving the fundamental uncertainty through an experimental design. Even an incremental refinement of this kind—providing clinicians with subgroup-specific estimates of complication risk, false-positive burden, and projected life-years gained across comorbidity strata—would represent a useful step beyond current “reasonably good health” guidance.

**Rating: B (good). Confidence: A (very high).**

## Innovation

**An incremental but well-justified advance, not a breakthrough.** The project adds to prior work primarily by integrating three dimensions of multimorbidity—comorbidity counts, functional limitation via a claims-based FRI, and spirometry-defined pulmonary impairment—within a single real-world screening cohort, and by deriving microsimulation model inputs from that same cohort rather than from trial populations or literature-based assumptions alone.

**Relationship to previous studies.** Previous work has addressed pieces of this picture separately. Kale et al. (2024) parameterized a CISNET microsimulation model for four comorbidities (COPD, diabetes, heart disease, and stroke) using literature-derived inputs, finding that comorbidities reduce life-years gained from screening by approximately 19–44% across age groups [2]. That work demonstrated the importance of comorbidity but did not use empirically estimated inputs from a real-world screened population, did not include functional limitation measures, and employed a different CISNET model. Toumazis et al. (2021) incorporated life expectancy into dynamic screening eligibility and showed improved efficiency over fixed-threshold strategies [3], but focused on optimizing screening schedules rather than on characterizing how specific multimorbidity patterns modify outcomes in contemporary practice. Caverly et al. (2018) identified life expectancy thresholds below which screening becomes preference-sensitive (approximately 10.5 years) [4], yet did not include empirical comorbidity-stratified estimates of screening harms. The proposal’s own preliminary latent-class analysis identified five distinct comorbidity profiles that independently predicted treatment selection and survival in lung cancer patients [5], establishing that comorbidity patterns beyond simple counts are prognostically meaningful.

**What is distinctive.** What distinguishes the current proposal is the decision to feed cohort-derived, subgroup-specific estimates of screening sensitivity, specificity, complication rates, Lung-RADS adherence, and competing mortality directly into the UM-LCS model [1]. This creates a form of internal consistency between observational estimates and simulation that prior comorbidity-modeling studies have not achieved. The application of the FRI to a lung cancer screening population is novel; this instrument was developed and validated for frailty assessment in older adults but has not previously been deployed in screening outcomes research [1]. The explicit comparison framework—finding thresholds where benefits and harms resemble those of an average-health subgroup [1]—provides a pragmatic, clinically interpretable structure for the simulation outputs.

The three participating sites differ in their patient populations, screening implementation pathways, and data systems [1]. This heterogeneity is not merely a sample-size consideration; it tests whether multimorbidity–outcome associations replicate across organizational contexts with different referral patterns and nodule management practices. Single-institution studies cannot provide this evidence.

**Limitations of the innovation profile.** The core conceptual insight—that competing mortality and treatment-related harms may outweigh screening benefit in patients with advanced comorbidity—is already established in the screening literature and in the American Thoracic Society research statement cited in the proposal. The project does not propose a new screening technology, a novel risk-prediction model, or a fundamentally different modeling framework. The NLP nodule-identification algorithm, while enabling, has been validated in prior work (96% sensitivity, 92% specificity) and is applied as infrastructure rather than advanced as a methodological contribution itself [1]. The innovation therefore resides in the specific combination of measures, the empirical grounding of model inputs, the diversity of settings, and the pragmatic clinical framing of the simulation outputs—a meaningful but bounded advance.

**Rating: B (good). Confidence: A (very high).**

## Rigor

**Design strengths appropriately matched to the aims.** The three-site cohort is a genuine strength. By including Kaiser Permanente Southern California, the Medical University of South Carolina, and VA Portland, the study captures geographic, socioeconomic, racial/ethnic, and care-delivery variation that single-institution or claims-only designs cannot provide [1]. The planned cohort of approximately 34,039 screened adults yields adequate power for the primary comparisons: a detectable 5% risk difference for procedure-related complications and 1.3–1.9% for false-positive findings at 80% power and two-sided alpha 0.05 [1].

Several analytical choices are well judged. The discrete-time survival models index time by screening examination number rather than calendar time, correctly reflecting the structure of screening-associated risk [1]. Time-varying covariates for comorbidity and functional status acknowledge that these characteristics change between screening rounds. Generalized propensity scores with multinomial logistic models are appropriate for the multiple comorbidity exposure categories [1], and the plan to weight prevalence estimates by inverse selection probabilities partially addresses selection into screening [1].

**Absence of an unscreened comparison group.** The central inferential limitation of the observational component is inherent to its design: the cohort contains only screened individuals. Aim 2 can estimate how screening-related harms vary across multimorbidity levels among those screened, but cannot estimate the counterfactual—what would happen to patients with different multimorbidity levels if they were not screened. The proposal appropriately shifts the causal question about net benefit to the microsimulation in Aim 3. The implication for rigor is that the observational aims cannot, on their own, determine whether screening causes net benefit or harm in any subgroup. The value of the cohort estimates rests on their quality as descriptive evidence and as inputs to the model, not on their capacity to support direct causal claims. This constraint is recognized, but it should be understood that the simulation’s validity depends on how well those inputs capture subgroup-specific parameters.

**Coarseness of the comorbidity exposure.** The primary comorbidity measure for Aim 2 uses a combined Charlson/Elixhauser score dichotomized as 0, 1, or at least 2 [1]. While standard in health services research, this is a relatively coarse summary that may obscure clinically meaningful distinctions—for example, between mild diabetes alone and severe heart failure alone, both scoring 1. The team’s own prior latent-class analysis demonstrated that comorbidity profiles beyond simple counts independently predict treatment selection and survival [5], yet the richer profiling appears reserved for descriptive Aim 1 rather than integrated into the outcome models of Aim 2. Using the more informative comorbidity profiles as the primary exposure—or at least as a planned secondary analysis—would increase the analytic yield from the methodological investment already made in that preliminary work.

**Underspecified handling of missing functional limitation data.** The FRI functional limitation assessment is available primarily at KPSC and VA (approximately 80% of the cohort) but is not available at MUSC [1]. The proposal describes general missing-data procedures—logistic models of missingness, inverse-probability weighting, and multiple imputation with ten datasets [1]—but does not address site-specific structural differences. Imputation across a site with integrated EHR data (KPSC), a site with claims-plus-EHR data (VA), and a safety-net site with EHR data only (MUSC) requires assumptions about whether the relationships between observed and missing FRI indicators are transportable across these different data environments. Without site-specific validation of the imputation models, functional limitation estimates for the MUSC subgroup—and any site-stratified analyses that depend on them—rest on untestable assumptions within the available data. This limitation affects the generalizability of functional-status findings and, if the missingness mechanism differs by site, could introduce bias that the generic imputation plan does not fully address.

**Rating: C (moderate). Confidence: B (high).**

## Feasibility

**A credible foundation with one critical dependency.** The five-year plan rests on existing data infrastructure, established multi-site collaborations, and a team with a demonstrated track record. The principal feasibility risk lies in the critical-path dependency between the observational arms (Aims 1–2) and the microsimulation refinement (Aim 3), which the current timeline does not fully resolve.

**Solid preliminary work reduces start-up risk.** The Watch the Spot (WTS) common data vocabulary already provides harmonized elements covering eligibility, demographics, smoking, nodules, utilization, comorbidity, and vital status across multiple institutions [1]. The NLP algorithm for identifying pulmonary nodules and extracting their characteristics has been validated with sensitivity and specificity above 95% [1], meaning core data-extraction tools are operational rather than speculative. The three participating sites each have established screening programs with identifiable screening order sets, EHR notification systems, or dedicated screening coordinators [1]; cohort identification does not depend on developing new clinical workflows. The retrospective data window (2016–2019) provides immediate access to several years of screening records without waiting for prospective accrual [1].

**Staggering is appropriate in principle.** The timeline allocates regulatory approvals to the first half of Year 1, cohort development to the second quarter, Aims 1–2 analyses to Years 1–3, and Aim 3 simulation work to Years 4–5 with manuscript preparation extending through Year 5 [1]. The data coordinating center at KPSC, led by Gould and Shen, provides centralized management for data capture, mapping, transfer, and analysis [1].

**The critical concern: overlap between simulation and cohort analysis.** The timeline shows simulation work beginning in the second half of Year 1 and continuing through Year 5, overlapping substantially with cohort assembly and Aim 1–2 analysis [1]. The cohort-derived estimates of screening sensitivity, specificity, complication rates, Lung-RADS adherence, and competing mortality that are intended to refine the UM-LCS model [1] will not be available in stable form until at least Year 3. Simulation work conducted in Years 1–3 must therefore rely on preliminary estimates or external inputs, which may require substantial revision when final cohort-derived parameters become available. The proposal does not specify which model inputs will use preliminary versus final cohort estimates, nor does it describe a structured iteration protocol between the cohort analysis team and the modeling team. This creates a genuine risk of rework or of the model being finalized with inputs that do not fully reflect the cohort findings.

**Secondary concern: missing data handling.** As discussed under Rigor, the FRI indicators are unavailable at MUSC, and the success of imputation depends on transportability assumptions across structurally different data sources [1]. Site-specific validation of imputation models is not described, and the feasibility of generating reliable FRI estimates for the MUSC subgroup remains uncertain within the current plan.

These dependencies do not render the project unworkable. The team’s prior success in combining EHR and claims data, the existing WTS infrastructure, and retrospective data availability provide a realistic path to completing Aims 1 and 2. The microsimulation model is already validated against NLST and PLCO outcomes [6] and can produce useful outputs even with literature-based inputs if cohort-derived estimates are delayed. However, the project’s distinctive contribution depends on integrating cohort-derived parameters into the model, and the current plan does not provide sufficient assurance that this integration can proceed efficiently within the stated timeline.

**Rating: B (good). Confidence: B (high).**

## Ethics and safety

**Low-risk observational study with appropriate protections.** The project involves no physical interventions, experimental treatments, or direct participant contact. It is a secondary analysis of existing clinical and administrative data combined with computer-based microsimulation modeling. The material risks are informational—centered on confidentiality of patient-level electronic health records, claims data, cancer registry entries, and mortality records—rather than physical or psychological harms.

**Proportionate data protection framework.** Each participating institution will maintain its own secure patient database. Names, medical record numbers, and dates of birth will be replaced by unique study identifiers, with the linkage key stored separately from analytic datasets. The data coordinating center at KPSC will use a common HIPAA-compliant structure for the centralized repository [1]. REDCap will support data transfer from MUSC and VA Portland to KPSC, with encrypted transmission and authenticated, authorized access [1]. These measures are well-established in multi-site observational research and provide reasonable protection against re-identification and unauthorized disclosure.

**Regulatory approvals and residual administrative details.** Regulatory approvals, including institutional review board authorization, will be sought at all study sites before data collection begins [1]. The timeline allocates the first half of Year 1 to IRB, subcontract, and regulatory approval processes [1]. The proposal does not discuss whether a waiver of informed consent will be sought or whether site-specific constraints on data sharing—particularly regarding VA data—might require additional agreements. These are administrative details that do not indicate an ethical deficiency but would need resolution during the approval phase.

No vulnerable populations are subjected to additional burdens beyond those inherent in secondary use of clinical data. The study does not alter the screening or treatment participants receive; it observes care that has already occurred or is occurring independently of the research. The inclusion of patient advocates for interpretation and dissemination [1] adds a dimension of community engagement, though the specific mechanism for this involvement is not detailed. The microsimulation modeling uses aggregate parameter estimates from the cohort and published sources (NLST, PLCO, SEER) [1] and does not involve identifiable individual data; no ethical issues arise from the modeling activities beyond transparent reporting of assumptions. The described protections are appropriate and proportionate to the informational risk profile.

**Rating: A (excellent). Confidence: A (very high).**

## Management

**Adequate expertise and coordination structure, with a gap at the observational-modeling interface.** The investigative team covers the disciplinary expertise required for each aim. Braithwaite contributes epidemiology and health services research; Gould and Silvestri bring thoracic oncology and pulmonary medicine; Meza and Jeon supply CISNET microsimulation capability; and Shen leads the statistical and data-coordinating functions at KPSC [1]. The prior collaborative track record—including the latent-class comorbidity analysis, the Watch the Spot data infrastructure, and the NLP nodule-identification algorithm [1]—demonstrates that core members have worked together productively on closely related problems. The inclusion of patient advocates for interpretation and dissemination [1] adds a translational dimension, though the proposal does not specify recruitment, terms of engagement, or timing of their input. This is a minor omission rather than a threat to the management plan.

**Data coordination builds on existing infrastructure.** The data coordinating center at KPSC, under Gould and Shen, provides a centralized hub for data capture, mapping, transfer, and analysis for Aims 1 and 2 [1]. Reuse of the Watch the Spot common data vocabulary reduces the coordination burden by building on an already-harmonized specification [1]. Transfer from MUSC and VA Portland via REDCap with encrypted transmission [1] is a standard workflow unlikely to introduce novel management challenges.

**Coordination mechanisms and the unresolved iteration gap.** Monthly team calls and annual in-person meetings [1] are conventional and appropriate for a five-year multi-site project. They provide a regular cadence for monitoring progress and maintaining alignment between the cohort analysis and simulation work. What is not described is how decisions will be made when the two streams need to iterate: which model inputs will be frozen with preliminary literature-based estimates while cohort analyses mature, and who holds authority to revise the simulation plan if cohort-derived parameters diverge from initial assumptions. This gap parallels the feasibility concern about timeline overlap. It does not suggest incapacity—the senior investigators are well positioned to establish such a protocol—but means the current plan does not provide assurance that this coordination risk has been anticipated. A structured checkpoint, such as a formal review of available cohort inputs at the Year 2 annual meeting before committing to final simulation scenarios, would materially strengthen the management plan without additional resources.

The proposal does not include individual effort allocations, an itemized budget, or a detailed staffing plan in the scientific narrative. This is typical for narrative sections of an R01 application, where such details are provided in separate administrative components, and the absence alone does not constitute evidence of insufficient resources. The management judgement is confined to coordination structures, expertise coverage, and scheduling logic described in the scientific text, all of which are sufficient to support delivery provided the iteration gap between the observational and modeling arms is addressed during project execution.

**Rating: B (good). Confidence: B (high).**

# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project aims to optimize EMPOWER, a web-based positive emotion regulation intervention, for young adult cancer survivors after active treatment. Using the Multiphase Optimization Strategy (MOST), a full factorial experiment will assign 352 survivors to 32 combinations of five skill components (e.g., mindfulness, gratitude, strengths) to determine which, individually or in combination, most efficiently reduce distress and improve psychological well-being. The most valuable expected knowledge is a set of data-driven, component-specific effect estimates that will enable the assembly of an efficient, scalable intervention package specifically tailored for this underserved population, ready for definitive evaluation in a future multisite trial.

The main strengths are the importance of the research question for a population with few tailored psychosocial resources, the appropriateness of the MOST framework for this optimization phase, and the efficient factorial design. The investigative team has strong and directly relevant expertise, a plan for credible recruitment, and a suite of validated, low-burden measurement tools. The decisive limitations are rooted in the design’s optimistic assumptions about the magnitude of component effects and participant retention. The expected effect-size criterion (d = 0.50 for retention) and planned detectable main effect (d ≈ 0.31) may exceed what is typical for brief, self-guided positive-psychology skills, and the pilot data suggest a more conservative retention rate, directly constraining the primary endpoint’s analytic power.

The project’s substantial value—addressing a genuine, unmet need with a transparent, data-driven optimization strategy—is weighed against a central risk: that the trial is designed to detect effects that may be larger than what the evidence suggests these components can produce. An inconclusive result, where few components are retained, would itself be informative, and the accumulated design and implementation experience would benefit the field. The revised retention criterion addresses a key threat to feasibility. The current plan offers a worthwhile, if uncertain, route to an optimized intervention package; its strengths justify proceeding, with the understanding that the trial will yield valuable knowledge even if its most optimistic component-selection goals are not fully met.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

**The scientific value is good.** The project addresses a significant and underserved psychosocial need—the persistent distress and impaired well-being experienced by young adult cancer survivors after active treatment. This population faces cancer during education, employment, relationship formation, and family planning, often with limited support, and carries many years of life ahead [1]. Few scalable, evidence-based supportive-care interventions target this group specifically, making the question both important and timely.

**Intended knowledge contribution.** The attainable contribution is twofold. First, the factorial optimization experiment will produce component-specific effect estimates for five emotion-regulation skills—noticing and capitalizing on positive events and gratitude, mindfulness, positive reappraisal, personal strengths and achievable goals, and acts of kindness—on four patient-centered primary outcomes: positive affect, life satisfaction, depression, and anxiety [1]. These estimates use validated PROMIS and NIH Toolbox computer-adaptive tests that reduce participant burden and support comparability across the heterogeneous survivor population [1]. Secondary outcomes extend the assessment to physical functioning, fatigue, social role satisfaction, and health behaviors [1]. Together, the results will enable assembly of an efficient, data-driven intervention package ready for a future confirmatory multisite trial [1]. This is precisely the knowledge the optimization phase of MOST is designed to deliver [1].

Second, the project will generate methodologically informative experience about the feasibility and yield of applying the MOST framework to emotion-regulation skills in cancer survivorship. The team’s prior multicomponent interventions (LEAF, LILAC, DAHLIA, IRISS) tested whole packages against controls [1], leaving component contributions unknown. The proposed systematic decomposition represents a practical advance in how supportive-care interventions are developed for this field.

**Theoretical grounding.** The approach combines reducing negative emotional states with increasing positive experiences, drawing on Broaden-and-Build Theory and Revised Stress and Coping Theory to hypothesize that positive emotions expand coping and social resources during stress [1]. Coping self-efficacy and social support are proposed mediating pathways [1], making the results potentially informative about mechanisms as well as effects.

**Calibration of attainable knowledge.** The value carries an important calibration: the knowledge gained depends on the trial’s ability to detect component effects at plausible magnitudes. As detailed in the rigor assessment below, the effect-size assumptions may be optimistic relative to prior work. The EMPOWER pilot produced uncontrolled pre-post effects exceeding d = 0.60 for positive affect and life satisfaction, but these overestimate the causal component effects the factorial design will isolate [2]. Meta-analytic estimates across youth psychological interventions place typical effects around g = 0.23 (95% CrI 0.16–0.30), with positive well-being outcomes around 0.28 [3]. If true component effects cluster in this range, the trial may retain few components at the planned d = 0.50 criterion [1].

An inconclusive optimization result would itself be scientifically informative—it would tell the field that brief, self-guided positive-psychology skills, as configured here, produce effects below a clinically meaningful threshold—but the stated goal of assembling a refined, multicomponent package would be partially met. Even with this uncertainty, the project offers genuine value: well-estimated component effects from a randomized factorial design, for a population that lacks tailored resources, with practical and conceptual implications for future supportive-care intervention development.

## Innovation

**The innovation is good.** The project makes a genuine methodological contribution by applying the Multiphase Optimization Strategy (MOST) to decompose a positive emotion regulation intervention for young adult cancer survivors, a population and setting where this systematic component-testing approach has not previously been used.

The innovation lies in the approach rather than in any single intervention component. The five skills—noticing and capitalizing on positive events and gratitude, mindfulness, positive reappraisal, personal strengths and achievable goals, and acts of kindness [1]—are individually established in the positive psychology literature and have been tested in prior multicomponent packages by this team. Table 1 of the proposal documents four prior interventions (IRISS, DAHLIA, LEAF, LILAC) that delivered similar skill bundles to HIV-positive individuals, diabetes patients, and cancer and dementia caregivers, with depression effect sizes ranging from d = −0.18 to −0.81 and positive-affect effects from d = 0.08 to 0.58 [1]. These studies evaluated whole packages against controls, leaving unanswered which components actually drove the observed benefits and which added only participant burden. The present project breaks from this package-level tradition.

**Two dimensions of advance.** First, substituting a 2⁵ full factorial design for a two-arm package-versus-control trial allows efficient simultaneous estimation of five main effects from 352 participants, with each component received by 176 and withheld from 176, balanced across the other four factors [1]. This yields component-specific evidence that directly informs assembly of a streamlined, data-driven package—precisely the optimization logic MOST was developed to support [1]. The design also permits exploration of component interactions, distinguishing this project from a series of separate two-arm experiments, even though interaction detection power is limited, as discussed under rigor.

Second, the systematic decomposition is implemented in an underserved population at a distinctive life stage. Young adult cancer survivors (diagnosed ages 15–39, currently 18–39, within five years of active treatment) experience distress during education, employment, relationship formation, and family planning, yet have few tailored, scalable psychosocial resources [1]. The existing multicomponent evidence summarized in the proposal’s Table 1 comes primarily from older adults, caregivers, and other disease contexts; none of the prior trials specifically targeted post-treatment young adult survivors. Deploying the MOST framework in this population generates both component-effect estimates and feasibility data—retention, engagement, and differential attrition patterns—specific to a group whose life circumstances differ materially from those of older cancer survivors or dementia caregivers.

The innovation does not extend to the individual components themselves, and this is appropriately acknowledged. The proposal does not claim to have invented gratitude journaling, mindfulness practice, or strengths identification. Its originality is in the systematic, transparent strategy for determining which of these established skills, in which combinations, produces measurable benefit for this specific population when delivered in a self-guided web format. That contribution—moving from “this package helps” to “these specific components matter and these do not”—represents a meaningful advance in intervention development methodology for psychosocial oncology.

## Rigor

**The rigor is moderate.** The MOST framework and factorial design are well-conceived for the optimization question, and the measurement approach is strong. However, the design’s capacity to deliver its stated aims is materially constrained by optimistic effect-size assumptions, a retention target that exceeds pilot evidence, and limited power for the interactions that justify the factorial structure. These issues do not make the trial unsound, but they narrow the attainable knowledge and warrant realistic expectations about what the optimization can resolve.

**Design quality.** The factorial experiment is methodologically appropriate for identifying useful components. Assigning 352 participants to the 32 combinations of five binary factors, with each component received by 176 and withheld from 176, balanced across the other factors, efficiently estimates five main effects simultaneously [1]. The analytic models—baseline-adjusted ANCOVA and longitudinal mixed-effects models with autoregressive covariance structures—are sound choices for estimating component-specific effects [1]. The use of alpha = 0.10 for component retention is defensible in an optimization context where retained components face subsequent confirmatory testing, reducing the cost of false negatives [1].

The measurement battery is a clear strength. The four primary outcomes—positive affect, life satisfaction, depression, and anxiety—are assessed with validated PROMIS and NIH Toolbox computer-adaptive tests that reduce participant burden (typically 15–20 minutes per administration) and produce scores comparable across the heterogeneous survivor population [1]. Secondary outcomes extend to physical functioning, fatigue, social role satisfaction, and health behaviors, and the protocol includes weekly assessments during the intervention to help distinguish component-related changes [1]. The proposed mediators—coping self-efficacy and social support—are also measured with established PROMIS instruments and are theoretically motivated [1].

**Calibration of effect-size expectations.** The decisive limitation concerns the calibration of effect-size expectations against the evidence base. The proposal uses d = 0.50 as the criterion for retaining components and reports that, with 256 participants retained at one month, the detectable main effect is d = 0.31 (95% interval 0.06–0.56) at 80% power [1]. Three lines of evidence suggest these benchmarks may be optimistic.

First, the team’s own EMPOWER pilot (single-arm, N = 33 starting the intervention) produced uncontrolled pre-post effects of d = 0.60 for positive affect and 0.62 for life satisfaction at 12 weeks, but these overestimate the causal component effects the factorial design will isolate [2]. Second, the LEAF randomized trial in dementia caregivers—testing a related facilitated positive-emotion intervention against an emotion-reporting control—yielded between-group effects of d = −0.25 for depression and 0.58 for positive affect [4]. These are whole-package effects that include facilitation; the present EMPOWER components are briefer, self-guided, and tested individually against inert-substitution controls. Third, a Bayesian umbrella review across approximately 1,150 primary studies of youth psychological and digital interventions estimated a posterior mean effect of g = 0.229 (95% CrI 0.157–0.301), with effects somewhat larger for positive well-being (0.28) than symptom reduction (0.19) [3].

If true component effects cluster around 0.20–0.30, the trial may retain few components at the d = 0.50 criterion and provide limited guidance for assembling an optimized package. The component-selection rule and the power calculation are thus vulnerable to an optimistic premise. The study will still produce well-estimated component effects from a randomized design, which is valuable knowledge even if the retention threshold is not met for most skills, but the stated goal of identifying a set of clearly useful components depends on effect sizes the available evidence does not strongly support.

**Retention assumptions.** The 72% retention assumption for the primary endpoint power calculation exceeds the team’s own pilot evidence. The EMPOWER pilot reported 61.4% retention at both 8 and 12 weeks [2]. The FoRtitude MOST trial in breast cancer survivors showed that telecoaching improved retention, but that trial used facilitated delivery [5]; EMPOWER is self-guided with inert-substitution content as the primary engagement mechanism. If retention falls to the pilot-observed 61%, the analytic sample at one month drops to approximately 215, and the detectable main effect shifts upward accordingly.

The proposal also documents that pilot dropouts had poorer baseline mental health, more depressive symptoms, and lower positive affect and life satisfaction [2]. Differential attrition by baseline distress could bias component-effect estimates even if overall retention approaches targets. The decision condition appropriately requires revising the retention assumption to 61.4% and adjusting the recruitment plan to ensure the target analytic sample can be reached for the one-month endpoint.

**Inert-control uncertainty.** The inert-control strategy introduces additional uncertainty. Absent components are replaced with lessons and practice intended to be inert—factual journaling and reading about nutrition or stress responses—and the protocol acknowledges that if this content itself improves well-being, estimated EMPOWER effects would be attenuated [1]. The FoRtitude trial offers a cautionary parallel: CBT skills and attention-control health management content produced comparable reductions in fear of recurrence, suggesting that nonspecific engagement or content effects can rival those of targeted skills [5]. The present design includes a pure control condition (Condition 1: all five components absent) receiving only inert content [1], which provides a partial reference, but the main-effect contrasts pool across all other component assignments where inert content substitutes for absent skills, so any improvement attributable to inert content reduces apparent component effects across all comparisons.

**Limited interaction power.** The design is explicitly underpowered for interactions, with a detectable interaction effect of d = 0.62 (95% interval 0.37–0.87) at 80% power [1]. The proposal acknowledges the study “may miss smaller interactions.” Since identifying synergistic or antagonistic combinations is a key rationale for the factorial structure over separate experiments, this limits the design’s ability to fully inform package assembly. The optimized package will rest primarily on main-effect evidence, which is appropriate for an optimization trial but represents a narrower knowledge contribution than a fully powered factorial experiment would provide.

**Exploratory analyses.** The mediation and moderation analyses (Aim 2) are appropriately framed as exploratory, limited to components judged effective in Aim 1 [1]. These analyses use the randomized factorial structure to estimate indirect effects and moderated effects by age and gender, generating hypotheses rather than establishing mechanisms—appropriate for the optimization stage, though power for detecting moderated effects within the factorial structure is not explicitly quantified.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility is good.** The two-site recruitment plan at established NCI-designated comprehensive cancer centers, supported by pilot data and a community partnership contingency, provides a credible path to enrolling the target 352 participants within the project period. The web-based delivery platform is already operational, and the four-year timeline accommodates the necessary overlapping phases of startup, recruitment, follow-up, and analysis.

**Recruitment infrastructure.** The Wake Forest Baptist Comprehensive Cancer Center and Northwestern’s Robert H. Lurie Comprehensive Cancer Center will identify candidates through tumor registries and EHR review, with provider permission preceding contact [1]. Recruitment uses a multi-channel strategy—letters, follow-up calls, local organizations, flyers, and electronic and community postings—that the pilot demonstrated can reach the target population. Pilot experience documented 20% participation among those contacted, with an overall accrual of 1.6 participants per month (95% CI 1.2–2.1) across 27.7 months [2]. The second pilot wave, which added community and online recruitment sources alongside clinic-based identification, improved accrual relative to the first wave [1]. The current plan estimates approximately 2,850 potentially eligible survivors over three years, and a conservative 13% recruitment assumption supports the 352 target; the analytic section also reports an anticipated 16.5% eligible-and-interested proportion from pilot experience [1].

**Retention as the key operational risk.** The chief feasibility concern is retention, which directly affects the analytic sample available for the primary endpoint. The proposal’s power calculation assumes 72% retention at one month [1], yet the EMPOWER pilot achieved 61.4% retention at both 8 and 12 weeks [2]. The decision condition requires revising the retention assumption to 61.4% and adjusting the recruitment plan to ensure the target analytic sample can be reached for the one-month endpoint. This is a manageable operational adjustment: given the 36-month recruitment window and 1.6 participants-per-month baseline accrual, over-enrollment to protect the analytic sample is achievable, particularly with the community partnership with Cancer Services providing a contingency route if site-based accrual lags [1].

**Delivery platform and assessment burden.** The web-based delivery infrastructure is a feasibility strength. The Wake Forest Research Information Systems Unit hosts the intervention [1], and the platform’s core functionality—scheduled release of lesson content, diary-style exercises with prior-response display, and restriction on skipping ahead—was demonstrated in the pilot [1]. The eight-week window for completing the five-week program accommodates interruptions common in this population. The REDCap-based screening, consent, and assessment workflow [1] is established at both sites and supports the multi-assessment schedule: baseline, weekly during intervention, and follow-ups at one week, one month, three months, and six months post-intervention [1]. PROMIS and NIH Toolbox computer-adaptive tests keep assessment burden to 15–20 minutes, which supports retention across repeated measurements [1].

**Timeline.** The four-year schedule places study startup in the first two quarters, optimization from later Year 1 into Year 4, data analysis from Year 3 into Year 4, and dissemination and follow-on grant preparation in the final period [1]. This timeline accommodates the three-year recruitment window with margin for the proposed retention adjustments. The recruitment and retention challenge intersects with the design’s effect-size assumptions, as discussed under rigor: if retention falls below the revised 61.4% target and component effects are smaller than anticipated, the trial may be underpowered to identify useful components. The conditions in the decision—over-enrolling to protect the one-month analytic sample and reframing the primary goal as estimating component effects with precision sufficient to rule out clinically meaningful benefit—are operationally realistic within the planned timeline and resources.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety are excellent.** The proposal describes proportionate, well-justified protections for a low-risk supportive-care intervention delivered remotely to a population that may face physical limitations and competing demands. The core ethical arrangements—informed consent, data security, appropriate exclusion criteria, and a self-guided format that preserves participants’ ongoing clinical care—are clearly specified and appropriate to the research activities.

**Consent and data protection.** The informed consent procedure is integrated into the recruitment and REDCap workflow: interested survivors complete an eligibility screener, then provide consent before the baseline questionnaire [1]. This sequenced approach ensures that potential participants understand the study before committing to assessment, without requiring in-person visits that would burden a geographically dispersed population. The proposal does not describe a detailed consent document or ethics committee approval status, but these are administrative prerequisites for funded research rather than scientific defects in the plan; the described flow is consistent with standard practice for web-based behavioral trials.

Data security relies on established institutional infrastructure. Unique study IDs and PINs control questionnaire access, and REDCap supports secure survey and database collection [1]. The Wake Forest Research Information Systems Unit hosts the intervention [1]. Both platforms are widely used in NIH-funded clinical research and incorporate encryption, access controls, and audit trails appropriate to the sensitivity of the collected data—demographic and clinical characteristics, psychological symptom measures, and health behavior reports.

**Exclusion criteria and risk.** The exclusion criteria are thoughtfully calibrated to the intervention’s risk profile and supportive-care purpose. The protocol excludes individuals with significant psychiatric history and those in palliative or hospice care [1]. These exclusions recognize that a brief, self-guided positive-emotion skills intervention is not designed to address severe psychopathology or end-of-life distress and that including such participants could expose them to an intervention mismatched to their needs. The proposal also excludes survivors with recurrence or multiple primary cancers [1], maintaining a focus on the post-treatment survivorship period where the intervention’s supportive-care rationale is strongest.

The intervention itself carries low risk. The five scheduled weeks of instructional material and daily home practice (approximately 20–30 minutes) teach skills such as gratitude journaling, mindfulness, reappraisal, goal-setting, and acts of kindness [1]. These are standard positive-psychology exercises with no documented harms in comparable populations. The control condition, which substitutes factual journaling and reading about nutrition or stress responses for absent components [1], is similarly benign and provides an attention-matched experience that maintains engagement without delivering potentially active treatment.

**Remote delivery advantages.** The web-based, self-guided delivery format carries practical ethical advantages. Participants can access the intervention from home on common devices, avoiding travel to study sites—meaningful for young adult survivors who may have physical limitations, employment obligations, or childcare responsibilities [1]. The eight-week window for completing the five-week program accommodates interruptions from treatment side effects or life demands [1]. The $20 per-assessment compensation, up to $100 total [1], provides modest recognition of participant time without constituting an undue inducement for this population.

**Minor gap in distress monitoring.** The proposal does not explicitly address distress monitoring or clinical referral pathways for participants who report elevated depression or anxiety during the trial. The self-guided format means there is no facilitator to observe participant distress in real time. The validated PROMIS depression and anxiety measures are administered at multiple timepoints [1], providing structured data that could trigger follow-up, but the protocol does not describe whether or how flagged scores would be addressed. This does not undermine the overall ethical judgement—the intervention is low-risk, the population is post-treatment rather than in acute crisis, and standard supportive-care trials routinely manage this through protocol-specified safety procedures that can be detailed at the institutional review board stage—but it represents an operational detail worth confirming before launch.

## Management

**Management is well-conceived.** The investigative team covers the disciplinary domains the project requires, the division of responsibilities is clearly specified, the schedule is realistic, and no critical gaps in expertise, resources, or coordination threaten delivery of the stated aims.

**Team expertise.** The PI team combines directly relevant expertise. John Salsman contributes young-adult oncology, survivorship research, and outcome-measurement experience; Judith Moskowitz contributes the positive-emotion intervention framework and its adaptation across disease contexts; and Lynne Wagner brings MOST trial design and execution expertise, including from the FoRtitude factorial trial in breast cancer survivors [1]. Additional collaborators cover psychosocial oncology, medical oncology, quantitative methods, survivorship care, and eHealth delivery [1]. This configuration matches the project’s needs: clinical access to the young-adult survivor population, mastery of the intervention content and its theoretical basis, methodological command of the factorial optimization design, and the statistical and informatics support to implement randomization, host the web platform, and execute the planned analyses.

**Division of responsibilities.** The responsibilities are appropriately distributed. The study statistician administers block randomization with block size 64 after baseline assessment [1]. The Wake Forest Research Information Systems Unit hosts the intervention platform [1]. Site PIs at the two comprehensive cancer centers lead participant identification through tumor registries and EHR review, provider permission, and multi-channel outreach [1]. REDCap manages screening, consent, and survey data collection [1]. This division maps specific operational tasks to the units with the relevant infrastructure and experience, reducing coordination risk.

**Timeline and deliverables.** The four-year schedule is detailed and plausible [1]. Study startup occupies the first two quarters. Aim 1 optimization runs from the second quarter of Year 1 through the second quarter of Year 4, providing approximately three years of active recruitment. Data analysis for Aims 1 and 2 begins in Year 3 and continues through Year 4. Abstracts and manuscripts are planned from the third quarter of Year 3 through the end of the project, and preparation of the follow-up confirmatory R01 occupies the final three quarters of Year 4 [1]. This schedule allows analysis to inform dissemination while follow-up data collection concludes, and reserves dedicated time for the next grant submission—a realistic acknowledgment that the optimization phase is designed to feed directly into a subsequent multisite trial.

The expected products are clearly specified and matched to the project’s stage: component-specific effect estimates, exploratory mediator and moderator findings, and an assembled intervention package suitable for confirmatory evaluation [1]. These deliverables do not require the trial to demonstrate package effectiveness—appropriate for an optimization-phase project [1].

**Confidence and operational adjustments.** The management assessment carries high confidence. The team, institutions, platforms, and schedule are documented with sufficient specificity, and the pilot experience demonstrates that the two sites can execute the recruitment and data-collection workflows [1]. The conditions attached to the overall recommendation—revising the retention assumption to 61.4%, adjusting recruitment to protect the one-month analytic sample, and explicitly planning for reporting null or inconclusive findings—are operational adjustments within the existing management structure and timeline, not redesigns that would require new expertise or resources.

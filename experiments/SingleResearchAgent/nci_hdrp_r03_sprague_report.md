# Project review

## Overall assessment

This R03 project, led by Brian L. Sprague, proposes a multicenter observational evaluation of digital breast tomosynthesis with synthesized two-dimensional mammography (s2D/DBT) compared with conventional DM/DBT for breast cancer screening. The planned work exploits a natural experiment created when seven radiology facilities within the PROSPR consortium uniformly transitioned from DM/DBT to s2D/DBT, generating a dataset of approximately 185,000 screening examinations (2011–2017) interpreted by 22 radiologists [1]. The three specific aims will compare overall screening performance including sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rates (Aim 1); screen-detected invasive cancer, DCIS, and benign lesion subtypes (Aim 2); and radiologists’ BI-RADS breast-density classifications (Aim 3) [1]. The most valuable expected contribution is population-based performance estimates for s2D/DBT—particularly sensitivity and false-negative rates derived from 12-month cancer-registry follow-up—that were unavailable from the existing small single-institution series and reader studies [1]. The study thereby provides evidence directly relevant to patients, providers, payers, and screening policy decisions at a time when many practices were already adopting this dose-reducing technology.

**The proposal is supported by a strong infrastructure and a clinically relevant question.** Outcome ascertainment is a clear strength: linkage to pathology reports and state cancer registries provides validated cancer diagnoses within 12 months, enabling the calculation of sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rates that prior reader studies could not address [1]. The multicenter design, drawing on established PROSPR registries with common data elements, includes both academic and community facilities and demographically distinct populations—rural, predominantly white Vermont and urban, racially diverse Pennsylvania—which supports broader generalizability [1]. The statistical approach using generalized estimating equations with the Miglioretti–Heagerty method is appropriate for the clustered data, and the project is feasible as a secondary analysis of existing registries with ethical arrangements already in place [1]. **The principal limitation is the before/after observational design without a contemporaneous control group.** Although the proposal uses common facilities, common radiologists, uniform adoption, and covariate adjustment to mitigate confounding, temporal trends in screening populations, radiologist experience, or secular changes in breast cancer incidence cannot be fully separated from the effect of the modality change [1]. The proposal acknowledges this and presents the analysis as a real-world performance characterization, but the evidence for causal attribution remains weaker than a randomized or contemporaneous comparison would provide. A second limitation is statistical power: with a projected 1,114 cancers, the study has 80% power only to detect a 5.6 percentage-point absolute difference in sensitivity, a wide margin that means a clinically meaningful degradation of 2–3 percentage points could not be reliably excluded [1]. The benign-lesion subclassification and ER/PR/HER2 subtype analyses are explicitly exploratory and appropriately labeled as such [1]. The single-vendor (Hologic) restriction limits the generalizability of findings to other manufacturers’ s2D algorithms [1].

**Overall, this is a well-conceived and carefully planned observational study that addresses a timely clinical question at a meaningful scale.** The robust outcome ascertainment—linkage to pathology and cancer registries providing validated 12-month cancer diagnoses—is the proposal’s strongest feature, enabling measures of sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rates that were unavailable from prior reader studies and small clinical series. The multicenter setting across academic and community facilities in two demographically distinct regions supports practical generalizability, and the inclusion of density classification as a distinct aim adds clinical relevance beyond simple performance benchmarking. These strengths are weighed against two material limitations: the before/after design without a concurrent control group cannot fully resolve temporal confounding, so the study is properly interpreted as real-world performance characterization rather than a definitive causal comparison; and the sensitivity comparison is underpowered for clinically relevant differences, meaning a modest but important degradation in sensitivity cannot be reliably excluded. The proposal’s transparent acknowledgment of these constraints, combined with its appropriate statistical methods and the genuine evidence gap it fills, supports the judgment that the current plan delivers worthwhile contributions that justify proceeding, while recognizing that the resulting evidence will be observational and require cautious causal interpretation.

## Scientific value

**The project addresses a clinically important and timely question at a scale that is directly relevant to implementation decisions.** At the time of the proposal, digital breast tomosynthesis was increasingly adopted for screening, yet the standard approach combined DBT with a conventional full-dose digital mammogram, approximately doubling radiation exposure relative to DM alone [1]. Synthesized 2D images reconstructed from the DBT acquisition offered a clear dose-reduction rationale—estimated at roughly 45% lower dose than combined DM/DBT—but the clinical evidence to support replacing conventional DM with s2D was limited to small single-institution series, reader studies that could not ascertain population-based cancer outcomes, and studies that included symptomatic patients or selected cancer cases, complicating inference about screening performance [1]. The absence of estimates for sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rates with 12-month cancer-registry follow-up represented a genuine evidence gap that directly constrained clinical, payer, and policy decisions at a moment when many practices were already moving toward s2D/DBT.

**The attainable knowledge from the three specific aims is both extensive and practically grounded.** Aim 1 will produce population-based measures of recall, cancer detection, sensitivity, specificity, false-negative rates, and positive predictive value for s2D/DBT compared with DM/DBT, using the ACR BI-RADS fifth-edition performance definitions and validated cancer diagnoses within 12 months of screening [1]. These are the performance metrics that screening programs, guideline panels, and payers require, and they were unavailable from the existing reader studies and small clinical series that could not ascertain cancers arising after a negative screen. Aim 2 will extend the comparison to lesion-specific outcomes—invasive cancer, DCIS, and benign lesion subtypes—addressing the clinically consequential question of whether s2D alters the balance between detecting potentially overdiagnosed DCIS and detecting invasive cancers [1]. The inclusion of benign lesion subclassification (non-proliferative, proliferative without atypia, atypical) adds a dimension relevant to understanding biopsy-related harms [1]. Aim 3 addresses an underappreciated but practically important question: whether the altered image appearance of s2D shifts radiologists’ subjective BI-RADS density classifications, with downstream consequences for density notification and recommendations for supplemental imaging [1].

**The multicenter design substantially strengthens the practical value of the results.** The seven participating facilities span academic and community settings across Vermont’s rural, predominantly white population and Pennsylvania’s urban, racially diverse population, with nearly half of screened patients at the Pennsylvania site being African-American [1]. This demographic and practice-setting variation supports generalizability beyond the single-institution or single-population studies that comprised the existing literature. The before/after comparison within the same facilities and radiologists, rather than across unrelated sites with different technologies, provides a more internally coherent evidence base than cross-sectional comparisons [1].

**The principal constraint on scientific value is the observational before/after design, which limits causal attribution.** The absence of a concurrent control group means that temporal trends in screening populations, radiologist experience, referral patterns, or secular changes in breast cancer incidence cannot be fully separated from the effect of the modality change [1]. The proposal’s protections—common facilities, common radiologists, uniform adoption, and covariate adjustment—mitigate but do not eliminate this threat. The study is therefore properly understood as providing real-world performance characterization rather than a definitive causal comparison, a reasonable contribution at this stage of evidence development when randomization is impractical once facilities have adopted the new technology [1].

A secondary constraint is the limited power for sensitivity comparisons: with a projected 1,114 cancers, the study achieves 80% power only for a 5.6 percentage-point absolute difference in sensitivity, meaning a clinically meaningful degradation of 2–3 percentage points cannot be reliably excluded [1]. This does not negate the value of the performance characterization, but it limits the strength of the non-inferiority inference for the most important cancer-detection endpoint. Despite these constraints, the project fills a genuine evidence gap with population-based estimates—including sensitivity and false-negative rates derived from 12-month cancer-registry follow-up—that were unavailable from existing studies, and the scale, multicenter setting, and inclusion of lesion-specific and density outcomes give the findings direct relevance to the clinical and policy decisions that practices were then facing.

## Innovation

**The primary innovation of this project lies not in a new technology or novel study design, but in its capacity to generate a more rigorous and clinically complete evidence profile for an implementation question that was, at the time, being answered with limited data.** The proposal moves beyond the reader studies and small single-institution clinical series that dominated the s2D/DBT literature, producing population-based estimates of key performance metrics—sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rates—that require validated cancer outcomes from at least 12 months of follow-up and were therefore unavailable from prior work [1]. This is an incremental but genuine advance: it does not introduce a new screening modality or a novel analytic method, but it applies established epidemiologic approaches from the PROSPR consortium to fill a specific, decision-relevant evidence gap at a meaningful scale.

**The proposal’s contribution can be located by examining what the existing literature at the time could and could not deliver.** Reader studies, such as the TOMMY trial, provided valuable information on cancer detection and recall under controlled interpretation conditions but could not ascertain cancers arising after a negative screen and therefore could not estimate sensitivity or the false-negative rate in a screening population [1]. Small single-institution clinical series, including the preliminary Pennsylvania data that motivated this proposal (5,366 s2D/DBT and 15,571 DM/DBT examinations), could report recall, cancer detection, and PPV1 but lacked the sample size for precise lesion-specific comparisons and could not address generalizability across diverse practice settings [1]. The proposed study assembles approximately 185,000 examinations from seven facilities, interpreted by 22 radiologists in both academic and community settings, with systematic linkage to pathology registries and state cancer registries [1]. The result is a dataset that can, for the first time, produce multicenter, population-based estimates of sensitivity and specificity for s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT using standard screening-performance definitions applied to a screening cohort.

**Aim 2 adds a further dimension by moving from aggregate cancer detection to lesion-specific outcomes, particularly the distinction between invasive cancer and DCIS.** The proposal explicitly engages with the hypothesis that s2D may alter the balance of detected lesion types—potentially lowering DCIS detection while maintaining invasive cancer detection—a question with direct implications for the benefit–harm trade-off in screening [1]. While the ER/PR/HER2 subtype and benign-lesion subclassification analyses are explicitly exploratory, the core comparison of invasive versus in situ detection by modality, grounded in pathology and registry data, extends beyond what prior s2D studies could address with adequate precision [1]. The detectable differences stated for Aim 2 (0.8 per 1,000 for invasive cancer, 0.5 per 1,000 for DCIS) are calibrated to baseline rates from the pooled PROSPR consortium data and provide sufficient resolution for clinically meaningful lesion-type comparisons, even if the exploratory subclassification analyses will be underpowered [1].

**Aim 3—the systematic evaluation of s2D’s effect on BI-RADS density classifications—introduces a question that was largely neglected in the existing s2D literature.** The proposal recognizes that the different image appearance of synthesized 2D views could alter radiologists’ subjective density assessments, independent of any biological change in breast composition, with potential consequences for density-notification laws and supplemental screening recommendations [1]. This is not a performance endpoint in the traditional sense, but it addresses a practical implementation concern that is well motivated by the known variability in density assessment documented within the PROSPR consortium itself [1]. The within-facility, within-radiologist before/after comparison provides a more controlled evaluation of this question than cross-sectional density-distribution comparisons across unrelated sites.

The innovation is thus best characterized as a substantial strengthening of the evidence base for a specific, timely implementation question, rather than a conceptual or methodological breakthrough. The proposal’s use of a before/after natural experiment is a pragmatic response to the reality that facilities had already adopted s2D/DBT, making randomization impracticable, and the analytic methods—generalized estimating equations with non-nested clustering, covariate adjustment, and registry-based outcome ascertainment—are well-established within the investigative team and the PROSPR consortium [1]. What is new is the combination of these methods with a multicenter dataset of sufficient size and demographic diversity to produce performance estimates, including sensitivity, that were unavailable and directly needed for clinical and policy decisions. The contribution is incremental but important, and it is appropriately scoped for an R03 mechanism.

## Rigor

**The study design is a pragmatic and appropriate response to the research question, with outcome ascertainment as its strongest feature, but the before/after observational structure imposes fundamental limitations on causal inference, and the sensitivity comparison is underpowered for clinically meaningful differences.**

### Design strengths

The multicenter before/after design exploits a genuine implementation change: seven radiology facilities uniformly transitioned from DM/DBT to s2D/DBT after at least one year of DM/DBT screening, creating a comparison within the same facilities and the same 22 interpreting radiologists [1]. This is a material design strength. By holding facilities and radiologists constant, the study avoids confounding from between-site differences in patient populations, referral patterns, equipment calibration, and radiologist thresholds that would complicate a cross-sectional comparison of unrelated sites using different modalities. The uniform facility-level adoption—rather than patient-level selection of s2D versus DM/DBT—further reduces the risk of indication bias in which women with specific risk profiles are differentially assigned to one modality [1].

The restriction to a single manufacturer (Hologic Selenia Dimensions equipment with C-View reconstruction) eliminates variation in s2D algorithms and acquisition parameters, which is appropriate for an initial evaluation although it limits generalizability to other vendors’ platforms [1]. The exclusion of transitional examinations that combined conventional DM, DBT, and s2D, and of women with a personal history of breast cancer, defines clean comparator cohorts that align with the intended screening population [1].

### Outcome ascertainment: the strongest element

Linkage to pathology reports and state cancer registries provides validated cancer diagnoses within 12 months of the screening examination, including cancers diagnosed outside the screening facility [1]. This is essential for the central aim of estimating sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rates—measures that cannot be derived from reader studies or clinical series lacking follow-up of negative screens. The use of the ACR BI-RADS fifth-edition performance definitions, with a positive screen defined as assessment 0, 3, 4, or 5 and cancer outcomes as diagnoses within 12 months, aligns the study with standard screening-performance benchmarks [1]. The collection of detailed patient covariates—age, race/ethnicity, breast density, family history, screening interval, and body mass index—from patient questionnaires, radiology systems, and electronic health records supports the planned multivariable adjustment [1].

### Statistical approach

Generalized estimating equations with the Miglioretti–Heagerty method for non-nested clustering within women, radiologists, and facilities appropriately handle correlated observations without requiring a hierarchical nesting structure [1]. The plan to examine interactions between patient characteristics and modality is a sensible exploratory extension, though the power for such interactions is likely limited. The power calculations are transparent and based on empirical performance estimates from the pooled PROSPR consortium data [1]. For the primary performance measures, the study is well powered: 80% power to detect absolute differences of 0.4 percentage points in recall and 0.8 per 1,000 in cancer detection, both of which represent clinically meaningful thresholds [1].

### Two principal limitations

**First, the before/after design without a contemporaneous control group cannot fully separate the effect of the modality change from temporal trends.** Secular changes in the screened population (e.g., shifts in age distribution, screening participation, or risk profiles), radiologist experience and learning curves, referral thresholds for biopsy, and background changes in breast cancer incidence all operate on the same temporal axis as the modality transition [1]. The planned covariate adjustment—for age, race/ethnicity, breast density, family history, and screening interval—can address measured population differences between periods, but unmeasured confounders remain a threat [1]. The proposal’s reliance on common facilities and radiologists mitigates some of these concerns—each radiologist serves as their own control—but the earlier multicenter DM/DBT versus DM analysis within the same consortium demonstrated that adjusted and unadjusted estimates can differ meaningfully after accounting for temporal population shifts [1]. This reinforces that the study is best interpreted as real-world performance characterization rather than a definitive causal estimate of the modality effect.

**Second, the sensitivity comparison is underpowered for clinically relevant differences.** With a projected 1,114 cancers across both periods, the study achieves 80% power only for a 5.6 percentage-point absolute difference in sensitivity [1]. Given that the baseline DM/DBT sensitivity is estimated at 90.9%, a detectable difference of this magnitude would mean sensitivity falling to approximately 85%—a dramatic degradation far larger than what would be considered clinically acceptable [1]. A more plausible concern, such as a 2–3 percentage-point decrement, cannot be reliably excluded. This limitation is intrinsic to the rarity of the outcome: sensitivity depends on cancers diagnosed after a negative screen, which are uncommon events. The proposal does not overstate the sensitivity comparison; it presents the power calculation transparently and focuses the primary hypothesis on recall and specificity [1]. The value of the sensitivity estimate will lie in its generation of a population-based point estimate with an appropriately wide confidence interval, providing a benchmark for future studies and meta-analyses even if the standalone comparison is imprecise.

### Density assessment

For Aim 3, the detectable difference of 0.6 percentage points in dense classifications is very small relative to the known inter-radiologist variability in BI-RADS density assignment—the Sprague et al. 2016 PROSPR study documented that 17% of women had discordant dense/non-dense classifications between consecutive mammograms interpreted by different radiologists [1]. The within-radiologist before/after comparison partially addresses this, because each radiologist serves as their own control, but year-to-year drift in individual radiologists’ density-calling thresholds could still introduce noise that exceeds the detectable difference. The density comparison is appropriately framed as a descriptive characterization rather than a hypothesis test with prespecified clinical significance thresholds.

Overall, the design, measurement, and analytic plans are well matched to the study’s goals as a real-world performance characterization, with the before/after structure and limited sensitivity power representing inherent constraints that are honestly disclosed and appropriately tempered in their inferential claims.

## Feasibility

**The project is highly feasible as a secondary analysis of existing, prospectively collected data within an established research infrastructure, with no critical dependencies that would prevent completion of the stated aims as designed.**

The feasibility of this study rests on three pillars: the data already exist, the infrastructure to access and link them is mature, and the investigative team has direct experience with both. All three conditions are met. The proposed dataset—approximately 185,000 screening examinations (98,850 DM/DBT and 86,850 s2D/DBT) performed during 2011–2017 and interpreted by 22 radiologists—is already captured within the University of Vermont and University of Pennsylvania PROSPR Research Centers [1]. The proposal does not require acquiring new mammograms for research, recruiting participants, or conducting prospective follow-up. Instead, it assembles eligible exam-level records from existing registries, pools common data elements, establishes twelve-month outcomes, and performs the three coordinated analyses [1]. This is a well-defined data-management and analytic workflow with no novel technology development, no equipment procurement, and no site initiation.

The PROSPR infrastructure provides the critical enablers. The Vermont and Pennsylvania centers have collected standardized breast screening and outcomes information since 2011, with common data elements and linkage infrastructure already established for pooled analysis [1]. The data sources—patient questionnaires, radiology systems, electronic health records, pathology reports, and state cancer registries—are described with sufficient specificity to confirm that the required variables for the three aims (demographics, BI-RADS assessments, density classifications, biopsy results, cancer diagnoses, and tumor characteristics) are available [1]. The Pennsylvania exam-level dataset will be securely transferred to Vermont for pooling, a data-sharing arrangement that mirrors prior multicenter PROSPR collaborations [1]. The ethical approvals are in place: Vermont’s passive consent with opt-out achieves approximately 95% participation, and Pennsylvania secured a waiver of informed consent [1]. No new ethical review or consent process is required.

The team’s prior work within this infrastructure provides direct evidence of feasibility. The preliminary Pennsylvania single-site analysis of 5,366 s2D/DBT and 15,571 DM/DBT screens—which reported recall, cancer detection, DCIS detection, and density classification comparisons—demonstrates that the data extraction, cohort definition, and analytic pipeline are operational [1]. The pooled Vermont, Pennsylvania, and Dartmouth DM/DBT performance estimates that inform the power calculations further confirm that multicenter data assembly and standardized outcome definitions are established [1]. The statistical methods—generalized estimating equations with the Miglioretti–Heagerty approach—are those the team has applied in prior PROSPR analyses, reducing methodological risk [1].

The critical dependencies are few and well managed. The study depends on the continued availability of the registry data and the cancer-registry linkages, both of which are maintained as part of the ongoing PROSPR center operations, not created de novo for this project. The pooled analysis depends on the secure data transfer from Pennsylvania to Vermont, a described step that reflects the established collaboration rather than a new negotiation. The team’s composition—integrating radiology, pathology, epidemiology, and multicenter PROSPR collaboration experience—matches the required expertise [1]. The single-vendor restriction to Hologic Selenia Dimensions equipment with C-View reconstruction simplifies the analytic task by holding the s2D algorithm constant; it does not affect whether the proposed study can be completed as planned [1].

In summary, the data are in hand, the infrastructure is proven, the methods are established, and the team has demonstrated the capacity to execute analyses of this type and scale. There is no identifiable obstacle that would prevent completion of the three aims as designed.

## Ethics and safety

**The project is a secondary analysis of existing clinical data with no new research procedures, no additional radiation exposure, and no direct patient contact; the ethical arrangements are proportionate, and the residual data-privacy risks are managed within an established research infrastructure.** The study does not introduce material safety concerns for participants, communities, or the environment.

The study uses screening examinations already obtained in routine clinical care and does not acquire new mammograms for research purposes [1]. There is no additional radiation exposure, no investigational imaging protocol, and no alteration of clinical management. The work consists entirely of assembling existing exam-level records from the Vermont and Pennsylvania PROSPR registries, linking them to pathology reports and state cancer registries, and performing pooled statistical analyses [1]. This places it squarely in the category of secondary data analysis, for which proportionate ethical oversight focuses on consent, data security, and the risk of re-identification rather than on direct participant harm.

The consent arrangements at the two sites reflect different regulatory and institutional contexts while remaining fit for purpose. Vermont’s PROSPR registry uses passive consent on its imaging questionnaire, with an opt-out checkbox; approximately 95% of eligible patients participate, and individuals who decline are excluded from the research dataset [1]. This approach respects autonomy while preserving the population representativeness that is critical for valid screening-performance estimates—opt-in consent models can introduce participation bias that distorts the very metrics the study aims to measure. Pennsylvania secured a waiver of informed consent for inclusion of women screened in its network, a mechanism that is appropriate for a minimal-risk registry-based study using data collected during standard care, where obtaining individual consent from a large, retrospectively identified cohort would be impracticable and would not alter the risk profile [1]. Both arrangements were reviewed and approved by their respective institutional oversight bodies.

Data security is addressed through the established PROSPR infrastructure. The proposal describes secure transfer of the Pennsylvania exam-level dataset to Vermont for pooled analysis, consistent with prior multicenter collaborations within the consortium [1]. The common data elements and linkage infrastructure that support pooled analysis were developed under the PROSPR program and include standardized data-handling procedures [1]. The residual risk of re-identification in a dataset containing demographic, clinical, and geographic information is real but is managed through standard registry security practices, including the removal of direct identifiers before transfer and the use of the PROSPR centers’ existing data-governance frameworks.

No environmental or community-level risks are raised by the project. The work does not involve hazardous materials, biological specimens, or interventions that could affect non-participants. The potential community benefit—improved evidence on the performance of a dose-reducing screening technology that may alter recall rates, biopsy rates, and density-based recommendations—is relevant to the populations served by the participating facilities and to the broader screening community.

The ethical dimension that warrants mention, though it does not affect the safety judgement, is the observational before/after design’s inability to resolve causal attribution [1]. If the modality change differentially affects subgroups, the resulting performance estimates could influence clinical recommendations. This is not an ethical defect in the study’s conduct—the design is pragmatically justified by the impracticality of randomization once facilities adopted s2D/DBT, and the transparent acknowledgment of the design’s limitations is the appropriate scientific response [1]. The study generates population-based performance characterization rather than definitive causal evidence, and its findings will appropriately be interpreted within those constraints.

## Management

**The management arrangements are appropriately scoped for a secondary data-analysis project conducted within an established multicenter consortium, and the documented expertise, resources, and collaborative infrastructure are sufficient to deliver the three stated aims as designed.** The project assembles existing registry data, pools common data elements, establishes twelve-month outcomes, and performs planned analyses within a functioning collaborative network; this is a well-understood analytic workflow that draws on infrastructure already in place [1].

**The team’s expertise directly matches the required tasks.** Principal investigator Brian L. Sprague and the broader group combine radiology, pathology, epidemiology, and multicenter PROSPR collaboration experience, with a track record of published analyses using the same data sources, outcome definitions, and statistical methods proposed here [1]. The preliminary Pennsylvania single-site analysis—which examined recall, cancer detection, DCIS detection, and density classification in 5,366 s2D/DBT exams versus 15,571 DM/DBT exams—demonstrates operational command of the data-extraction, cohort-definition, and analytic pipeline [1]. The pooled Vermont, Pennsylvania, and Dartmouth DM/DBT performance estimates that inform the power calculations further confirm that multicenter data assembly and standardized outcome definitions are not aspirational but established [1]. This institutional knowledge reduces the risk that the project will encounter unforeseen data-compatibility or definitional obstacles.

**The resource base is the mature PROSPR infrastructure, not newly assembled capacity.** The Vermont and Pennsylvania PROSPR centers have collected standardized breast screening and outcomes information since 2011, and the common data elements and linkage infrastructure that enable pooled analysis are already operational [1]. The data sources—patient questionnaires, radiology systems, electronic health records, pathology reports, and state cancer registries—are maintained as part of ongoing center operations, not created de novo for this project [1]. The secure transfer of the Pennsylvania exam-level dataset to Vermont for pooling mirrors prior multicenter collaborations within the consortium and does not require negotiating new data-sharing agreements from scratch [1]. The single-vendor restriction to Hologic Selenia Dimensions equipment simplifies the analytic task by holding the s2D reconstruction algorithm constant, removing a source of between-platform variability that would otherwise require additional methods development [1].

**The distribution of responsibilities between the two centers is logically structured.** Vermont serves as the pooling and analytic hub, consistent with its role as the lead PROSPR center for the prior multicenter DM/DBT performance analyses [1]. Pennsylvania contributes its exam-level dataset and site-specific clinical and pathology expertise. The ethical approvals are in place at both sites—passive consent with opt-out in Vermont, waiver of informed consent in Pennsylvania—removing a potential administrative dependency [1]. The project does not require new equipment, site initiation, participant recruitment, or prospective follow-up, all of which would introduce management complexity beyond what is needed here [1].

The main limitation in the supplied management documentation is the absence of a detailed schedule or explicit risk-mitigation plan. The proposal summary describes the workflow—assemble eligible records, pool data, establish outcomes, perform analyses—but does not specify milestones, target completion dates, or contingency plans for plausible delays such as data-transfer logistics or unanticipated between-site data-compatibility issues. However, the collaborative history argues against treating this as a material threat to delivery. The team has executed comparable multicenter analyses within the same infrastructure, the data already exist, and the analytic methods are established within the group. The project’s scale and complexity are well within the demonstrated capacity of the consortium. The management arrangements are sufficient to support delivery of the three stated aims.

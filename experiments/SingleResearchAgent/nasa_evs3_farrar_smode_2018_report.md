# Project review

## Overall assessment

S-MODE proposes a coordinated airborne and oceanographic investigation of the hypothesis that submesoscale ocean dynamics make important contributions to the vertical exchange of climate and biological variables in the upper ocean. The investigation will combine direct in situ measurements, airborne remote sensing, theory, and numerical simulation to determine how kilometer-scale fronts and eddies move water and its properties between the surface boundary layer and the ocean interior, operating in the California Current region offshore of San Francisco with a pilot campaign and two seasonal intensive operations periods. The most valuable expected knowledge is a benchmark observational dataset that can discriminate between competing high-resolution model predictions, with the core contribution being three-dimensional characterization of submesoscale frontal structure and vertical velocity from two independent methods (Lagrangian floats and Wave Glider divergence arrays), together with a systematic analysis of the relationship between remotely sensed centimeter-depth surface currents and subsurface currents through the boundary layer.

The proposal is strongly supported by demonstrated instrument maturity (TRL 6+, multiple prior flight campaigns), substantial aircraft accommodation margins, a well-defined threshold/baseline approach with explicit descope options, a pilot campaign to test platform interoperability, a capable science team led by experienced investigators, a coherent management plan with named leads and scheduled data delivery, and a clearly articulated scientific rationale grounded in a substantial modeling and observational literature. The Wave Glider divergence arrays and Lagrangian floats provide two complementary vertical-velocity methods that offer cross-validation. The principal limitation is that the most exploratory goal—developing a remotely sensed proxy for vertical flux—depends on an empirical vertical length scale whose generalizability across fronts, seasons, and forcings cannot be established before the field measurements; the achievable accuracy of the proxy-based flux remains uncertain. Operational coordination of three aircraft with different speed and altitude characteristics to maintain the one-hour coincidence requirement introduces practical complexity, though the defined operating modes and pilot campaign mitigate this risk.

The central trade-off is between the high scientific value and strong implementation on the one hand, and the inherent uncertainty of the most ambitious goal on the other. The threshold mission (Goals 1 and 3) is robust: it will characterize submesoscale three-dimensional structure and relate airborne surface-current measurements to subsurface currents, producing a benchmark dataset that alone would advance model evaluation. The baseline mission adds seasonal contrast and exploratory flux quantification; the flux proxy's dependence on an empirical vertical length scale prevents confident prediction of its ultimate accuracy, but the proposal treats this candidly as exploratory, and even partial methodological progress would be valuable against the well-supported core. The operational complexity of coordinating three aircraft with different flight characteristics to maintain one-hour coincidence in dynamic field conditions requires careful execution, but the pilot campaign, operational modes, and capable team appear equal to the task. Weighing the strong foundations and high expected returns of the threshold mission against the acknowledged uncertainty of the flux proxy, with the latter not endangering the threshold science, the current plan justifies support.

**Rating:** Excellent (A). **Confidence:** High (B).

## Scientific value

**Scientific value: Excellent (A). Confidence: High (B).** The investigation addresses a fundamental and unresolved question in upper-ocean dynamics: whether submesoscale fronts and eddies at scales of approximately 0.2–25 km drive substantial vertical exchange of heat, carbon, nutrients, and biological properties between the surface boundary layer and the ocean interior. This question has been motivated by a large body of modeling research showing that increasing model resolution produces abundant submesoscale features with strong vertical velocities, yet the quantitative predictions remain sensitive to grid spacing, parameterized turbulence, and air-sea exchange formulations [1]. Systematic observations capable of discriminating between competing model representations have been sparse because the relevant spatial and temporal scales—kilometer-sized features evolving over hours to days—challenge conventional ship-based and satellite measurement approaches. S-MODE directly targets this observational gap.

The most valuable attainable knowledge is a benchmark observational dataset that combines wide-swath airborne mapping of surface currents and vorticity with three-dimensional in situ characterization of temperature, salinity, velocity, and optical structure within individual submesoscale features [1]. The dataset is explicitly designed to test high-resolution model predictions: the proposal identifies that California Current simulations from ROMS at 500 m and MITgcm at approximately 2 km spacing both generate submesoscale structure but differ in the relative prominence of eddies and fronts, the magnitude of vorticity, and the seasonal contrast between March and September [1]. A snapshot of small-scale variability is insufficient to attribute an observed vertical velocity to submesoscale circulation, because the relevant scales overlap with inertial currents and internal gravity waves that have different dynamics [1]. The sustained sampling strategy—repeated surveys over days, multiple feature realizations, and contrasting spring and fall mixed-layer conditions—directly addresses this attribution challenge [1].

**Threshold mission: structural and kinematic characterization.** The threshold mission (Goals 1 and 3) alone delivers high scientific value. Goal 1 will characterize the evolving three-dimensional temperature, salinity, density, velocity, and optical structure of submesoscale features using two complementary vertical-velocity methods: Lagrangian floats measuring water-following motion directly and Wave Glider divergence arrays integrating horizontal divergence to derive independent vertical velocity estimates [1]. Goal 3 will systematically relate remotely sensed surface currents at centimeter depth to subsurface currents through the upper tens of meters, testing how the relationship varies with wind stress, wave properties, and density stratification [1]. These measurements address a practical impediment to using satellite and airborne current observations for ocean dynamics: prior Loop Current Eddy comparisons between DopplerScatt and ADCP data showed that radar surface-current speeds agreed well with optical measurements (correlation \> 0.86) but could diverge in direction by 10–20°, and that both surface measurements differed substantially from 5 m ADCP currents due to strong vertical shear [2].

The combination of structural characterization and surface-to-subsurface current relationship already provides a discriminating benchmark for model evaluation. By measuring the same features that models simulate—fronts, eddies, and their associated vertical velocity fields—S-MODE’s dataset allows direct comparison between observed and simulated structure, rather than relying on indirect or statistical metrics. The two independent vertical-velocity methods further strengthen this contribution by providing a measurement that has been a persistent challenge in observational oceanography.

**Baseline mission: seasonal contrast and flux exploration.** The baseline mission extends the scientific return by adding a second seasonal intensive operations period, increasing the number of feature realizations and providing direct contrast between spring (deeper mixed layer, stronger forcing) and fall (shallower mixed layer) conditions [1]. This seasonal contrast directly tests model predictions about the seasonal cycle of submesoscale activity, which currently differ between ROMS and MITgcm simulations [1]. Goal 4 adds an exploratory effort to construct a remotely sensed proxy for vertical flux of the form ⟨W\_D′ C′⟩, where W\_D is estimated from surface divergence multiplied by a vertical length scale calibrated with in situ data, and C is a remotely sensed tracer such as chlorophyll or sea surface temperature [1]. The proposal candidly acknowledges that “complete flux quantification is difficult, but even partial progress would advance understanding of vertical transport” [1]. Even if the flux proxy achieves only partial accuracy, the structural dataset from Goals 1–3 will serve the broader modeling, air-sea interaction, and satellite remote-sensing communities as a discriminating benchmark.

**Broader applications.** The data products also support practical satellite-validation applications, including preparation for the SWOT altimetry mission through the overlap region near a planned SWOT fast-repeat crossover [1], and evaluation of satellite-derived ocean-color and sea-surface-temperature products in a challenging high-gradient environment [1]. The seawater samples analyzed for chlorophyll-a, particulate and total organic carbon, oxygen, and nutrients provide essential ground-truth for the remotely sensed optical products [1].

## Innovation

**Innovation: Excellent (A). Confidence: High (B).** S-MODE’s principal originality lies in its coordinated measurement strategy, which combines for the first time wide-swath airborne mapping of submesoscale surface currents with direct, water-following vertical-velocity measurements and independent divergence-based vertical-velocity estimates, all embedded within repeated three-dimensional surveys of targeted frontal features. This configuration advances substantially beyond the observational approaches that have defined the field to date.

**Airborne surface-current context for nested in situ sampling.** The central novel capability is the airborne DopplerScatt radar, which will provide daily maps of surface currents, winds, vorticity, and divergence at approximately 1 km effective resolution over a domain of roughly 75 × 300 km [1]. Pre-S-MODE deployments over the Gulf of Mexico Loop Current Eddy demonstrated that DopplerScatt achieves current-speed correlations exceeding 0.86 against the independent ROCIS optical system, with noise levels below 0.1 m s⁻¹ at 400 m resolution in its high-resolution mode [2].

The innovation is not the radar alone, however, but its integration into a nested field campaign in which those surface maps simultaneously guide and provide spatial context for an array of in situ platforms. The DopplerScatt maps locate fronts and eddies; daily maps then direct the ship, gliders, floats, and drifters to the features of interest, while simultaneously providing the broader spatial context that an isolated ship survey cannot supply. No prior field experiment has exploited such synoptic airborne surface-current context to target Lagrangian sampling within actively evolving submesoscale features.

**Dual vertical-velocity methods with cross-validation.** The use of two independent methods to estimate vertical velocity within these features constitutes a second original contribution. Lagrangian floats equipped with high-rate ADCPs and vertical beams will directly measure the water-following velocity and its vertical component with a precision of approximately 0.001 m s⁻¹ [1]. In parallel, four Wave Gliders arranged in a nominal 1 km square will measure horizontal divergence as a function of depth; integrating this divergence supplies a second, physically independent estimate of vertical velocity [1]. The ability to cross-validate vertical-velocity retrievals from platform types with fundamentally different measurement principles—water-following versus fixed-array—within the same frontal structure substantially strengthens the observational foundation and represents a methodological advance beyond campaigns that have relied on a single technique.

**Improvement over prior observational benchmarks.** The novelty of S-MODE can be usefully contrasted with two relevant prior efforts. The Lagrangian Submesoscale Experiment (LASER) in the Gulf of Mexico reconstructed submesoscale surface velocity fields from dense drifter arrays and revealed convergence up to 8*f* and vorticity up to 13*f* [3], demonstrating that vigorous submesoscale circulation exists in the open ocean. However, LASER lacked the synoptic airborne context that would have allowed targeted sampling of specific frontal features, and it had no Lagrangian vertical-velocity measurements.

Similarly, long-term mooring arrays in the subtropical Pacific detected submesoscale vertical velocities of 10–50 m day⁻¹ and their seasonal modulation [4], but moored time series cannot map the spatial structure and evolution of individual fronts. S-MODE fills both gaps: the combination of airborne coverage, Lagrangian floats, and Wave Glider divergence arrays captures the spatial structure, temporal evolution, and vertical exchanges of multiple features within a known mesoscale setting.

Goal 3 adds a further original element through its systematic investigation of the relationship between remotely sensed centimeter-depth currents and subsurface currents. DopplerScatt samples the top few millimeters to centimeters of the ocean, whereas the circulation relevant to vertical exchange extends through tens of meters [1]. Using dense drifter arrays to bridge the radar measurement to the upper 60 cm and ADCP profiles to extend the comparison deeper, stratified by wind stress, wave properties, and stratification [1], goes beyond the single case study reported in the Loop Current Eddy comparison to establish when and where surface-current maps can be interpreted as proxies for deeper flow. Goal 4’s exploration of a remotely sensed flux proxy is acknowledged as exploratory, but the attempt to build a calibrated relationship between surface divergence patterns and vertical tracer flux, tested against in situ measurements and high-resolution models, is an original approach that could open remote sensing to applications previously confined to in situ techniques [1].

## Rigor

**Rigor: Good (B). Confidence: High (B).** The research design is carefully constructed to support the intended inferences for the project’s primary structural and kinematic goals, with well-specified measurement requirements and two independent methods for estimating the critical vertical-velocity variable. The principal limitation is that the most exploratory component—the remotely sensed flux proxy of Goal 4—depends on an empirical vertical length scale whose generalizability cannot be established before the field data are collected, introducing uncertainty that is appropriately acknowledged but limits confidence in that specific element.

**Goal 1: well-specified requirements with performance margin.** The design’s strength is most evident in Goal 1, which aims to quantify the three-dimensional structure of submesoscale features. The proposal specifies demanding but physically meaningful accuracy requirements matched to the phenomena under study: surface-current gradient precision of 2 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹ against typical submesoscale values near 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹, SST precision of 0.05 °C to resolve fronts with approximately 0.5 °C contrast across 5 km, and float positioning to roughly 300 m to place vertical-velocity records in spatial context [1]. The traceability matrix systematically links each observable to its required sampling, required accuracy, and the demonstrated performance of the assigned instrument [1]. DopplerScatt’s demonstrated current precision of 0.0086 m s⁻¹ over 2 km substantially exceeds the 0.08 m s⁻¹ requirement, providing comfortable margin for the derivative products—vorticity, divergence, and strain—that are central to the scientific interpretation [1].

**Attribution of vertical velocity to submesoscale dynamics.** A critical inference challenge is attributing observed vertical velocities to submesoscale frontal circulation rather than to inertial currents, internal tides, or other wave motions that occupy overlapping spatial and temporal scales [1]. The proposal addresses this through sustained, repeated sampling: each frontal feature is occupied for several days with ship surveys repeating approximately every 1–2 hours at 1 km resolution, while the surrounding mesoscale context is mapped by Seagliders on two-day repeat cycles [1].

The baseline mission’s extension to a second seasonal intensive operations period provides a direct test of how submesoscale activity responds to contrasting mixed-layer depth, stratification, and forcing, which competing model simulations currently disagree about [1]. The observing-system simulation experiment framework, using ROMS and MITgcm at resolutions from 2 km down to a 30 m nonhydrostatic grid, will test whether the planned sampling patterns can separate the signals of interest from tidal and inertial variability before the field campaigns begin [1].

**Cross-validation through independent methods.** The use of two independent vertical-velocity methods—Lagrangian floats measuring water-following motion directly and Wave Glider arrays integrating horizontal divergence with depth—provides internal cross-validation that strengthens inference for Goal 1 [1]. The float method requires the water-following capability demonstrated in prior deployments where independent float-motion and ADCP measurements both indicated subduction [1]. The divergence method requires four Wave Gliders maintaining a nominal 1 km square while resolving velocity differences of approximately 0.02 m s⁻¹ across that spacing [1]; the Nortek Signature 1000 ADCPs with integrated motion sensing on each glider are specified for this purpose [1]. Systematic comparison between these two physically independent estimates of vertical velocity within the same frontal structures will allow the team to assess whether the divergence method, which is more readily extensible to future autonomous arrays, captures the vertical exchange measured directly by the Lagrangian reference.

**Goal 3: systematic test of surface-to-subsurface current relationship.** Goal 3’s design follows logically from the prior Loop Current Eddy comparison. The proposal will assemble simultaneous, co-located velocity observations from DopplerScatt (centimeter depth), dense drifter arrays (upper 60 cm), and shipboard and glider ADCPs (tens of meters), and examine how the relationship varies with wind stress, wave properties, and density stratification [1]. The stratification of analysis by environmental conditions—comparing frontal and non-frontal regions, high and low wind, different wave states—directly tests the mechanisms that cause the radar-to-subsurface relationship to differ from simple Ekman or Stokes-drift expectations. This systematic approach represents a substantial advance over the single-case Loop Current Eddy comparison, where speed agreement was good but direction differences of 10–20° between DopplerScatt and ROCIS were observed, and both surface measurements differed markedly from 5 m ADCP currents [2].

**Goal 4: acknowledged uncertainty in the flux proxy.** Goal 4 introduces the principal rigor limitation. The proposed flux proxy ⟨W\_D′ C′⟩ uses a vertical velocity proxy W\_D = D × h, where D is surface horizontal divergence and h is an empirical vertical length scale calibrated from in situ vertical-velocity measurements [1]. The core uncertainty is whether a single length scale, or a simple functional relationship, can adequately represent the vertical structure of divergence across different frontal types, seasons, mixed-layer depths, and forcing regimes. The length scale will be inferred from the limited set of features where both Lagrangian float vertical velocities and DopplerScatt divergence are available, and separately assessed in numerical models [1]. The proposal explicitly acknowledges that it is “the most exploratory goal” and that “complete flux quantification is difficult, but even partial progress would advance understanding” [1].

This transparency is appropriate: the flux proxy is treated as a methodological development rather than a threshold requirement, and the proposal correctly notes that the threshold mission (Goals 1 and 3) does not depend on it. The statistical treatment is sensibly planned: stability, convergence, and sensitivity to instrumental noise will be tested explicitly, and comparisons will be made with mesoscale omega-equation estimates and Seaglider turbulent-flux estimates [1]. The modeling component will provide independent flux estimates against which the proxy can be evaluated [1]. The combination of in situ calibration, model testing, and statistical evaluation provides a defensible framework for assessing whether the proxy approach yields useful information, even if the resulting fluxes carry substantial uncertainty. The limitation therefore concerns the confidence with which Goal 4’s quantitative targets can be expected to be met, rather than a flaw in the investigation’s logic for testing the proxy concept itself.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility: Excellent (A). Confidence: High (B).** The investigation can be carried out with the stated methods, platforms, and resources. The instruments are at a mature stage (TRL 6 or higher) with demonstrated flight heritage in the proposed configurations, the aircraft operate with substantial mass and power margins, and a pilot campaign is explicitly designed to test the integrated operations before the main field deployments. The threshold/baseline structure provides a credible descope path.

**Instrument maturity and demonstrated capability.** Instrument maturity is a decisive strength. PRISM has operated on the ER-2 and other aircraft at the proposed altitude of approximately 65,000 ft [1]; MASS has participated in 16 field campaigns over seven years [1]; MOSES has been deployed in two Gulf of Mexico campaigns [1]; and DopplerScatt has completed multiple successful campaigns, including the 2017 Gulf of Mexico deployment that produced maps of surface vorticity and divergence [1]. The oceanographic team has extensive experience with the proposed in situ platforms: Seagliders, Lagrangian floats, instrumented Wave Gliders, and underway CTD surveys all have operational track records within the team [1]. This breadth of demonstrated capability means that the feasibility question is not whether individual instruments will perform, but whether their integration into coordinated, simultaneous operations is achievable.

**Pilot campaign for integration risk reduction.** The pilot campaign directly addresses this integration risk. Scheduled for April 2020 with a September backup, it includes all three aircraft systems, a research vessel, four Wave Gliders, and approximately 30 surface drifters [1]. Seagliders and Lagrangian floats are omitted from the pilot only to limit costs and because the team already has extensive experience with those platforms; surface drifters substitute for the frontal float during this limited operation [1]. The pilot will test platform-to-shore interoperability, the space-time sampling strategy, and agreement between velocity and gradient measurements from different instruments. The year-long gap between the pilot and the first main campaign (April 2021) provides time for processing, intercomparison, and correction of any interoperability problems [1].

**Aircraft accommodation and platforms.** Aircraft accommodation margins are substantial. The DopplerScatt and MOSES payload on the King Air has a 55% mass margin (497 lb required versus 772 lb capability), a 510% volume margin, and margins of 25% in altitude and 20% in endurance [1]. The MASS payload on the Twin Otter has a 127% mass margin (265 lb versus 600 lb) and margins exceeding 300% in altitude and 13% in endurance [1]. These margins provide considerable tolerance for operational adjustments, and the proposal identifies alternative aircraft if the preferred platforms become unavailable [1]. A 79-day UNOLS vessel request with R/V Sally Ride as the nominal platform matches the field scope, and six planned postdoctoral researchers provide adequate analytical capacity for the complex multi-instrument data integration [1].

**Risk management.** The risk matrix identifies the main threats to feasibility—fog, cloud, rain, weak winds, airspace restrictions, instrument anomalies, and platform loss—with credible mitigations [1]. Campaign seasons were selected to avoid the worst seasonal fog while capturing contrasting submesoscale conditions [1]. A 200% campaign-duration margin absorbs weather-related delays [1]. Early coordination with air traffic control addresses airspace risks [1]. Instrument performance risks are managed through ground testing, test flights, and procurement of likely replacement components six months before campaigns [1]. Autonomous platform losses are mitigated through laboratory testing, reorganization of remaining platforms and ship tracks, and replacement of early-campaign losses from reserve [1].

**Multi-aircraft coordination challenge.** The most demanding operational challenge is coordinating three aircraft with fundamentally different flight characteristics: the King Air carrying DopplerScatt and MOSES at 28,000 ft, the ER-2 carrying PRISM at 65,000 ft, and the Twin Otter carrying MASS at lower altitude [1]. Maintaining the one-hour coincidence requirement for SST and ocean-color alignment with current maps—corresponding to substantially less than 1 km displacement for a typical current of 0.1 m s⁻¹ [1]—requires careful scheduling. The three defined operating modes (broad survey, fast-sampling, and fast-synoptic) and the near-real-time decision-making process for daily mission selection [1] provide a workable framework. The proposal estimates that the faster systems can cover the intensive area in under an hour and MASS in about two hours [1], suggesting the one-hour requirement is achievable with disciplined execution. The pilot campaign will provide the first real-world test of whether this coordination can be maintained consistently in field conditions; the presence of three experienced instrument teams and a Science Investigation Manager with explicit coordination responsibility [1] supports confidence.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety: Excellent (A). Confidence: High (B).** The proposed field operations involve standard oceanographic and airborne measurement techniques that are well established within the research community and do not introduce novel ethical or safety concerns. The proposal identifies and appropriately manages the material risks associated with the campaign, and no significant ethical issues concerning human participants, animal subjects, data privacy, or community impact are raised.

The investigation does not involve human participants, animal subjects, or sensitive personal data. The fieldwork is conducted entirely in the open ocean, approximately 300 km offshore of San Francisco [1], and does not require access to culturally sensitive sites, protected areas, or coastal communities. No ethical review beyond standard institutional and agency oversight for environmental field research is indicated, and the proposal’s compliance with NASA operating requirements for aircraft and vessel operations [1] provides an appropriate governance framework.

**Environmental considerations.** The principal environmental consideration is the deployment of approximately 100 surface drifters and the operation of autonomous platforms that remain at sea. The proposal explicitly specifies CARTHE-style biodegradable drifters [1], which are designed to minimize long-term marine debris impact. The drifters follow the upper 60 cm of the water column with low wind slip [1], and their eventual degradation reduces the environmental footprint compared with conventional non-biodegradable designs. The remaining autonomous platforms—Wave Gliders, Seagliders, and Lagrangian floats—are recovered at the end of each deployment period. A risk of platform loss is acknowledged in the risk matrix, with the residual consequence and likelihood rated as 4 (significant mission risk or consumption of all contingency) and 1 (\<5% probability) [1]. Lost platforms would represent a minor and localized addition to seafloor material, and the low probability reflects the team’s experience with these systems.

**Aircraft and vessel safety.** Aircraft safety is managed through NASA’s established airworthiness and operations framework. All airborne instruments are at TRL 6 or higher with demonstrated flight heritage in the proposed configurations, and the aircraft accommodation requirements fit within the stated capabilities of the King Air, ER-2, and Twin Otter [1]. The risk matrix addresses airborne instrument anomalies and performance risks with mitigations including ground testing, test flights, instrument team availability for repairs, schedule flexibility, and early procurement of likely replacement components [1]. Airspace risks are addressed through region selection avoiding nearby warning areas and early coordination with air traffic control [1]. Vessel and aircraft operations follow standard protocols; the research vessel operates under UNOLS procedures with R/V Sally Ride as the nominal platform [1].

**Operational safety management.** Operational safety for shipboard personnel, aircraft crews, and science parties follows established institutional procedures. The pilot campaign provides an explicit opportunity to test platform interoperability and correct any safety-related coordination issues before the main campaigns [1]. The defined reporting structure—with the PI, Science Investigation Manager, and NASA Ames Earth Science Project Office deployment support [1]—ensures clear lines of safety responsibility. The proposal identifies seasonal fog as the most significant weather hazard, with campaign timing selected to avoid the worst conditions and a 200% campaign-duration margin to absorb weather-related delays [1]. No risks to third parties or coastal populations are identified. Overall, the investigation’s risk profile is typical of well-managed oceanographic field research, and the identified mitigations are appropriate to the described hazards.

## Management

**Management: Excellent (A). Confidence: High (B).** The scientific leadership structure, analytical capacity, and delivery plan are well matched to the scope and complexity of the investigation. Responsibilities are clearly assigned across the main work packages, the team members bring established expertise in their respective domains, and the data delivery schedule and milestone framework provide concrete accountability for the project’s stated products.

**Leadership structure and expertise.** The leadership structure divides the investigation into three coherent work packages under a single Principal Investigator and a Science Investigation Manager. PI J. Thomas Farrar (WHOI) provides overall scientific direction; Ernesto Rodriguez leads aircraft measurements, overseeing DopplerScatt, PRISM, MOSES, and MASS operations; Andrey Shcherbina leads in situ observations, coordinating the research vessel, Wave Gliders, Seagliders, Lagrangian floats, and drifters; and Eric D’Asaro leads science analysis and modeling [1]. Paul Matthias serves as Science Investigation Manager, a role that bridges the scientific and operational elements and provides dedicated coordination capacity [1]. Figure 6.1 depicts the management structure with NASA Ames Earth Science Project Office providing field deployment support, creating clear lines of reporting and decision-making [1].

Each named lead has documented experience with the relevant instruments and platforms: D’Asaro’s prior Lagrangian float deployments demonstrated the water-following and subduction-tracking capability central to Goal 1 [1]; Rodriguez led the DopplerScatt development and the Gulf of Mexico demonstration that established its surface-current retrieval performance [1]; and Shcherbina’s prior work on submesoscale velocity-gradient statistics provides the intellectual foundation for the divergence-array method. This distribution of expertise covers all critical technical areas without gaps.

**Analytical capacity and synthesis planning.** Analytical capacity is appropriately resourced. Six postdoctoral researchers are planned for two-year appointments distributed across the analysis phases [1]. Early appointments support operational optimization through the observing-system simulation experiments; a second cohort focuses on analysis of the first intensive operations period to inform the second; later appointments address air-sea interaction, Wave Glider and wave data analysis, and integrated frontal structure [1]. Two science workshops per year are planned during the main analysis phase (2022–2024), providing structured opportunities for cross-team synthesis and publication planning [1]. This staffing level is adequate for the complex multi-instrument data integration required: each airborne instrument stream (DopplerScatt, PRISM, MASS, MOSES) and each in situ platform type generates substantial data volumes requiring specialized processing, and the cross-platform comparisons central to Goals 1–4 demand dedicated analytical effort.

**Data management and delivery.** The data management plan establishes clear deliverables with named leads and scheduled release dates. Data products are organized by level: Level 0–2 products from the April 2020 pilot are due by October 2020, and those from the April 2021 and September 2021 main campaigns are due by October 2021 and March 2022 respectively [1]. Level 3 derived products—including DopplerScatt vorticity and divergence fields, PRISM chlorophyll-a and POC, and MASS directional wave spectra and whitecap statistics—follow with a lag of approximately six to twelve months after each campaign [1]. Each product line has an identified lead and deposition is specified at the NASA Physical Oceanography Distributed Active Archive Center (PO.DAAC) [1]. The target of six months from campaign completion to public data release is ambitious but realistic given that the instrument teams have established processing pipelines from prior deployments.

**Timeline and milestones.** The milestone schedule maps the full five-year project period from July 2019 to June 2024 [1]. The first year covers historical data analysis, pilot planning, instrument integration, and the April 2020 pilot. The second year processes pilot data, refines the experiment plan, and executes the first main campaign. The third year completes the second main campaign and begins cross-campaign processing and early environmental-dependence analyses. The final two years are dedicated to full vertical-velocity and dissipation data synthesis, model-observation comparison, and publication. This phasing appropriately separates development, field execution, and analysis, with sufficient time allocated to the synthesis phase where the investigation’s main scientific contributions will be produced.

The schedule includes backup windows (September 2020 for the pilot, September 2021/April 2022 for main campaigns) to absorb weather or technical delays [1]. The quarterly risk review process [1] provides a mechanism for identifying and addressing emerging management issues before they affect the scientific schedule. The combination of clear leadership roles, adequate analytical staffing, scheduled data deliveries, and a phased timeline with operational margins supports confidence that the project can deliver its stated aims.

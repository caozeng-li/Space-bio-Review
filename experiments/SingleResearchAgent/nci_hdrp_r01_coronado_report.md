# Project review

## Overall assessment

The PRECISE project aims to test whether telephone-based patient navigation, targeted using a prediction model, improves completion of follow-up colonoscopy among safety-net patients with a positive fecal test. The study is set at Sea Mar Community Health Centers and involves an external validation of an existing adherence prediction model, followed by a pragmatic patient-randomized trial comparing navigation to usual care for patients at moderate or low predicted adherence [1]. The most valuable expected knowledge is evidence on whether scarce navigation resources can be efficiently directed to the patients most likely to forgo necessary follow-up, integrated with a prospective economic evaluation to inform practical, sustainable implementation in community settings [1].

The proposal is supported by substantial preliminary work from the STOP CRC trial and rests on a strong, committed partnership with a large community health center that has an integrated EHR and an existing screening outreach infrastructure [1]. The study also benefits from a clear, manualized intervention protocol and a capable, multidisciplinary team [1]. The main limitations concern the prediction model’s modest discrimination (c-statistic \~0.65) and the trial’s power, which is designed to detect an absolute improvement of about 13 percentage points [1]. These factors create a risk that a modest but clinically meaningful navigation effect could go undetected, and the value of a stratified strategy hinges on external validation of a model whose performance may vary across settings.

The PRECISE study's trade-off lies between its important, policy-relevant question and the methodological constraints that affect its ability to deliver a definitive answer. The pragmatic design, strong partnership, and embedded economic analysis are genuine strengths that favor successful implementation and translation. However, the judgment is materially affected by a modest prediction model and a trial powered only to detect a relatively large effect. Although the plan correctly identifies the model validation as a key analytic step, the study's core contribution—evidence for efficient, targeted navigation—depends on outcomes at the limits of its design sensitivity. The plan is sufficient for a conditional judgment: it will provide useful knowledge on navigation effectiveness and implementation, but a non-significant primary result would require careful interpretation rather than serving as a definitive negative finding. The project represents a well-crafted test of an important strategy, though the expected knowledge gain is tempered by its power and the model's performance.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

**Scientific value is good, not excellent, because the question is important and the attainable evidence would be policy-relevant, but the knowledge gain is bounded by a modest prediction model and a trial powered only for a relatively large effect.**

PRECISE asks a sharply focused, consequential question: can scarce patient navigation resources be directed efficiently to the safety-net patients most likely to forgo a necessary follow-up colonoscopy after a positive fecal immunochemical test? [1]. The background evidence that many control patients complete colonoscopy without navigation, and that low follow-up rates concentrate among identifiable patient subgroups, makes this a well-motivated target for a risk-stratified strategy. In STOP CRC, only 54% of FIT-positive patients completed colonoscopy within one year despite 87% being referred, and 44% of referred non-completers had no recorded reason for non-completion [1]. Providing prospective evidence on effectiveness, costs, and cost-effectiveness by predicted-adherence stratum would directly inform health-system decisions about whether to implement targeted or universal navigation in community settings with limited resources [1].

The specific contributions that the study is positioned to deliver are substantial in kind. A positive result would supply actionable evidence that a parsimonious eight-variable EHR-based prediction model can identify patients who benefit most from navigation, and that a centralized telephone protocol delivered by clinic-based staff can improve colonoscopy completion at an acceptable cost [1]. The embedded economic evaluation using a health-plan perspective, with sensitivity analyses that separate research from replication costs, would give health systems the information needed to weigh implementation [1]. Even a null primary result, provided the estimate is sufficiently precise, would establish an upper bound on the achievable effect of telephone navigation in a pragmatic safety-net context and thus calibrate expectations for future programs.

**The attainable value is materially conditioned by two features of the design.** First, the prediction model that underpins the entire stratification logic shows only modest discrimination: the bootstrap-corrected c-statistic is reported as 0.65 from the development cohort of 1,122 FIT-positive patients, with six-month colonoscopy probabilities ranging from approximately 25% in the lowest quintile to 93% in the highest [1]. While those quintile separations appear encouraging, the published development paper reports a narrower range and an explained variation of only 14%, and the predictor set described in the proposal does not fully match the variable set in the published model [1]. This instability and modest discrimination mean that the external validation at Sea Mar is a genuine test rather than a formality. If the model deteriorates at the new site—a decline in c-statistic of at least 0.05 is specified as the threshold for inferior separation—the stratification scheme loses its empirical foundation, and the study's ability to assess *targeted* effectiveness is compromised [1]. In that scenario, the trial would still provide evidence on the average effect of navigation in a lower-adherence population, but the distinctive contribution on efficient targeting would be weakened.

Second, the power calculation is designed to detect an absolute improvement of 12.9 percentage points (from a baseline of 44% to 56.9%) at 80% power, assuming an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.03 [1]. This detectable effect is defensible but relatively large. Published navigation trials in comparable populations have reported absolute increases ranging from a non-significant 10.1 percentage points (Green et al. 2014) to 24.3 percentage points in a small FIT-positive subgroup (Reuland et al. 2024 SCORE trial) [1]. If the true effect in Sea Mar's moderate- and low-adherence patients is, for example, 8–10 percentage points—still a clinically meaningful improvement—the trial as powered would not distinguish it from chance. A non-significant result would therefore be uninterpretable, and neither a positive nor a negative finding would be definitive in isolation.

The project's scientific value is thus real but qualified: it addresses a question of direct practical importance with a credible plan, yet the knowledge it can attain sits at the boundary of what the design sensitivity and model performance can support. The study will produce useful evidence on navigation implementation and costs regardless of the primary outcome, and a well-powered null result with a tight confidence interval would be informative, but the capacity to deliver a clear verdict on *targeted* navigation depends on factors that the design acknowledges but cannot fully control.

## Innovation

**The innovation is good, representing a well-justified incremental advance whose value is tightly linked to the performance of the prediction model.**

The study innovates by formally testing a risk-stratified patient navigation strategy for colonoscopy follow-up in a safety-net setting, departing from the more common approach of evaluating a universal navigation program. The central question—whether scarce navigation resources can be directed to the patients most likely to forgo follow-up—shifts the framing from "does navigation work on average?" to "can we identify who needs it most and deliver it efficiently?" [1]. Combining an externally validated prediction model with a pragmatic, patient-randomized trial and a prospective economic evaluation by predicted-adherence stratum is the project's principal novel contribution [1].

Previous navigation trials have demonstrated that patient navigation can increase colonoscopy completion, but they have typically assessed average effects across all-comers, leaving open the question of whether resources are being spent on patients who would have completed the procedure without assistance [1]. The STOP CRC preliminary work established that barriers clustered around practical domains—preparation, transport, time off work, cost, and fear—that navigation is designed to address [1]. These findings motivate the targeted approach, but they do not themselves demonstrate that a risk model can effectively triage patients. PRECISE proposes to test that proposition directly by randomizing only the moderate- and low-adherence strata while observing the high-adherence group separately, thereby generating evidence on both the effectiveness of navigation and the efficiency gains from stratification [1].

**Several features add incremental originality to the core stratified design.** The embedding of navigation within existing clinic workflows using trained clinic-based staff (nurse practitioners or equivalent) rather than a parallel research-only service explicitly addresses the sustainability gap that has limited the translation of many navigation studies into routine practice [1]. The adaptation of the New Hampshire Colorectal Cancer Screening Program materials to local referral arrangements, insurance coverage, and low-cost colonoscopy resources, together with EHR-integrated documentation, reinforces this implementation-oriented approach [1]. The planned qualitative work with patients, navigators, and clinic staff will capture contextual factors affecting adaptation and sustainability, adding descriptive evidence that complements the quantitative outcomes [1].

The economic evaluation also contributes an innovative dimension by estimating cost-effectiveness and budget impact separately by predicted-adherence stratum, rather than for the intervention population as a whole [1]. This allows the study to answer not only *whether* navigation is cost-effective but *for whom* it offers the best value, which is precisely the information a health system needs to implement a risk-stratified strategy. The use of numbers needed to navigate by probability stratum, net-benefit regression, and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves over willingness-to-pay thresholds provides a nuanced framework for decision-making [1].

The innovation rating is nonetheless bounded by the prediction model itself, which is the linchpin of the stratification logic. The model's modest discrimination and the apparent instability of its predictor set across cohorts mean that the *feasibility* of efficient targeting—as distinct from the *concept*—remains uncertain [1]. If the Phase I validation shows a material decline in discrimination, the study's most distinctive innovative feature is weakened, and the project would default to evaluating universal navigation in a lower-adherence population, which is a valuable but less original contribution. The innovation is well-conceived and appropriately grounded in prior work, but its realization is contingent on empirical outcomes that the proposal correctly identifies as a critical decision point [1].

## Rigor

**Rigor is moderate, because the overall design is sound at the structural level but is constrained by the modest performance of the central prediction model and by power sensitivity that limits the interpretability of the primary outcome.**

The design follows a coherent two-phase logic: externally validate the adherence prediction model in a historical Sea Mar cohort, then use the validated model to stratify a prospective cohort and randomize patients in the lower four quintiles of predicted adherence to centralized telephone navigation or usual care [1]. This sequence is appropriate for the stated goal of testing a risk-stratified strategy. The specification that Phase I validation is a genuine analytic step—with pre-defined criteria for acceptable discrimination decline (c-statistic reduction ≥0.05) and a plan for model updating if calibration is poor—demonstrates methodological awareness of the model's provisional status [1]. Patient-level randomization, stratified by predicted-adherence level, age group, and sex, with clinic treated as a random effect in a multilevel logistic model, correctly matches the intervention delivery mode: navigation is a centralized telephone-based service, so patient-level allocation avoids the efficiency loss of a cluster-randomized design while the random effect accounts for residual clinic-level correlation [1].

**Several design elements strengthen the inferences that can be drawn.** The intention-to-treat framework preserves the randomized comparison against selection bias from non-adherence to the navigation protocol [1]. Outcome ascertainment uses EHR codes supplemented by chart audits for patients without coded completion, and the auditor is blinded to treatment assignment, reducing ascertainment bias in the primary endpoint of one-year colonoscopy completion [1]. The prior finding that EHR codes alone captured approximately 80% of colonoscopy reports justifies the supplemental chart abstraction, and the plan to request missing reports, preparation quality, and completeness data from GI facilities addresses a known source of outcome misclassification [1]. The fidelity monitoring plan—recording 25% of telephone contacts during the first three months and during any new navigator's first three months, with content review against the six-call protocol—provides a basis for distinguishing implementation failure from intervention ineffectiveness [1]. The assessment of usual-care encounters in a 10% sample addresses the risk of contamination, which is a relevant concern when navigators become embedded in clinic workflows [1].

The economic evaluation is methodologically proportionate: a health-plan perspective over a one-year horizon, costed using Medicare fee schedules and staff activity logs, with explicit separation of research costs from replication costs in sensitivity analyses [1]. The use of two-part models to handle cost distributions, bootstrapping for uncertainty, and net-benefit regression with cost-effectiveness acceptability curves provides a standard and defensible framework [1].

The qualitative component is appropriately scoped as a complementary descriptive inquiry. Purposive sampling of approximately 60 patients across predicted-adherence strata and colonoscopy completion status, together with 25–30 staff interviews, will capture implementation context, barriers, and experience that the quantitative endpoints cannot supply [1]. The use of content analysis guided by the Chronic Care Model and emergent themes, with consensus resolution of coding disagreements and advisory-board interpretation, follows recognized practice for applied health services research [1].

**These strengths are materially limited by two design features that affect the trial's capacity to support its central inferences.** The first concerns the prediction model itself. The model was developed on 1,122 FIT-positive patients from STOP CRC with a bootstrap-corrected c-statistic of 0.65—modest discrimination at the development stage, before transport to a new setting [1]. The predictor set described in the proposal (age, Hispanic ethnicity, BMI, prior-year clinic visits, missed appointments, previous CRC screening, prior-year influenza vaccination, and clinic site) differs in composition from the published development model, which includes race, insurance type, GINI income inequality index, and long-term anticoagulant use [1]. This instability in variable selection across cohorts indicates that the model may not capture a stable underlying adherence signal, and its performance at Sea Mar—a distinct community health center population with different demographic composition, insurance mix, and referral pathways—cannot be assumed.

The Phase I validation plan is technically appropriate: it will assess discrimination via the c-statistic, explained variation via an R-squared measure based on the D-statistic, and calibration by comparing predicted with Kaplan–Meier observed probabilities in quintiles, with model updating through intercept recalibration or coefficient revision if needed [1]. These methods can detect deterioration and adjust for systematic over- or under-prediction, but they cannot rescue a model whose predictor set simply lacks discriminative power in the new population. If the validated c-statistic approaches 0.60 or lower, the stratification into adherence quintiles will misclassify a substantial proportion of patients, and the trial's core logic—randomizing only lower-adherence strata to test *targeted* navigation—loses its empirical basis. The design correctly identifies this as a decision point, but it does not specify what would constitute an acceptable minimum performance for proceeding with stratified randomization, beyond the 0.05 decline threshold [1].

The second limitation is the trial's power. The calculation assumes 44% one-year colonoscopy completion under usual care in the moderate- and low-adherence population, a plausible downward adjustment from the 54% observed in STOP CRC overall [1]. With an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.03 and 28 clinics with an average cluster size of 33, the detectable absolute improvement is 12.9 percentage points at 80% power and two-sided alpha 0.05 [1]. The assumed ICC of 0.03 falls within the interquartile range reported for process-of-care outcomes in primary care cluster trials, but it is not estimated from Sea Mar's own colonoscopy completion data and may underestimate the true clustering if clinic-level referral practices, GI facility relationships, or patient case-mix generate greater between-clinic variation [1]. A higher ICC would further increase the minimum detectable difference. The proposal also reports a detectable difference of 9.2 percentage points without the design effect (OR 1.45), but this is irrelevant under the planned clustered analysis and may create a misleading impression of sensitivity [1].

Whether an effect of 12.9 percentage points is realistic is uncertain given the available evidence. The Green et al. 2014 nurse navigator trial found a non-significant 10.1 percentage-point increase, and the FIT-positive subgroup in the Reuland et al. 2024 SCORE trial showed a 24.3 percentage-point difference that was also non-significant with only 66 patients [1]. These estimates bookend a wide range of possible true effects, and the trial is powered to detect effects at the upper end of published point estimates. A non-significant primary result would therefore be difficult to interpret: a true effect of, for example, 8 percentage points—clinically meaningful in a safety-net population—could not be distinguished from a null effect, and the observed confidence interval would be consistent with both a small benefit and no benefit. This does not make the trial uninformative, but it does mean that a null finding would not constitute a definitive negative result and that the precision of the effect estimate—rather than the hypothesis test—would be the primary contribution.

The secondary and moderation analyses are appropriately described but are acknowledged to be underpowered [1]. The approximately 59 expected cancers in the overall positive-test population preclude meaningful comparison of time to cancer treatment, and the interaction odds ratios required for 80% power in moderation analyses (2.98 with the design effect, or 2.17 without it) are large relative to plausible effect-modification magnitudes [1]. The proposal is appropriately cautious about these analyses and emphasizes effect estimation over hypothesis testing, which is the correct stance [1]. The qualitative interviews cannot compensate for imprecise quantitative moderation estimates, but they are not designed to do so; their contribution is descriptive and contextual.

The design's rigor therefore reflects a pragmatic compromise: the trial is structured to detect a relatively large navigation effect in a population selected by a prediction model of modest and uncertain discrimination. The validation, analysis, fidelity, and economic plans are technically sound within that framework, and the study will produce useful evidence regardless of the primary outcome. The constraint is that the knowledge gained sits at the limit of what the design sensitivity permits, and the study's most distinctive inference—that navigation can be efficiently targeted—rests on the prediction model performing adequately at a new site, which remains to be demonstrated.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility is good, supported by a strong site partnership and credible volume projections, but it depends on sustained enrollment yield and on the outcome of Phase I model validation, with no detailed contingency for shortfalls.**

The proposal rests on a solid operational foundation. Sea Mar Community Health Centers operates 28 participating nonspecialty clinics serving approximately 250,000 patients, with an integrated EHR and an existing FIT outreach program that already identifies screening-eligible patients and distributes fecal test kits [1]. The STOP CRC trial demonstrated the team's capacity to conduct large-scale, pragmatic research within comparable community health center networks, and the involvement of Sea Mar's medical director as a co-investigator signals organizational commitment beyond passive permission [1]. These assets make the proposed workflow—EHR-based risk classification at the point of a positive FIT result, followed by centralized telephone navigation—a realistic extension of current operations rather than a de novo research infrastructure.

**The enrollment arithmetic is tight but defensible.** Planning data from Sea Mar estimate 1,186 positive FIT results annually, with 20% classified as high adherence and the remaining 80% (approximately 949 patients) falling into the moderate- and low-adherence strata targeted for randomization [1]. After an assumed 3.5% exclusion for patients not requiring colonoscopy, the available pool is approximately 924 patients, which matches the target sample size exactly [1]. The 18-month planned enrollment period provides a modest buffer—if annual volumes hold, the required number could be reached within roughly 12 months of active recruitment, leaving some margin for seasonal variation or slower initial accrual [1]. The timeline allocates months 15–34 for the intervention, which accommodates both the enrollment window and the one-year follow-up for the last enrolled patients without compressing the data-collection phase [1].

**Three dependencies nonetheless concentrate risk.** First, the volume projection assumes stable FIT positivity rates, screening-eligible population size, and distribution across adherence strata. A downturn in screening participation, a shift in the positivity rate, or a higher-than-expected proportion of patients with recent colonoscopy or other clinical exclusions would reduce the eligible pool. The 3.5% exclusion estimate appears optimistic: the STOP CRC chart abstraction identified 25.9% of non-referred patients as having had a recent colonoscopy, though this applied to the non-referred subset rather than the full positive-FIT population [1]. Even a modest increase in combined exclusions would erode the narrow margin between projected volume and required sample size. No explicit contingency is described for extending enrollment, adding clinics, or adjusting the analysis plan if the target is not met.

Second, the entire stratification scheme depends on the Phase I external validation demonstrating that the prediction model retains adequate discrimination at Sea Mar [1], as discussed under Rigor. If the model's c-statistic declines materially, the empirical basis for restricting randomization to the lower four quintiles weakens. The study could proceed by randomizing all eligible patients regardless of predicted adherence and testing a universal navigation effect, but the design does not articulate this fallback explicitly, and the power calculation would need revisiting because the control-group completion rate would likely be higher with high adherers included [1].

Third, the navigation delivery itself must function within existing clinic resources. The planning estimate of 126 minutes per navigated patient, totaling approximately 970 hours for 462 patients, assumes an efficient six-call protocol delivered by nurse practitioners or equivalent staff with EHR access [1]. The training plan—two didactic days plus eight weeks of coaching—appears proportionate, but staff turnover, competing clinical demands, or lower-than-expected contact completion rates could reduce intervention intensity and dilute the estimated effect [1]. The fidelity monitoring will detect protocol drift but cannot prevent it [1].

These dependencies are individually manageable, but their coincidence in a study with a precisely calculated sample size creates a feasibility profile that is strong in its institutional foundations yet sensitive to execution variables. The project can be carried out as described, provided Sea Mar's FIT volumes remain close to projections and the prediction model validates adequately. The absence of explicit contingencies for volume shortfalls or model underperformance does not make the plan infeasible, but it means that the trial's ability to deliver a clear answer depends on favorable outcomes at two sequential decision points that the proposal correctly identifies but does not fully hedge.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety are excellent: the arrangements are proportionate, respect participant autonomy, and incorporate credible data-protection and oversight measures that match the study's minimal-risk profile.**

The intervention itself—telephone-based patient navigation to assist with follow-up colonoscopy after a positive fecal immunochemical test—does not alter the clinical indication for colonoscopy or the standard referral pathway. Navigation provides practical and informational support (scheduling, bowel preparation, transport, understanding results) while the decision to undergo colonoscopy remains with the patient and their clinical providers [1]. Patients in the usual-care arm continue to receive the existing notification and referral process, and the proposal explicitly states that if navigation proves effective, it will subsequently be offered to control-group and surveillance-group patients who have not completed colonoscopy [1]. This deferred-provision commitment respects the ethical principle that a beneficial service identified through research should be made available to participants who were assigned to a comparator arm, and it is feasible within the study timeline given the one-year primary outcome window and the planned analysis-to-dissemination sequence [1].

**The consent and privacy procedures are well-matched to the data flows and the intervention.** Risk classification is performed on de-identified predictor data extracted from the electronic health record, with study IDs assigned before the prediction model is applied [1]. The statistician controls the randomization and is the only team member with access to predicted probabilities; other staff are blinded to the risk classification, which protects against differential treatment of participants based on predicted adherence [1]. Secure quarterly data transfers and controlled clinical-record access limit the exposure of identifiable health information [1]. Patients provide verbal consent for the recording of navigation telephone calls, and the recording plan—25% of calls in the first three months and during any new navigator's first three months, with a reduction to 5% thereafter—collects only the data needed for fidelity monitoring [1]. Advisory-board review uses de-identified interview data [1]. These layered protections are appropriate for a study that collects identifiable health information and audio recordings but does not administer an experimental drug, device, or invasive procedure.

The qualitative component introduces additional ethical considerations that the proposal addresses adequately. Purposive sampling of approximately 60 patients who did or did not complete colonoscopy, and interviews in English or Spanish exploring understanding, barriers, and experience, require sensitivity to participants' health literacy, language preferences, and potential distress about a missed or delayed cancer screening follow-up [1]. The use of bilingual materials and interviewers, the planned codebook development with consensus resolution, and the involvement of the community advisory board in interpreting de-identified findings provide structural safeguards against misrepresentation of participant experiences [1]. The 25–30 staff interviews are similarly protected through de-identification and advisory-board oversight.

The proposal includes explicit scheduling for institutional review board approval in months 1–3, before any data collection or participant contact, and the five-year timeline accommodates this without compressing subsequent activities [1]. No material risks to participant safety, privacy, or community standing are evident beyond those inherent in any health-services study involving patient contact and clinical data. The risk of psychological distress from discussing barriers to colonoscopy is minimal and is mitigated by the navigators' clinical training (nurse practitioners or equivalent staff), the structured and supportive nature of the six-call protocol, and the availability of usual clinical follow-up [1]. The study does not involve experimental treatments, withholding of indicated care, or deception. The environmental and operational risks are negligible.

The ethics and safety plan is comprehensive relative to the study's minimal-risk profile, and the integration of privacy protections, consent procedures, deferred access to effective navigation, and community oversight constitutes a well-judged framework. No substantial deficiencies or unaddressed risks are identifiable from the proposal materials.

## Management

**Management is good: the leadership is experienced, the team covers the necessary disciplines, the partnership with Sea Mar is institutionally committed, and the five-year timeline is realistic, though it does not articulate an explicit fallback if Phase I model validation fails.**

The proposal documents a management structure that is proportionate to a pragmatic trial embedded in a single community health center network. Gloria Coronado, the principal investigator, led the STOP CRC trial—a large, multi-site pragmatic study involving 26 clinics and approximately 50,000 patients that provides directly relevant experience with the study population, the intervention modality (FIT outreach and follow-up), and the operational demands of conducting research within community health centers [1]. The co-investigator team includes the medical director of Sea Mar (Ricardo Jimenez), which anchors the study in organizational leadership rather than relying solely on research-driven relationships with frontline staff [1]. The team further includes expertise in patient navigation and implementation, gastroenterology, prediction modeling, economic evaluation, qualitative methods, and health-information technology, matching the disciplinary demands of the validation, trial, economic, and qualitative components [1].

**The timeline and training plan support coordinated delivery.** The schedule allocates 60 months across a logically sequenced set of activities: regulatory approval (months 1–3), model validation (months 4–7), navigation-material adaptation (months 5–9), baseline data collection (months 8–12), randomization (months 12–14), staff training (months 14–17), the intervention period including enrollment and follow-up (months 15–34), qualitative interviews (months 34–40), follow-up electronic medical record data extraction (months 36–46), chart abstraction (months 39–53), analysis (months 47–60), and manuscript preparation and dissemination (months 49–60) [1]. The one-year primary outcome window for the last enrolled patients fits within the follow-up data-extraction window without compressing the analysis phase, and the qualitative work is scheduled late enough to capture the full implementation experience but early enough to inform interpretation of the quantitative findings [1].

The navigator training and supervision plan is structured to support quality and consistency. Two didactic days followed by eight weeks of coaching, competency and fidelity checklists, weekly team meetings for the first three months transitioning to bi-weekly meetings, and quarterly specialist consultation provide a graduated support framework that should identify and correct protocol drift [1]. The advisory board—comprising clinic and patient representatives, gastroenterology providers, researchers, payers, and policymakers—meets quarterly throughout the five years and provides a mechanism for external input into material adaptation, interpretation of findings, and dissemination strategy [1].

**Sea Mar's institutional contribution is material and documented.** It commits staff for navigation delivery, training resources, tracking infrastructure, and a clinical champion, while its integrated EHR and existing FIT outreach workflow reduce the research-specific implementation burden [1]. The adaptation of navigation materials to local referral arrangements, insurance coverage, and low-cost colonoscopy resources, together with EHR-integrated documentation, embeds the intervention in routine operations rather than constructing a parallel research service [1].

**The principal management gap concerns the absence of an explicit contingency for the Phase I model validation outcome.** The validation occurs at months 4–7, after which the study must apply the validated model to stratify the prospective cohort and initiate randomization at months 12–14 [1]. If the model's discrimination proves inadequate, the stratification logic for Phase II is undermined, and the study design would need to pivot to a universal navigation comparison or adjust the randomization scheme [1]. The timeline does not allocate a decision point or revision window for this contingency, and the proposal does not specify who holds the authority to determine whether model performance is sufficient to proceed with the stratified design as planned, though the quarterly advisory board meetings and the statistician's role in the validation analysis suggest that such a decision could be made within the existing governance structure [1]. The five-year schedule has sufficient float in the later analysis and dissemination months to absorb a modest delay, but a major redesign following a validation failure would stress the timeline.

The management arrangements are otherwise coherent and well-matched to the project's scope. The multidisciplinary team, institutional partnership, supervision plan, and advisory structure provide the coordination and expertise needed to execute the validation, trial, economic evaluation, and qualitative components. The absence of an explicit model-validation contingency does not undermine the overall management adequacy, but it leaves an acknowledged scientific dependency without a documented operational response.

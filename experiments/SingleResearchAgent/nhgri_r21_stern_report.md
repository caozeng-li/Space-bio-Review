# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project combines epidemiological analysis with historical methods to conduct the first systematic, population-based quantitative study of approximately 15,000 eugenic sterilization recommendations from nine California state institutions between 1921 and 1952. Using a pre-existing, digitized database with 212 variables, the team will analyze demographic and diagnostic patterns, link records to census data to estimate population-based risks, and qualitatively examine institutional correspondence to understand practices of consent and resistance. The most valuable expected knowledge is a rigorous, data-driven account of which groups were disproportionately affected by reproductive control, connecting broad institutional patterns with individual experiences and providing an evidentiary basis for contemporary bioethical discussions.

The project is supported by extremely strong preliminary work, including a completed REDCap database, IRB approvals, and a compelling pilot analysis at Pacific Colony that demonstrates feasibility and reveals significant disparities. The integration of quantitative and qualitative expertise from the investigators (Stern, Kardia, and Harlow) is a major strength. Key limitations that materially affect the judgement include a temporal mismatch between decennial census denominators and multi-year recommendation numerators, and the inherent constraints of surname-based ethnic identification, which has documented validity problems for women and some ethnic groups in California. These issues introduce some uncertainty into the precision of rate ratio comparisons but are largely acknowledged by the investigators and do not invalidate the core descriptive and comparative contributions.

The project's significant scientific value and high feasibility, grounded in a unique and rich dataset, ultimately outweigh its manageable limitations. The methodological concerns are largely transparent and do not prevent the study from achieving its primary goal: a contextualized demographic epidemiology of reproductive control. The qualitative component's robust design further enriches the quantitative findings. This is a carefully prepared and novel project that promises a well-supported, impactful contribution. The current plan is strong and merits support.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**Scientific value is excellent (A).** The project addresses a genuine and important gap in the historical epidemiology of reproductive control. California performed roughly 20,000 of the approximately 60,000 documented eugenic sterilizations in the United States during the twentieth century [1], yet systematic, population-based quantitative accounts of who was affected remain scarce. The proposal will produce the first comprehensive demographic description of approximately 15,000 sterilization recommendation records from nine state institutions across 1921–1952, linked to census and institutional population data [1]. This alone constitutes a substantial descriptive contribution.

Beyond description, the study asks how sterilization risk varied by gender, age, ethnicity, nativity, diagnosis, institution, and period, and how patients and families experienced and contested these practices [1]. The attainable knowledge is both empirically grounded and conceptually significant: a contextualized account of the institutional routines, classificatory decisions, and coercive pressures through which eugenic policies operated, with direct implications for contemporary discussions of genetic and reproductive technologies [1].

Two features of the design give the knowledge claims their reach. First, the project distinguishes selection at two levels: disproportionate institutionalization (comparing institutional populations with catchment-area census aggregates) and disproportionate recommendation within institutions (comparing sterilization recommendations with the institutional census population) [1]. This dual comparison matters because it avoids conflating the two processes and clarifies the institutional mechanisms involved. Second, the qualitative component preserves individual experience. The corpus includes forms, interdepartmental correspondence, approximately 50 handwritten patient protest letters from Patton Hospital, and documentation of refusal and non-consent [1]. The commitment to distinguishing institutional claims about a person from that person's own responses, and to examining how release conditions constrained meaningful choice [1], ensures that the resulting account is not reduced to demographic counts.

The value of this integrated quantitative–qualitative architecture extends to the project's dissemination aims. Suitable findings, visualizations, and narratives will inform a companion digital archive being developed separately with digital-humanities collaborators [1], and the team plans to present results to history, public-health, ethics, and ELSI audiences [1]. The evidence base developed here—linking institutional patterns with documented experiences of consent and resistance—offers an unusually concrete foundation for bioethical discussions that often proceed without systematic empirical reference.

Material constraints on the source data are acknowledged in the proposal. Diagnostic and clinical variables are available only for the 1935–1952 period [1], which limits trend analysis across the full 1921–1952 span. These constraints do not define the primary contribution: they reduce the temporal resolution of some comparisons without undermining the project's capacity to deliver the first systematically documented, population-based demographic account of California's sterilization recommendations, together with a substantial qualitative analysis of institutional practice.

## Innovation

**The project adds important originality beyond prior eugenics scholarship through its systematic integration of epidemiological methods with close historical analysis, applied to an unusually comprehensive and previously unanalyzed body of records (B).** The broad history of eugenic sterilization is documented [1], and qualitative case studies from other jurisdictions have identified patterns. However, no previous study has combined population-based quantitative analysis with systematic qualitative examination of institutional correspondence using the full set of California recommendation records. The proposal's contribution lies in this integration, not in any single disciplinary novelty.

Three features distinguish the project from existing work. First, the dataset itself is original. The approximately 15,000 digitized recommendation records from nine institutions, with 212 coded variables, represent roughly one quarter of the documented national total of eugenic sterilizations [1]. The team has already located, digitized, and de-identified these records [1], creating a research resource that does not currently exist in comparable form. The pilot analysis at Pacific Colony demonstrated that these records support epidemiologically meaningful comparisons: the adjusted logistic model yielded an odds ratio of 2.07 (95% CI 1.65–2.60) for Spanish surname and 3.21 (95% CI 2.67–3.86) for age under 18 [1], establishing that the data can sustain the planned hypothesis tests across all nine institutions.

Second, the study innovates methodologically by constructing population denominators from historical census microdata and institutional reports, enabling rate-based comparisons rather than simple count descriptions. The use of Poisson regression with institutional population as the denominator addresses the inferential gap that has limited previous historical studies [1]. The dual comparison strategy, examining both disproportionate institutionalization and disproportionate selection within institutions [1], adds analytical depth that is absent from existing descriptive accounts.

Third, the qualitative component is designed to serve as more than illustrative context. The systematic coding of approximately 50 handwritten patient protest letters from Patton Hospital, alongside documentation of refusal and non-consent across all institutions [1], will produce an evidence-based analysis of how consent was requested, how refusals were handled, and how release conditions constrained choice [1]. This qualitative work is directly integrated with the quantitative analysis rather than treated as a separate interpretive layer: the proposal commits to preserving the distinction between institutional claims and patient responses [1], an analytical discipline that strengthens both components.

The project does not claim to discover previously unknown eugenic practices or to establish causal mechanisms. Its originality is more specific and more attainable: it will produce the first population-based demographic epidemiology of eugenic sterilization in California, with a level of quantitative precision and qualitative depth that existing scholarship has not achieved.

## Rigor

**The research design is generally rigorous for an exploratory historical-epidemiological study at this stage, supporting its central descriptive and comparative inferences (B).** Several methodological constraints reduce the precision of some risk estimates without undermining the primary contribution, and these limitations are mostly acknowledged in the proposal.

The design rests on two complementary analytical strategies: quantitative analysis of sterilization recommendation records linked to population denominators (Aims 1 and 2), and qualitative analysis of institutional correspondence and patient documents (Aim 3). The quantitative component proceeds from straightforward descriptive tabulation to population-based rate comparisons, a logical sequence supported by the pilot work at Pacific Colony.

**Quantitative design.** The proposal uses approximately 15,000 recommendation records as the numerator and institutional residents enumerated in decennial census microdata as the denominator, with Poisson regression to estimate rate ratios [1]. The pilot analysis at Pacific Colony demonstrated that this approach yields interpretable results: comparing 1,003 recommendations from 1935–1944 with 1,068 residents enumerated in the 1940 census, the adjusted logistic model produced odds ratios of 2.07 (95% CI 1.65–2.60) for Spanish surname and 3.21 (95% CI 2.67–3.86) for age under 18 [1]. These pilot findings credibly support extending the design to all nine institutions and multiple census years.

**Temporal structure of denominators.** Census denominators are point-in-time snapshots (1920, 1930, 1940), while recommendation numerators span multi-year periods [1]. The proposal acknowledges this mismatch and partially addresses it through institutional annual and biennial reports and tract-level 1950 data [1], but the resulting rate estimates will reflect period-averaged rather than strictly contemporaneous comparisons. This does not invalidate the analysis—the pilot example shows that meaningful associations can be detected—but it reduces the temporal resolution of the rate ratios and limits finer-grained trend analysis.

**Design terminology.** The case-cohort designation is imprecise. The design is better characterized as a repeated cross-sectional comparison of sterilization recommendations to the contemporaneous institutional census population. This terminological imprecision does not affect the statistical validity of the planned Poisson regression with the log of institutional population as an offset, which is appropriate for estimating rate ratios in this context.

**Spanish-surname identification.** The plan to apply Census Bureau Spanish-surname lists consistently across recommendation and census records, with manual review for misspellings [1], is a defensible approach. The proposal demonstrates awareness that historical census racial categories changed across decades and commits to constructing comparable variables rather than assuming interchangeability [1]. However, the Spanish-surname method has known validity limitations that are relevant in California: sensitivity and specificity are lower for women than for men, for people of higher socioeconomic status, and in populations with high concentrations of Filipino, Italian, or Portuguese surnames [2]. The manual review of misspellings addresses enumerator and staff recording errors but does not mitigate these structural validity constraints. Differential misclassification by gender could introduce bias into the female–male comparisons. This is a genuine constraint on the precision of ethnicity-specific rate ratios rather than a fatal flaw, since the internal consistency of the method across sources and the large effect sizes seen in the pilot (odds ratios above 2.0) suggest that misclassification is unlikely to produce artefactual findings of the anticipated magnitude.

**Qualitative design.** The qualitative component is well structured for its aims. The corpus includes forms, interdepartmental correspondence, patient letters, and documentation of refusal and non-consent [1]. The proposal commits to systematic coding of protest letters and refusal cases, distinguishing institutional claims from patient responses, and situating findings within the histories of eugenics and institutionalization [1]. The procedures for thematic coding and integration with quantitative results are described in general rather than detailed terms—a limitation of presentation rather than design—but the analytical commitments stated reflect appropriate historical method.

**Integration and inferential scope.** The quantitative and qualitative components are designed to be mutually informative rather than merely parallel. Emerging quantitative patterns will guide qualitative investigation, and close reading of documents will contextualize numerical associations [1]. This integration supports the project's intended inference—a contextualized demographic epidemiology of reproductive control—without overclaiming causal relationships. The design appropriately treats the historical records as products of institutional routines whose categories must themselves be analyzed, rather than as transparent windows onto objective characteristics of the people recorded.

The main variables are unavailable before 1935 [1], which constrains adjusted comparisons and trend analysis across the full 1921–1952 period. This limitation does not prevent valuable descriptive and comparative work for the period where richer data exist.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility is high (A).** The project rests on an unusually solid foundation of completed preparatory work, existing data infrastructure, and demonstrated access to all necessary records, making the 24-month analytical plan credible.

The principal feasibility strength is that the project's most demanding and uncertain phases—record location, digitization, variable coding, and ethics approvals—are already complete. The team has located microfilm reels containing sterilization recommendations, supplementary letters, and rosters from all nine California state institutions; digitized these records; developed a de‑identified, HIPAA‑compliant REDCap instrument with 212 variables; and secured IRB approvals from both the California Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects and the University of Michigan biomedical IRB [1]. Data entry for the approximately 15,000 records is scheduled for completion before the analytical phase begins [1]. This means the funded work faces no archival discovery risk, no digitization backlog, and no unresolved ethics barriers; it begins with an analyzable dataset in hand.

Access to the population denominator sources is equally well established. The team will use census microdata available through the University of Michigan's Institute for Social Research (1920, 1930, 1940) and tract-level 1950 reports [1]. These are standard research resources that do not require special negotiation. Institutional annual and biennial reports supplement the census data. The data availability table [1] demonstrates that the investigators have systematically mapped which variables exist in which sources and years, reducing the risk of discovering unbridgeable gaps during analysis.

The pilot work provides the strongest feasibility evidence. The Pacific Colony pilot analyzed 1,003 sterilization recommendations from 1935–1944 against 1,068 residents enumerated in the 1940 census and produced interpretable adjusted odds ratios [1]. This exercise validated the entire analytical pipeline—record extraction, variable harmonization, denominator construction, and regression modeling—on a subset of the data. Extending this demonstrated workflow to all nine institutions and additional census years is an expansion of scale rather than a novel methodological undertaking.

The 24-month schedule distributes tasks sensibly: Year 1 creates the census companion dataset, produces descriptive statistics, begins population-based hypothesis tests, and initiates qualitative coding; Year 2 expands hypothesis testing, examines institutional variation, develops the analysis of refusals and consent, and prepares publications [1]. The parallel conduct of quantitative and qualitative analysis is appropriate given that emerging quantitative patterns are intended to guide qualitative investigation, and the schedule reflects this interdependence without creating rigid sequential dependencies.

The companion archive (Aim 4) is the one element whose feasibility is not fully within this grant's control. The archive development is described as a parallel project for separate humanities support, with this study contributing analytical findings [1]. This arrangement is appropriate for the scope of the proposed work: the primary deliverables are the quantitative and qualitative analyses themselves, and the dissemination plan through conferences, peer-reviewed publications, and ELSI audiences does not depend on archive completion [1].

The existing secure-computing environment at the University of Michigan School of Public Health, the project manager's role in coordinating data access, and the team's prior experience constructing the REDCap instrument [1] further reduce operational risk. No critical-path activity depends on unproven technology, external cooperation not yet secured, or results from a preceding phase. Confidence in this judgement is high, reflecting the unusual degree to which the preparatory infrastructure has been validated before the analytical phase begins.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety arrangements are appropriate and proportionate (A).** The project is retrospective research on historical records involving no direct contact with the people represented, no intervention, and no collection of new biological or clinical specimens. The central ethical obligation is to protect the privacy and dignity of a vulnerable historical population—adults and children recorded in institutional files that contain identifiable information, medical and psychiatric classifications, and intimate institutional allegations about sexuality and behavior [1].

The foundation of the data-protection architecture is the de-identification of the analytical dataset. Source files contain identifiable information, but the team will extract data into HIPAA-compliant REDCap instruments, and the identifier used during data entry will be excluded from exported data and all reports [1]. This means that the analytical work proceeds on a dataset from which direct identifiers have been removed. Digitized source copies and coded data are further protected through password-protected servers, with source copies additionally stored in a locked safe [1]. The protocol requires that all team members complete research-responsibility, confidentiality, and REDCap training and be included on the IRB protocol before gaining access [1]. These measures collectively prevent casual or unauthorized exposure of identifiable records and create an audit trail governing data access.

Institutional oversight is already in place. The project has secured approval from both the California Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects and the University of Michigan biomedical IRB [1]. Dual IRB review is appropriate given that the records originate from California state institutions while the research is conducted at the University of Michigan.

The resource-sharing plan commits to release of suitable data after acceptance of the principal findings, with identifiers removed and disclosure risk minimized, using institutional data-use agreements as appropriate [1]. The plan explicitly subjects sharing to the privacy restrictions on the historical records, which is the correct ordering of priorities: transparency and replicability are served, but not at the expense of exposing information about identifiable individuals.

The qualitative work with patient protest letters and refusal documentation raises distinct but manageable ethical considerations. These documents record the voices of people contesting their own sterilization, and their use in research requires care to avoid reproducing the institutional gaze that the project seeks to analyze. The proposal's commitment to distinguishing institutional claims from patient responses, and to examining how release conditions constrained meaningful choice [1], reflects an analytical orientation that treats these documents as evidence of agency and resistance rather than simply as administrative records. The research does not re-identify individuals in published outputs or archive materials [1].

No environmental or operational safety issues arise from this desk-based research. The overall ethical framework is proportionate: the research addresses questions of acknowledged historical and contemporary importance using records whose subjects cannot give consent, through safeguards that minimize privacy risk while enabling systematic analysis.

## Management

**Management is strong (A).** The project's leadership, team expertise, timeline, and coordination mechanisms are well matched to the stated aims, and the proposal documents a credible operational foundation.

The principal investigator, Alexandra Minna Stern, brings established historical expertise on California eugenics [1]\[证据72: ref 2, ref 5\]. Collaborators Sharon Kardia and Siobán Harlow contribute epidemiological and quantitative methods expertise [1], creating a team structure in which the historical and population-health dimensions are represented by senior investigators with relevant track records. This combination is essential because the project's central contribution depends on integrating quantitative rate estimation with historically informed interpretation of institutional categories and documents. The disciplinary division of responsibility—Stern leads the historical and qualitative analysis, Kardia and Harlow guide the epidemiological design and statistical modeling—is clearly delineated and avoids the ambiguity that can arise when mixed-methods projects lack identifiable methodological leadership for each component.

The operational infrastructure is already in place and has been validated through prior work. The team has constructed the REDCap instrument, established the secure-computing environment at the University of Michigan School of Public Health, and completed data entry for a substantial portion of the records [1]. A project manager will coordinate data access, document requests under the resource-sharing plan, and manage distributions [1], providing an administrative function that protects investigator time for analysis and interpretation. The proposal does not provide detailed CVs or itemized budgets in the supplied passages, but the documentation of completed IRB approvals from two institutions, the existing database with 212 variables, and the pilot analysis [1] constitutes substantive evidence of management capacity rather than merely asserted competence.

The 24-month schedule is appropriately specified and avoids over-compression [1]. Year 1 focuses on constructing the census companion dataset, producing descriptive statistics, beginning population-based hypothesis tests, and initiating qualitative coding. Year 2 expands hypothesis testing, examines institutional variation, develops the analysis of refusals and consent, and prepares publications. The parallel conduct of quantitative and qualitative work is intentional: emerging quantitative patterns are expected to guide qualitative investigation, while close reading of documents will contextualize numerical associations [1]. The schedule reflects this interdependence without creating rigid sequential dependencies. Dissemination—conference presentations, lectures, and journal submissions—begins during the analytical period rather than awaiting its conclusion [1].

The companion digital archive (Aim 4) introduces a dependency that the proposal handles transparently. The archive is being developed as a parallel project with separate humanities support, using an existing Omeka prototype [1]. This study contributes analytical findings, visualizations, and narratives to that archive, but the archive's further development is not funded by this grant. The management implication is that Aim 4's deliverables—suitable content for the archive—are within the team's control, while the archive's completion depends on external factors. The proposal's dissemination plan does not rely on archive completion, and the primary analytical outputs (Aims 1–3) are independently deliverable. This arrangement is appropriate for the stated scope and does not create an unfunded mandate.

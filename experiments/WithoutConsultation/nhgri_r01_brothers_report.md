# Project review

## Overall assessment

This four-year mixed-methods project aims to characterize ethical and regulatory challenges in networked biorepositories—collections that coordinate biospecimens and data across multiple institutions—and to develop practicable governance recommendations. Building on the investigative team's prior work mapping single US biobanks, the study shifts the unit of analysis to the network itself, examining how heterogeneous consent terms, layered oversight, access policies, and community obligations are reconciled when repositories collaborate. The most valuable expected knowledge includes the first systematic cross-network empirical description of how these organizational arrangements generate distinctive stewardship dilemmas, a conceptual reframing of operational concerns such as funding and sustainability as ethical obligations, and stakeholder-tested guidance for consent, privacy, return of results, access, and oversight.

The project is strongly supported by a coherent three-aim design combining a national network inventory, multi-stakeholder interviews, longitudinal participant observation in five purposefully varied cases (e.g., eMERGE, NBSTRN, Mayo Clinic Biobank), and normative analysis refined through focus groups and an expert symposium. The investigative team brings established expertise, prior empirical baselines, and documented access to the observation networks. Material limitations include an observational access profile that is notably thin for two cases, an interview recruitment strategy that acknowledges but does not fully solve the problem of leadership-mediated sampling, and—most significantly for the normative aim—an unspecified method for transforming empirical descriptive findings into justified prescriptive recommendations. The project's published interim results confirm the feasibility of the retrospective component while underscoring the difficulty of separating network-level from single-bank concerns, a boundary condition that the proposal's design can only partially address.

On balance, the proposal's strong descriptive and conceptual ambitions, coupled with a methodologically coherent mixed-methods design, make it a worthwhile contribution to ELSI scholarship and practice. The plan's main limitations—an under-specified normative integration pathway, uneven depth in the prospective observation settings, and an interview recruitment strategy that incompletely mitigates leadership gatekeeping—collectively temper the strength of the generalizable normative claims that can be drawn, but do not undermine the substantial descriptive mapping, conceptual reframing, and stakeholder-grounded guidance that will result. The project represents a timely and well-resourced effort to inform governance of a rapidly evolving research infrastructure, and it merits support with the understanding that its normative conclusions will carry more advisory weight than definitive prescriptive authority.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**Scientific value: excellent.**
The project addresses a genuinely important and under-characterized domain of research ethics, and its attainable knowledge—descriptive, conceptual, and practically oriented—represents a substantial contribution to ELSI scholarship and biorepository governance.

**Descriptive contribution: the first systematic cross-network empirical characterization.**
Prior work by the investigative team established a baseline for single-repository operations: a national survey of 456 US biobanks found that 16% described themselves as formal or informal networks, partnerships, or consortia, but could only identify this as a peripheral feature needing further study [1] [2]. The current project directly addresses that gap by treating the network itself as the primary unit of analysis. The three-aim design—combining a systematic inventory with adapted Boolean searches of PubMed, NIH RePORTER, and CTSA sites, multi-stakeholder interviews targeting up to 100 participants across varied roles, and longitudinal participant observation in five purposefully selected cases—will produce a descriptive map of how network configurations (centralized versus federated, mature versus developing) shape the kinds of ethical challenges that arise [1]. The value of this mapping is not merely taxonomic; it identifies which governance arrangements are associated with which challenges, providing an evidence base that the current guidelines literature—which addresses funding and sustainability as operational rather than ethical concerns—does not supply [1] [3].

**Conceptual contribution: distinguishing network-specific from single-repository ethics.**
The project's conceptual move is to distinguish challenges that are structurally specific to networks from those that are merely single-repository problems writ large. The published results from the funded project demonstrate what this reframing yields: the 2024 Cadigan et al. paper, reporting 38 interviews across 31 networks, showed that network operators themselves consider funding, utilization, and sustainability to be stewardship obligations—ethical commitments to partners, client investigators, and participants—yet these issues have been treated as purely operational in existing best-practice documents [3]. The finding that networking did not necessarily increase funding, utilization, or sustainability challenges the policy presumption that networking is inherently beneficial [3]. These are substantive conceptual contributions that move beyond the "bookends" of participation—consent at entry and return of results at exit—to the operational middle where stewardship is distributed across institutions with different policies, community commitments, and funding models [3].

**Practical contribution: stakeholder-grounded governance guidance.**
The translational aim—formulating generalizable recommendations for consent, privacy, security, access, governance, and oversight, refined through up to ten focus-group sessions and an expert symposium—is a well-structured pathway from empirical description to practicable guidance [1]. The focus groups serve as a stakeholder validation step that tests whether recommendations are perceived as adequate or implementable, with recommendations perceived as inadequate receiving particular attention in revision [1]. This iterative, empirically informed process grounds the recommendations in the observed realities of network operations rather than in abstract principle alone.

**Limitations and contribution scope.**
The empirical-normative integration method is underspecified: the proposal describes *what* will be done (normative core examines literature; focus groups test recommendations) without specifying *how* descriptive findings will be transformed into justified prescriptive claims [1]. This means the resulting recommendations will carry more advisory weight than definitive prescriptive authority. The published outcomes corroborate this assessment: the 2017 "Modernizing Research Regulations" article uses empirical observations as context for a normative essay arguing that the Common Rule framework is "a poor fit for the research systems, collaborations, and multisite infrastructures" of networked biorepositories, rather than demonstrating a systematic derivation of recommendations from data [4]. However, this limitation does not undermine the value of a well-grounded, stakeholder-informed set of governance recommendations for a rapidly evolving research infrastructure. Even recommendations framed as empirically informed consensus guidance—rather than as deductively justified normative conclusions—can materially improve practice in a domain where networks currently operate without systematic ethical guidance.

## Innovation

**Innovation: good.**
The proposal represents a genuine conceptual advance by shifting the unit of analysis from individual biobanks to networks, but its primary originality lies in the mixed-methods empirical ambition rather than in a wholly new theoretical framing of the problem.

**The network-level conceptual shift.**
The investigators' prior work—a national survey of 456 US biobanks and six in-depth case studies—established an empirical baseline on single-repository operations, identifying ethical issues in consent, underutilization, and sustainability [1]. That work treated networks as a peripheral organizational feature: 16% of surveyed biobanks described themselves as formal or informal partnerships or consortia, but the studies analyzed these as characteristics of individual repositories rather than as the primary research object [1] [2]. The current proposal reframes the ethical analysis around structurally network-specific problems: reconciling heterogeneous consent terms when two sites with different donor agreements contribute to the same network, the interaction between local institutional IRBs and centralized network governance, and the ethical dimensions of alternative architectures (centralized versus federated data storage, cloud-based sharing versus registry-linked local tissue) [1]. These are emergent features that cannot be reduced to single-repository ethics: a donor's consent at site A, for example, may not be honored when data are accessed through a portal managed by site B with different policies. The five observation cases deliberately span this architectural variation—from the mature study-based pooling of eMERGE through the cloud-based sharing platform of Sage Bionetworks-Synapse to the planned system-wide cloud repository of the Mayo Clinic Biobank—enabling comparison of how configuration shapes ethical deliberation [1].

**Relationship to the independent literature.**
The proposal appropriately cites McGuire et al. (2011) on eMERGE data-sharing challenges, which documented real network-level problems including inconsistent IRB determinations across sites and tensions between broad data-sharing policies and community-specific trust relationships [5]. However, the proposal does not engage with several important independent contributions. Jane Kaye's (2011) "From single biobanks to international networks: developing e-governance" laid out the conceptual argument that governance systems designed for "one researcher, one project, one jurisdiction" are inadequate for networked research and proposed a shift toward digital governance mechanisms to enable continuous governance of data flows [6]. Dove, Knoppers, and Zawati (2014) proposed a "Safe Harbor Framework for International Ethics Equivalency" that directly addresses the harmonization problem across jurisdictions through a supranational agency with registration, compliance review, and monitoring functions [7]. The BBMRI-ERIC literature provides STS-informed analysis of how pan-European biobank networking reconfigures local practices and creates tensions between centralized frameworks and local autonomy [8]. These omissions mean the proposal does not fully situate its contribution within the broader conceptual landscape of network governance scholarship.

These gaps are significant but not disqualifying. The Kaye paper is programmatic rather than empirical—it calls for new governance thinking without providing systematic characterization of actual network operations. The Dove et al. safe-harbor model addresses international regulatory harmonization, whereas this project focuses on operational practices within US networks. None of the independent works combines retrospective inventory, prospective participant observation across multiple contrasting networks, and normative analysis refined through stakeholder engagement—the specific methodological configuration that constitutes this proposal's primary contribution. The originality thus resides principally in the empirical ambition and mixed-methods design: generating the first cross-network empirical characterization of how diverse US networked biorepositories actually navigate governance tensions, enriched by longitudinal observation of deliberation in real time, and feeding that evidence into stakeholder-validated guidance. The conceptual framing of network-specific ethical challenges, while well-articulated and appropriate, builds on insights already present in the ELSI literature rather than originating a wholly new theoretical construct.

## Rigor

**Rigor: good.**
The research design is methodologically coherent and well-suited to the project's descriptive, conceptual, and stakeholder-informed aims, though three limitations—an under-specified normative integration pathway, uneven depth in prospective observation settings, and an interview recruitment strategy that incompletely mitigates leadership gatekeeping—collectively constrain the strength of the generalizable normative claims the project can support.

**Design architecture.**
The three-aim structure is logically sequenced: a retrospective Aim 1 (systematic inventory and stakeholder interviews) maps the landscape and supplies organizational detail for case selection; a prospective Aim 2 (longitudinal participant observation in five purposefully varied networks) captures governance deliberation as it occurs; and a translational Aim 3 integrates empirical findings with normative analysis refined through stakeholder focus groups and an expert symposium [1]. The shared Framework Analysis and common ATLAS.ti codebase across interviews, documents, and field notes provide a practical mechanism for triangulating stated policies, reported experiences, and observed deliberations [1]. The biweekly team reflection sessions in Aim 2—where investigators discuss how their presence may have influenced deliberation and colleagues contribute interpretations, with significant points incorporated into field notes before coding—constitute a recognized strength grounded in the ethnographic tradition (Emerson, Fretz, and Shaw's *Writing Ethnographic Fieldnotes* is cited) that addresses reflexivity more systematically than many ELSI observational studies [1].

**Case selection and observation access.**
The five provisional cases span two theoretically relevant dimensions: development stage (mature: eMERGE, Kentucky Cancer Registry; new: NBSTRN, Sage Bionetworks-Synapse; in-development: Mayo Clinic Biobank) and network architecture (study-based pooling, registry-linked local tissue, state-held biosamples with virtual repository, cloud-based sharing, locally held specimens with planned system-wide cloud repository) [1]. This purposive maximum-variation logic supports the process-oriented claim that the project will examine how organizational context influences deliberation and choice of solutions [1]. What five cases cannot support—and what the proposal does not claim—is statistical representativeness or prevalence estimation; this is an appropriate trade-off for qualitative case comparison.

However, Table 4 reveals a material imbalance in observation access [1]. Only the Mayo Clinic Biobank provides access across all six observation settings: in-person collaborator meetings, advisory board, conference calls, community advisory board, ethics advisory board, and IRB meetings. For three networks, community advisory board access is unavailable; IRB meetings are accessible only at Mayo. The Kentucky Cancer Registry—selected to represent the mature registry-linked configuration—offers only in-person collaborator meetings and conference calls, excluding advisory boards, ethics advisory boards, and IRB meetings. These gaps mean that, for the registry-based case, the observation component will capture primarily investigator-facing governance deliberations, missing the formal oversight and community-input processes most relevant to the study's ethical questions about how local and network-wide interests are balanced [1]. The proposal acknowledges that observation in changing groups requires coordination with network leadership and the study IRB [1], but does not discuss how these specific access gaps will affect the strength of cross-case comparisons. The Kentucky Cancer Registry risks becoming an interview-and-document case rather than a genuinely observed one, attenuating the contribution of a configuration explicitly included for its contrast value.

**Interview recruitment and dissenting voices.**
The multi-perspective interview design—targeting scientific and administrative leaders, operations managers, informaticists, ethics advisers, IRB members, community advisers, and client researchers—represents a genuine advance over prior studies that typically interviewed one representative per network [1] [3]. The deliberate effort to seek less powerful or dissenting stakeholders is a recognized strength [1]. However, the recruitment pathway introduces a constraint: initial contacts are identified through the inventory process, which favors publicly visible leadership, and snowball sampling from these contacts risks reproducing leadership-selected accounts.

The Kärgel (2026) study of organization-driven research documents precisely this mechanism: gatekeepers actively curated samples with a preference for leadership roles, centrally coordinated interview scheduling, and routed communication through hierarchical channels, producing samples that consisted exclusively of senior personnel and narratives that exhibited compliance-oriented communication with primary concern about information flow back to superiors [9]. Kärgel further found that informal access routes produced more detailed and critical accounts, including descriptions of unjustified sanctions and pressure to marginalize colleagues [9]. The proposal's use of public organizational information alongside snowball sampling partially addresses this concern by creating a pathway to identify IRB members or community advisory board members independently of leadership referrals [1], and the biweekly reflexive process provides a diagnostic mechanism for detecting leadership-filtered accounts. But the proposal does not specify operational strategies for recruiting outside hierarchical channels—such as direct approaches offering interview confidentiality independent of organizational gatekeepers—that the methodological literature suggests are necessary to overcome these effects.

**Normative integration pathway.**
The critical limitation for Aim 3 is the under-specified method for transforming empirical descriptive findings into justified prescriptive recommendations. The proposal describes a staged process: coded data identify recurrent challenges; a "normative core" examines conceptual and legal literature and evaluates candidate solutions against compatibility with ethical/legal standards, costs and trade-offs, and usefulness across configurations; focus groups test preliminary recommendations; and a symposium synthesizes feedback [1]. This arrangement—researcher-driven normative analysis informed by empirical data and refined through stakeholder engagement—resembles the consultative approach in the Davies et al. taxonomy of 32 distinct empirical bioethics methodologies and is a legitimate paradigm within the field [10].

However, the proposal does not name or justify a specific integration method, does not specify the independent normative standards or theories that will govern the analysis, does not explain how conflicts among competing normative frameworks (e.g., donor autonomy versus research benefit versus community stewardship, tensions the proposal itself anticipates between local IRBs and central network priorities [1]) will be adjudicated, and does not clarify the epistemic role of stakeholder testing—if focus-group participants find a recommendation inadequate, is it revised, abandoned, or retained on normative grounds? The Ives et al. consensus standards require that "the method of integration should be explained and justified, including details of what is integrated with what, how and by whom" (94% consensus) and that normative analysis include "attempts to justify position X to person Y with the use of ethical reasoning" (81% consensus) [11]. The team possesses relevant expertise—Richard Sharp brings normative analysis and biobank experience, Mark Rothstein legal and privacy analysis, Eric Juengst philosophical research ethics on the advisory committee—but the proposal does not specify how that expertise will be deployed to meet these standards [1]. The underlying logic is discernible and the approach is situated within a recognizable empirical bioethics paradigm; what is missing is the methodological specification that would allow evaluation of how the normative work will be conducted as rigorous scholarship rather than as informed consensus-building.

**Temporal overlap between observation and recommendation drafting.**
The four-year schedule places preliminary recommendation preparation at Q9–Q12, while observation continues through Q14 [1]. This means initial recommendations are drafted before the final two quarters of observation conclude. The ongoing team reflection (biweekly through Q14) provides a mechanism for late-arriving insights to reach the normative work, but the sequencing creates some risk that recommendations are primarily shaped by retrospective data, with observation serving a supplementary rather than foundational role. The published outcomes corroborate this concern: the retrospective interview component has already produced publishable findings in the 2024 Cadigan et al. paper, while prospective observation results have not yet been reported, suggesting the temporal asymmetry in the schedule reflects a real sequencing challenge [3].

**Overall assessment of rigor.**
The design is well-conceived for its descriptive and stakeholder-grounded aims, with particular strengths in the purposive case-selection logic, the multi-perspective interview structure, and the reflexive observation practice. The limitations—an under-specified normative integration method, uneven observation depth across cases, recruitment dependence on leadership-mediated sampling, and the temporal overlap of data collection and recommendation drafting—do not undermine the project's capacity to produce a rich descriptive characterization of network governance challenges and context-sensitive practice guidance. They do, however, place important boundary conditions on the strength of the normative claims: the resulting recommendations will carry advisory weight grounded in empirical observation and structured stakeholder input, rather than carrying the authority of a fully specified and justified normative framework.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility: good.**
The proposal demonstrates solid overall feasibility grounded in documented investigator access, prior empirical track record, and a realistic multi-year schedule, though the depth of prospective observation access is uneven across cases and the interview recruitment strategy relies on an incompletely specified mechanism for capturing dissenting stakeholder views.

**Observation access: documented but uneven.**
The five provisional observation cases come with documented leadership agreement and varying access depth [1]. The advisory committee embeds a representative from each network (Rex Chisholm for eMERGE, Thomas Tucker for Kentucky Cancer Registry, Michael Watson for NBSTRN, Megan Dorr for Sage Bionetworks, Janet Olson for Mayo Clinic Biobank), which formalizes access commitments within a visible governance structure and makes withdrawal organizationally consequential for the partners themselves [1]. This arrangement strengthens the prospect of sustained cooperation over the twelve-quarter observation window (Q3–Q14) [1].

However, the depth of that access is materially uneven in ways that affect what can be feasibly observed. As discussed under Rigor, Table 4 reveals that the Kentucky Cancer Registry offers only in-person collaborator meetings and conference calls [1]. For the mature registry-based configuration that this case was specifically selected to represent, the observational record will be thin [1]. Three of the five networks lack community advisory board access, meaning the prospective component cannot directly observe how community voices influence network governance, despite the interview design identifying community advisory members as proxies for donor perspectives [1]. These gaps do not make the observation component unworkable—collaborator meetings and conference calls, available across all five cases, cover the core governance venues where network-level decisions about consent harmonization, access, and data sharing are actually made—but they mean that recommendations about community engagement and IRB review of networked research will rest primarily on interview and document data rather than on direct observation for most cases.

**Interview recruitment: recognizes the right problem with incomplete mitigation.**
The proposal identifies the correct problem—leadership-mediated sampling can filter out critical perspectives—and proposes a mitigation: in selected networks, the team will deliberately seek less powerful or dissenting stakeholders [1]. As discussed under Rigor, the Kärgel (2026) study documents the risk at issue, and the proposal's use of public organizational information alongside snowball sampling provides a partial corrective by enabling direct identification of IRB members or community advisory board members independently of leadership referrals [1]. What remains unspecified is the operational mechanism for this independent outreach—for instance, whether direct approaches will offer confidentiality assurances credible enough to enable critical commentary when observation access itself depends on leadership cooperation. The biweekly reflexive process [1] provides a diagnostic mechanism for detecting leadership-filtered accounts, but it is diagnostic rather than corrective.

**Schedule resilience.**
The schedule provides reasonable resilience against the risks identified above. Observation runs Q3–Q14 while preliminary recommendations are prepared Q9–Q12, meaning approximately 1.5 years of observation data feed the initial normative analysis, with continuing observation available for refinement [1]. The explicit provision that findings from Aim 1 may justify replacing or adding cases within time and budget is a genuine contingency, not merely rhetorical [1]. The network inventory (Aim 1a) runs from Q1–Q8, creating a window to identify alternative networks before observation is substantially complete [1]. The team's published track record—a 72% response rate in the national biobank survey (456 of 636 biobanks) and completion of 38 interviews across 31 networks in the preliminary networked-biorepository study—provides credible evidence that the retrospective components are achievable within the planned resources [3] [2]. The principal remaining risk concerns the compressed Q9–Q12 recommendation-preparation window, which has no explicit contingency if the Q7–Q10 challenge-identification phase is delayed [1].

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety: good.**
The proposal identifies the relevant risks for this type of qualitative ELSI research—discomfort, professional repercussions, and indirect identification of individuals or networks—and proposes proportionate safeguards, though the management of consent in the observational component requires careful ongoing coordination that the proposal acknowledges but does not fully resolve.

**Participant-facing procedures.**
Interview and focus-group participants will receive study information, opportunities for questions, and documented consent; they may decline questions, stop recording, or withdraw [1]. Transcripts and published reports will use study identifiers or pseudonyms, and network names will generally be withheld unless permission is obtained [1]. These are standard and appropriate protections for research with adult professionals and stakeholders. The decision not to offer participant incentives is noted but unproblematic for this population [1].

**Confidentiality and data security.**
The proposal commits to restricting access to recordings, transcripts, and linkage information to the research team; paper records will be locked and computer files password protected. Original recordings will be destroyed once transcription and coding are complete [1]. These measures are adequate for the sensitivity level of the data, which involve professional accounts of organizational governance rather than protected health information or vulnerable-participant data.

**Managing professional risk.**
The most material risk is that participants—network staff, IRB members, community advisers—could face professional repercussions if critical accounts of their own organization's governance practices become attributable. The proposal recognizes this through its pseudonymization policy and the provision that network names will be generally withheld [1]. The focus-group design incorporates explicit attention to power differences so that participants can speak freely, with groups composed to enable open discussion, a measure that directly addresses the risk that junior staff or dissenting stakeholders might self-censor in the presence of organizational superiors [1]. The deliberate effort to include less powerful stakeholders in interview recruitment—and to do so through public organizational information rather than solely through leadership-mediated snowball sampling—is itself a risk-mitigation measure, as it reduces the likelihood that only sanitized, leadership-endorsed accounts enter the study record [1].

**Observational consent in changing groups.**
The proposal correctly identifies a genuine methodological and ethical challenge: observation in governance meetings involves transient participants, making explicit individual consent impracticable for every person present at every session. The proposed response—maintaining transparency about the study's presence, providing repeated reminders, and securing appropriate authorization through coordination with network leadership and the study IRB—is a proportionate approach for research involving adult professionals in organizational settings [1]. This is not fully specified (the proposal does not describe what form the "repeated reminders" will take or how the study IRB will authorize a waiver of individual consent for observational settings), but the approach is consistent with recognized practice in ethnographic and organizational research where the unit of observation is the deliberative process rather than individual behavior.

**No identified material ethical defects.**
The research does not involve specimen donors as participants [1]; it does not generate individual-level genomic or clinical data; it does not involve vulnerable populations, invasive procedures, or environmental risks. The identified risks—discomfort during interviews, professional repercussions if accounts are attributed, and indirect network identification—are proportional to the knowledge contribution and are addressed through the suite of confidentiality, pseudonymization, and withdrawal measures described. No material ethical or safety defect is evident in the proposal as submitted.

## Management

**Management: good.**
The proposal's management arrangements are fundamentally sound, drawing on a collaborative team with demonstrated success in biobank ELSI research and clearly allocated responsibilities, though three modest concerns—compressed scheduling for the recommendation phase, under-specified advisory committee engagement in normative work, and the absence of a technical specification for cross-site data coordination—limit full confidence in the normative deliverable timeline.

**Team expertise and responsibility allocation.**
The investigative team is well-constituted for the proposed work. Brothers and Goldenberg share overall oversight, with a sensible division: Goldenberg leads the inventory, interviews, and focus groups at Case Western Reserve, while Brothers leads primary-document analysis, observation, and policy development at Louisville [1]. This two-site structure distributes the substantial fieldwork burden across institutions that each have pre-existing relationships with specific observation networks. The named co-investigators bring complementary depth: Jean Cadigan contributes ethnographic methods grounded in the prior national biobank survey and qualitative case studies [1]; Richard Sharp provides normative analysis and biobank experience [1]; Mark Rothstein supplies deep and directly relevant legal expertise on genetic privacy and biobanking [12] [13]; and Heather Harrell takes primary responsibility for document analysis, supported by her prior work co-authoring biobanking privacy law analyses with Rothstein [13] [14] [1]. Susanne Rivera contributes regulatory and IRB expertise; the absence of identified peer-reviewed publications suggests a practice-based rather than research-based role—her IRB knowledge is relevant but does not independently constitute normative scholarship [1].

The advisory committee is notably well-matched to the observation cases: each of the five networks has a representative, and the committee additionally includes ELSI scholars Gail Henderson and Eric Juengst, who bring philosophical research ethics capacity that strengthens the normative core [1].

**Cross-site coordination and analytical infrastructure.**
The shared ATLAS.ti project and common codebook across interviews, documents, and field notes provide a practical mechanism for data integration [1]. The specified procedure—two coders, one per lead site, independently coding ten transcripts and iterating to at least 90% agreement before distributing remaining work—follows established Framework Method practice and supports systematic comparison across data types. The biweekly team observation debriefings add a further coordination mechanism that serves both reflexivity and analytic development [1]. However, the proposal is silent on how ATLAS.ti project files will be synchronized across institutions (server-based, cloud-hosted, or merged copies). The team's most recent networked-biorepository publication used Dedoose rather than ATLAS.ti for a methodologically similar interview study, suggesting the designated platform is not yet locked in; the demonstrated coding process (primary/secondary coder, weekly team meetings, iterative codebook refinement) is robust and platform-agnostic, but specifying the technical arrangement that supports real-time shared access would strengthen confidence in the two-site workflow [3].

**Schedule feasibility.**
The four-year schedule adopts deliberate overlap between data collection and analysis: observation runs Q3–Q14, while preliminary recommendations are prepared Q9–Q12 [1]. This iterative logic is legitimate for this mixed-methods design—challenges identified early can be refined through continuing observation, and the focus groups (Q11–Q14) and symposium (Q13–Q14) provide explicit mechanisms for testing and revising preliminary recommendations against later findings [1]. The practical risk is compression: if the Q7–Q10 challenge-identification phase is delayed—plausible given that transcription and qualitative analysis run Q7–Q12—the Q9–Q12 window for preparing preliminary recommendations becomes very tight, and the schedule provides no explicit contingency for this [1]. The team's published record confirms they can execute the retrospective component efficiently (38 interviews across 31 networks completed before the 2024 publication), suggesting the pipeline is viable, but the recommendation-preparation phase is the most schedule-sensitive segment of the project [3].

**Normative core and advisory committee engagement.**
The normative core includes philosophical and legal/regulatory specialists who contribute throughout instrument development and interpretation [1]. Rothstein's legal expertise on biobanking privacy is directly applicable and well-established [12] [13]. Sharp's ELSI translational research experience is relevant [15]. The philosophical dimension, however, appears to rely substantially on the advisory committee: Juengst is identified as providing philosophical and normative research ethics, and Henderson as providing ELSI and biobanking expertise [1]. The proposal does not specify how frequently the advisory committee meets, how its input feeds into the written normative reports, or whether Juengst and Henderson are expected to contribute actively to the domain-specific reports that individual investigators will lead [1]. The proposal states that leads will produce reports with "team deliberation," but the role of advisory committee members in that deliberation is left implicit [1]. This creates ambiguity about whether the philosophical depth of the normative analysis depends on the named investigators or on external advisors whose engagement intensity and availability are not specified. Given that the normative integration method itself is underspecified, this ambiguity compounds the uncertainty about how the translational aim will achieve its prescriptive deliverables within the four-year period. The advisory committee membership is qualitatively strong; the management gap is in specifying the mechanism through which that strength will be deployed.

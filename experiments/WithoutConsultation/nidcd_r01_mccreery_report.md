# Project review

## Overall assessment

This R01 proposal, led by an experienced team at Boys Town and the University of Iowa, addresses why school-age children who use hearing aids (CHL) continue to have disproportionate difficulty understanding speech in noise. The project will conduct cross-sectional experiments and 24-month longitudinal assessments with children who have normal hearing (CNH) and mild-to-severe hearing loss, examining three interconnected domains: the sensory and cognitive contributions to spectral resolution, individual perceptual weighting of speech frequencies, and susceptibility to competing talkers. The central hypothesis is that selective attention, working memory, and language contribute to listening performance, with hearing loss adding immediate and cumulative effects of reduced audibility [1].

The project’s most valuable expected contribution is the first longitudinal dataset on how spectral resolution, perceptual weighting, and masked speech recognition co-develop in CHL—a systematic characterization that only this team is positioned to deliver at this scale and quality. The proposal is supported by the team’s extensive preliminary work and demonstrated productivity, including strong pilot data and a participant database from the predecessor Complex Listening study [1]. The measurement approach incorporates meaningful improvements over prior work, including narrowband spectral-ripple carriers with level roving and an efficient method for estimating individual band-importance weights [1]. The explicit consideration of alternative outcomes and the planned preregistration and data sharing are commendable scientific practices [1].

Material limitations, however, affect the project’s ability to deliver its promised mechanistic account. The spectral-ripple ISI manipulation intended to parse sensory from cognitive contributions confounds memory demands with rehearsal strategies and differential attentional lapses, making the core measurement model uncertain. The central Aim 2 hypothesis that selective attention drives frequency-weighting patterns is directly contradicted by the team’s own recently published data (Bosen et al. 2025), which shows no cognitive predictors of individual weights after controlling for age [2]. The Aim 3 comparison in CHL is undermined by the team’s prior finding that attention does not predict two-talker speech performance in this group [3] and by the confounding effects of heterogeneous personal hearing-aid processing. The longitudinal retention assumption of 10% attrition is optimistic relative to the team’s own published experience [1] [4] [5].

Overall, this is a well-resourced descriptive program whose mechanistic hypotheses are not adequately supported by the proposed methods. The project’s core strength is its systematic, multi-measure, longitudinal characterization of a clinically important population by an experienced team with proven productivity. The descriptive data on the co-development of spectral resolution, perceptual weighting, and masked speech recognition in children with hearing loss will be a valuable addition to the literature regardless of whether the specific hypotheses are confirmed. This descriptive contribution supports the team’s long-term translational goal of individualized amplification. However, the proposal overclaims in promising an integrated mechanistic account. The aims are designed as parallel descriptive studies rather than mutually informing components of a single causal model, and the central novel prediction is directly contradicted by the team’s own published evidence. The persistent confounds in the spectral-ripple ISI manipulation, the uncertain stability of individual weight estimates, and the device heterogeneity in the speech-masking experiment in CHL all limit the inferences that can be drawn. These limitations do not make the project uninformative, but they shift its primary contribution toward description rather than the causal explanation that the proposal’s framework promises.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

**The question behind this proposal is of substantial clinical and scientific importance, and the project would deliver meaningful descriptive knowledge—but the promised integrated mechanistic explanation is inadequately supported by the design.**

The persistent difficulty that children who use hearing aids experience when understanding speech in noise, even after amplification restores audibility, is a well-documented and clinically consequential problem. Because children learn and socialize in noisy environments, this deficit directly affects educational outcomes and quality of life [1]. The proposal’s central framework—separating contributions of selective attention, working memory, and language to spectral resolution, perceptual weighting, and susceptibility to competing speech, with hearing loss adding immediate and cumulative effects of reduced audibility—is coherent and addresses a genuine gap in the literature [1].

The project’s most valuable attainable contribution is the first longitudinal dataset on how spectral resolution, individual frequency-band importance weights, and masked speech recognition co-develop over 24 months in school-age children with hearing loss. Cross-sectional comparisons between children with normal hearing, children with hearing loss, and (for Aim 2) normal-hearing adults would extend existing findings into under-characterized domains [1]. The team’s demonstrated capacity to recruit and retain the target populations—drawing on participant databases from the predecessor Complex Listening study and the Outcomes of Children with Hearing Loss (OCHL) program—means this longitudinal infrastructure is realistically attainable [1] [4]. The explicit consideration of alternative outcomes, including stable band-importance weights, weak ISI effects, and language rather than attention as the primary predictor, is a scientific strength that means the project can yield publishable findings regardless of whether specific hypotheses are confirmed [1].

**The project’s integrated framework is not supported by the design of the aims.** The framework posits three routes—spectral attention, frequency-weight inhibition, and stream segregation—united by shared cognitive-linguistic contributors [1]. Yet the experiments do not specify cross-aim models that would test, for instance, whether children who show stronger attention-related concentration of spectral weights at 1–2 kHz (Aim 2) also show less susceptibility to two-talker masking (Aim 3), or whether spectral-ripple thresholds mediate the relation between attention and speech-in-noise performance (Aim 1). The schedule reinforces this separation: Aim 1 data collection completes in years 1–3, Aim 2 in years 2–4, and Aim 3 in years 3–5, producing findings on largely different timelines that limit iterative hypothesis refinement [1]. The project’s yield is therefore more likely to be three sets of correlated individual-differences findings—each valuable—than a single integrated mechanistic account.

**The practical contribution toward individualized amplification is appropriately distant.** Finding that children differ in their band-importance weights does not, by itself, indicate that fitting to individual weights improves outcomes; that would require an intervention study. The most actionable finding would be if individual weights prove stable over 24 months, supporting their use in prescription models—but this is a finding about measurement practicality rather than about tailoring amplification [1]. The immediate practical yield is a systematic characterization that identifies which measures most strongly predict speech-in-noise outcomes and how they change with development, providing building blocks for future translational work.

Judgement: **B** (confidence: B)

## Innovation

**The project’s most original contribution is its longitudinal design, which would add genuinely new data on how spectral resolution, perceptual weighting, and susceptibility to competing speech co-develop in children with hearing loss. The specific mechanistic claims that distinguish the proposal from prior work, however, are substantially weakened by the team’s own published findings, shifting the primary contribution toward systematic description rather than the promised causal account.**

**Longitudinal tracking is the clearest advance.** Multi-year tracking of spectral resolution, individual frequency-band importance weights, and masked speech recognition in the same children with hearing loss is not available in the literature, and this team is uniquely positioned to deliver it, drawing on participant databases from the predecessor Complex Listening study and the OCHL program [1] [4]. The explicit developmental hypotheses—that selective attention and language will shape individual weighting patterns over time, that baseline spectral resolution may contribute to later speech outcomes through cognitive development, and that growth in aided audibility will shift the contribution of affected frequency bands—are scientifically motivated questions for which longitudinal data are genuinely needed [1]. The improved spectral-ripple task with narrowband carriers and level roving addresses known confounds in earlier broadband approaches [1] [6], and the efficient method for estimating individual band-importance weights within 30 minutes represents a methodological advance over the team’s prior four-hour procedure [1].

**The central Aim 2 hypothesis is directly contradicted by the team’s own published data.** The proposal predicts that selective attention concentrates perceptual weights at 1–2 kHz and reduces them at 4–8 kHz [1], but Bosen et al. (2025), using the identical six-band filtered-word method in 42 children with normal hearing aged 6–13, found that “none of the vocabulary and cognitive tasks predicted frequency importance weights after controlling for age” [2]. The published developmental data show that weights at 2 and 4 kHz increase with age—the opposite direction from the proposal’s prediction that attention, which improves with age, reduces high-frequency weighting. The proposal’s preliminary evidence for this hypothesis rests on a pilot of five children [1] that conflicts with the full published sample. This does not invalidate the aim—documenting developmental trajectories in CHL remains scientifically valuable—but it substantially reduces the theoretical novelty of the specific claims that form the proposal’s inferential core, shifting the expected contribution toward description of what changes developmentally rather than explanation of why.

**Aim 3’s cross-sectional findings are already substantially established.** The finding that selective attention predicts two-talker speech performance in CNH but not in CHL has been published by this team (McCreery et al. 2020; Lalonde et al. 2023) [3] [7]. The proposal’s novelty in Aim 3 resides primarily in the longitudinal extension (Experiment 3c) and the within-subjects comparison of both masker types in the same children with the full cognitive battery. These are worthwhile additions, but they are confirmatory and incremental rather than representing a new direction.

The project’s value as a descriptive and measurement contribution is not diminished by these observations. The longitudinal data, the improved measurement methods, and the systematic characterization of an under-studied clinical population represent genuine additions to the literature. But the innovation here is in the systematic, multi-measure, longitudinal design rather than in the theoretical framework itself.

Judgement: **B** (confidence: B)

## Rigor

**The design supports several valuable descriptive inferences, but its ability to deliver the proposed mechanistic account is undermined by specific confounds in the measurement model and by contradictions from the team’s own published data.** These limitations do not make the project uninformative, but they shift the attainable inference from causal explanation toward systematic characterization of individual differences and developmental change.

**The analytical approach has genuine strengths.** The shared cognitive battery—including PPVT-IV, WASI Vocabulary, PEBL Flanker and Switcher tasks, and AWMA working memory measures—is well-justified by prior work linking these constructs to speech-in-noise performance [1] [7]. The use of linear mixed models with participant intercepts and path analysis to distinguish predictors, mediators, and outcomes is appropriate [1]. The power calculations using simulated datasets with 80% power, the commitment to preregistration, and the plans for independent scoring and data sharing through the Open Science Framework [1] strengthen confidence in the replicability of the findings. The explicit alternative hypotheses—stable weights, weak ISI effects, or language rather than attention as primary predictor—are scientifically mature acknowledgements [1].

However, three measurement and design issues materially weaken the inferences for the proposal’s central mechanistic claims.

### The Aim 1 ISI manipulation cannot cleanly separate sensory from cognitive contributions

The proposal predicts that increasing interstimulus intervals from 0.4 to 3 seconds will strengthen associations with attention and working memory, thereby parsing sensory resolution from cognitive demand [1]. Longer ISIs, however, may engage qualitatively different processes—verbal rehearsal strategies, categorical recoding of the percept—rather than providing a quantitative gradient of memory load. Children who spontaneously label stimuli can maintain a categorical representation with less decay, and younger children are less likely to deploy such strategies. Because rehearsal deployment correlates with age, language, and cognitive development—all of which also differ between CNH and CHL groups—the ISI manipulation confounds memory demands with strategy use.

Differential attentional lapses at longer ISIs present a further concern. Witton et al. (2017) demonstrated through simulations that groups with higher lapse rates exhibit higher estimated thresholds even when their veridical thresholds are identical, and that significant but spurious group differences can emerge with as few as 15 participants per group when lapse rates differ by 10% [8]. The proposal’s prediction that attention will predict “lapses or variability” [1] implicitly acknowledges this risk but does not propose a mechanism to distinguish attention-related lapses from genuine memory decay.

The adaptive tracking parameters—number of reversals, step sizes, stopping rule—are not specified, leaving the measurement precision for individual-difference and longitudinal analyses unevaluable [1]. Witton et al. showed that procedures with few reversals produce broad, upwardly biased threshold distributions and that even with 100 reversals the two-standard-deviation range remains at ±20% of the true threshold [8]. Without specified parameters and pilot test-retest data in the target age range, the ability of the spectral-ripple task to support growth-curve models in Experiment 1c is uncertain. Gifford et al. (2018) found no significant correlation between spectral modulation detection and speech understanding in pediatric CI recipients—a dissociation from the robust adult correlation—and 21 of 36 pediatric participants performed near chance, suggesting that spectral ripple tasks in children may tap cognitive and task-engagement factors more heavily than in adults [9].

### The central Aim 2 hypothesis is directly contradicted by the team’s own published data

As noted under Innovation, Bosen et al. (2025), using the identical six-band filtered-word method, found no cognitive predictors of individual weights after controlling for age [2]. The published developmental data show that weights at 2 and 4 kHz increase with age—the opposite direction from the proposal’s prediction. Additionally, the word–nonword contrast cannot isolate attention from lexical knowledge: Bosen et al. confirmed that bands at 500, 1000, and 2000 Hz carried higher weights for real words than for nonwords, demonstrating that lexical knowledge shifts spectral weighting [2]. Children with larger vocabularies may weight bands differently because they extract more top-down information, not because they attend differently. The statistical approach of controlling for vocabulary when relating attention to weights assumes these predictors are separable, yet vocabulary, attention, and age all develop together; the published null result suggests these constructs cannot be statistically disentangled at the individual level with the proposed sample sizes.

The stability of individual weight estimates from 300 trials of a fixed pseudorandom design also remains unvalidated. The Shen and Kern (2018) qBIF procedure that achieves adequate accuracy at approximately 250 trials uses adaptive, entropy-minimizing stimulus selection [10], and Bosen et al. (2026) found that reliability drops steeply with fewer trials in a related fixed design (r = 0.26 at 2,500 trials vs. r = 0.81 at 41,000 trials) [11]. Without test-retest data for the planned procedure, the precision needed for correlating individual weights with attention and language measures is unsubstantiated.

### The Aim 3 comparison in CHL is undermined by prior evidence and device heterogeneity

The proposal predicts that selective attention will reduce susceptibility to competing speech in both CNH and CHL [1]. However, Lalonde et al. (2023), from this same team using the same maskers and speech materials, found a significant hearing-group × attention interaction: attention predicted TTS thresholds in CNH but not in CHL [3]. For CHL, vocabulary and aided audibility—not attention—predicted TTS performance [3]. The authors suggested that “an inability to segregate the target and masker speech may have limited the extent to which selective attention skills were required” [3], consistent with the finding that 29 of 41 CHL required SNRs above 0 dB, where the target dominates the mix and stream segregation is less critical [3]. If the TTS condition does not differentially recruit selective attention in CHL, the SSN–TTS contrast cannot isolate attentional contributions in the group of greatest clinical interest.

Additionally, testing CHL through their personal hearing aids introduces device heterogeneity—manufacturers, models, compression parameters, frequency lowering, noise reduction, directional microphones—that interacts with speech recognition in masker-specific ways [1]. Souza et al. (2019) demonstrated that signal processing modifications interact with individual working memory to affect speech recognition [12]. The proposal measures aided SII as a covariate but cannot separate device-driven effects from the cognitive contributions it seeks to measure.

These concerns do not invalidate Experiment 3a (CNH only), where the cross-sectional attention–TTS relationship is well supported by the team’s prior work [7], but they substantially weaken the inferential basis for the CHL cross-sectional comparison and the longitudinal trajectories in Experiment 3c.

Taken together, these limitations mean the project’s primary rigorous inference is a systematic, multi-measure characterization of how spectral resolution, perceptual weighting, and masked speech recognition co-develop in children with and without hearing loss—a descriptive contribution of genuine value—rather than the mechanistic parsing of sensory and cognitive routes into an integrated causal account.

Judgement: **C** (confidence: B)

## Feasibility

**The recruitment plan is feasible in its overall magnitude given the team’s demonstrated track record and institutional infrastructure, but the longitudinal retention assumptions are more optimistic than the team’s own published data support, and the staggered-entry design creates genuine pressure on the timeline that the proposal does not fully resolve with explicit contingency planning.**

**The team has a strong recruitment foundation.** The predecessor OCHL study recruited approximately 317 CHL and 117 CNH across three sites over roughly 6.5 years, using eligibility criteria very similar to the current proposal [13]. The current targets of approximately 200 CHL and 150 CNH across five years at two sites reflect a per-site annual rate exceeding the OCHL precedent, but this is mitigated by several favorable factors. The direct predecessor Complex Listening study already assembled a pilot dataset of 80 CNH and 130 CHL [1], confirming that the team has assembled a sample of the size and composition needed and that existing participant databases provide an established recruitment pipeline [1] [4]. The two sites are major pediatric audiology research centers with distributed referral networks [14]. National prevalence estimates place the population of children aged 5–17 with any bilateral hearing loss at approximately 0.27%, or roughly 145,000 children nationwide [15], so the absolute numbers of eligible children are sufficient—the limiting factor is the team’s capacity to identify, contact, and enroll participants, which their track record directly addresses.

The reuse strategy is efficient. The visit burden table shows 955 total subject visits across all experiments, with explicit reuse of cohorts (Experiment 1b uses the same cohort as 1a; Experiment 3c reuses the 3b cohort) [1]. This means the overall recruitment target of approximately 200 CHL supports approximately 2.5 full experiment cohorts, consistent with the plan if attrition is modest.

**Longitudinal retention is the primary vulnerability.** The proposal assumes “roughly 10% attrition and additional recruitment as children age out” [1], but the team’s own published data indicate substantially higher attrition. In Tomblin et al. (2018), the CHL sample dropped from 180 at age 5 to 108 at age 8—a 40% reduction over three years [5]. The Walker et al. (2019) speech-in-noise study, closest to the current proposal in design and population, shows that of 290 enrolled children, only 57 contributed data at all four planned visits, while 88 contributed data at only one visit [4]. A more realistic attrition range of 15–25% per year would mean the 80 CHL enrolled at baseline for a longitudinal experiment could yield 48–58 children with complete 24-month data, reducing power for the growth-curve analyses in Experiments 1c, 2c, and 3c. The overall recruitment buffer is shared across competing demands: attrition replacement, aging-out replacement, and the separate samples needed for cross-sectional experiments.

**The staggered-entry design compounds this pressure.** Children enter at ages 6–12 and are followed for 24 months. A child entering at age 11 or 12 will age out before completing follow-up, or their trajectory will extend into adolescence where the proposal acknowledges attention measures may “become less informative” [1]. The schedule shows cross-sectional data collection staggered across years 1–4, with each aim’s longitudinal component extending two years beyond its start [1]. New recruitment in later years must simultaneously feed new cross-sectional experiments and backfill longitudinal cohorts, yet the proposal does not specify annual enrollment targets or model the interaction between attrition and aging-out replacement rates. The reuse chains create dependency: if Aim 1 retention falls materially below 80%, the cohorts for Aims 2c and 3c are correspondingly reduced.

These are implementable refinements rather than fatal flaws—the core recruitment approach rests on a solid foundation—but they represent genuine execution risks that the current proposal does not adequately address with explicit modeling or contingency planning.

Judgement: **B** (confidence: B)

## Ethics and safety

**The proposal describes ethically appropriate procedures and proportionate risk management, providing a sound plan for protecting the well-being of child and adult participants, their data, and clinically relevant findings.**

The study will recruit children aged 6–12 and normal-hearing adults aged 19–40 [1]. The research involves behavioral auditory testing, standardized cognitive and language assessments, and probe-microphone measurements of hearing-aid output—procedures that fall within the category of minimal risk when properly conducted. Auditory signals will be limited below potentially damaging levels using hardware and software safeguards, which is the standard and appropriate protection for psychoacoustic testing with pediatric populations [1]. Probe-microphone measurements, which require insertion of a flexible tube into the ear canal, are addressed with a practical discomfort management plan: the tube will be removed and repositioned if a child reports discomfort. This is consistent with routine pediatric audiology practice.

**Consent, assent, and clinical feedback are appropriately addressed.** Children and caregivers will complete the applicable consent and assent documents, with questions addressed at study visits [1]. The use of age-appropriate assent alongside caregiver consent is standard ethical practice and particularly important for extended testing sessions of 2–3 hours per visit [1]. The study will proceed under institutional review with a single-IRB arrangement for the two participating institutions [1]\[證據56\]. The provision to share clinically relevant audiological, language, or cognitive results with a child’s care or education team—with caregiver permission—demonstrates attention to reciprocity obligations when research assessments generate findings with potential clinical significance. This is particularly relevant for children with hearing loss, whose aided audibility, speech recognition, and language measures may identify needs that could inform clinical or educational intervention. The proposal appropriately conditions such sharing on caregiver permission.

**Data management and privacy protections are well-specified.** Research records and audio-video recordings will be protected by access controls and password-protected storage, and published reports will not identify individual children [1]. De-identified data and documentation will be shared through the Open Science Framework under data-sharing agreements that restrict reidentification and require secure use and return or destruction of data after analysis [1]. These arrangements align with current best practices for sharing human subjects data while protecting participant privacy, particularly for a population that is relatively small and potentially identifiable through combinations of demographic and clinical characteristics. The commitment to release experimental code through the Boys Town GitHub repository further supports transparency. The inclusion of 25 normal-hearing adults for the Aim 2 validation comparison [1] is ethically uncomplicated, as adults can provide informed consent and the testing procedures are non-invasive and minimal-risk.

No material ethical gaps are evident. The proposal does not involve deception, experimental interventions that alter clinical care, withholding of established treatment, or procedures that exceed minimal risk. The research questions directly address documented difficulties that children with hearing loss experience—a study design that is both scientifically necessary and ethically appropriate.

Judgement: **A** (confidence: B)

## Management

**The management plan is well-articulated for its experimental structure, but it does not fully address the schedule risks created by optimistic retention assumptions and the sequential timing of aims, which could affect the delivery of the implied cross-aim integrative analyses.**

The five-year schedule logically sequences data collection, manuscript preparation, and the reuse of participant cohorts across experiments [1]. The division of labor across two established performance sites—Boys Town National Research Hospital and the University of Iowa—is appropriate, drawing on existing participant databases, shared measures, and demonstrated collaborative productivity in the predecessor OCHL and Complex Listening studies [1] [4]. The reuse strategy, where the 80 children with hearing loss from Aim 1 serve as the longitudinal cohort for Aims 2c and 3c, is efficient and reduces duplication [1]. The commitment to preregistration, code release through the Boys Town GitHub repository, and de-identified data sharing through the Open Science Framework reflects sound data stewardship [1].

**Retention assumptions create schedule risk.** The principal vulnerability is the interaction between longitudinal retention and the sequential timing of the three aims. As discussed under Feasibility, the proposal’s assumption of approximately 10% attrition [1] conflicts with the team’s own published experience, where the CHL sample dropped 40% over three years [5] and only 57 of 290 enrolled children contributed data at all four planned visits in the most comparable prior study [4]. Attrition that reduces the longitudinal CHL sample from 80 at baseline to 50–60 at 24 months would reduce power for the growth-curve models in Experiments 1c, 2c, and 3c; the proposal does not specify which hypotheses remain testable under these conditions.

The schedule compounds this risk: Aim 1 cross-sectional data collection occupies years 1–2, Aim 2 begins in year 2, and Aim 3 in year 3 [1]. This staggered structure, while operationally coherent for managing visit burden, means that iterative hypothesis refinement across aims is constrained by timelines that produce findings on largely different schedules. If retention is lower than assumed, the later aims inherit smaller samples that were not part of the original power calculations, and no contingency plan for reduced longitudinal cohorts is specified.

**Expertise and infrastructure are not in question.** The PI and collaborators have a demonstrated record of executing complex longitudinal pediatric audiology research and producing high-quality publications from this infrastructure [3] [7] [2]. The management challenge is one of planning rather than capability: the proposal would be strengthened by explicit attrition modeling drawn from the team’s own OSACHH retention data, specification of annual enrollment targets needed to maintain each experimental cohort, and identification of which specific hypotheses remain testable under realistic sample-size scenarios at 24 months. These refinements would not require changes to the experimental design but would provide a clearer basis for evaluating whether the management plan can deliver the project’s stated aims.

Judgement: **B** (confidence: B)

# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project investigates how emerging commercial and clinical models of whole genome sequencing (WGS) interact with the legal, regulatory, and payment systems that shape access to genetic testing. The planned work is organized into three integrated aims: (1) a systematic landscape analysis of the WGS industry, including technologies, business models, and interviews with industry leaders; (2) a structured, multi-round Policy Delphi process with approximately 40 diverse stakeholders to identify and prioritize policy questions and develop alternative approaches for three selected high-priority issues in the domains of analytical validity, patents and licensing, and reimbursement; and (3) active dissemination of findings to policymakers through briefings, white papers, and electronic resources. The most valuable expected knowledge is a structured, evidence-informed account of how a diverse group of expert stakeholders prioritizes the most pressing WGS clinical-integration policy challenges and what alternative policy approaches they consider desirable and feasible, along with the reasons for agreement and disagreement. This integrated knowledge product—combining an industry landscape, stakeholder-articulated priorities, and policy alternatives within a single project—addresses a genuine gap, as prior work had examined these policy domains in isolation.

The main supporting evidence for this plan lies in its methodologically appropriate design and team experience. The modified Policy Delphi is fit for purpose, explicitly scoped to clarify options and reasons for disagreement rather than to produce a statistical consensus, which is a realistic and well-defended goal given the limited empirical evidence and diverse stakeholder interests. The project has a logical dependency chain (landscape→prioritization→policy analysis) and draws on a team with demonstrated prior work in genetic testing oversight, intellectual property, and reimbursement policy, including direct operational experience with Delphi processes. The critical limitation is that the rigor of several components is moderate, not high. The landscape analysis search strategy is not systematic by formal review standards, and the initial organization list appears underweighted toward the interpretation/bioinformatics sector. The legal analysis method is described only in outline, with no operational protocol or cross-walk template to ensure comparability across the three collaborating institutions. The composite scoring method for question prioritization does not discuss sensitivity to its equal-weighting assumption or specify a tie-breaking rule, limiting the transparency of how top-priority issues will be selected. These are limitations of specification rather than fatal design flaws, but they directly affect the depth and robustness of the knowledge the project can claim.

The main trade-off in this evaluation is between a well-designed, policy-relevant research plan that addresses a genuine gap and specific, recognizable limitations in the rigor of the landscape analysis, the legal analysis specification, and certain aspects of the Delphi design. The integrated architecture—landscape, Delphi, legal analysis—is the project's core strength and represents a valuable contribution that no single prior study provided. The proposal is appropriately candid about its non-representative sampling and the conditional nature of its inferences. The limitations identified—an underweighted interpretation sector in the initial landscape, an underspecified legal analysis method, asymmetric domain expertise, and a thin prior-work differentiation—are real and warrant attention during implementation, but they do not fundamentally undermine the project's ability to deliver useful, structured policy knowledge. The project's achievable contribution, a structured account of informed stakeholder priorities and viable policy alternatives for three critical domains, is proportionate to its scale and methodological constraints.

**Overall, the current plan offers a worthwhile contribution with recognized limits that can be managed within the proposed design.**

## Scientific value

**The project addresses an important and timely policy question whose attainable knowledge is substantial, multi-faceted, and proportional to its design.** The core question—how emerging clinical and commercial models of whole genome sequencing interact with the existing legal, regulatory, intellectual property, and payment systems that shape access to genetic testing—was genuinely pressing at the time of the proposal, as WGS was moving from research into clinical diagnosis and management [1].

**Three integrated products, each with distinct value.** The expected knowledge contribution is organized across three integrated aims. Aim 1 produces a systematic landscape analysis of the WGS industry, identifying organizations developing sequencing technologies, providing sequencing services, and interpreting sequence data, and characterizing their technologies, business models, patents, CLIA certification status, health claims, and third-party coverage [1]. This landscape fills a recognized documentary gap: the proposal correctly observes that "a clear description of the emerging industry is therefore necessary before identifying gaps or proposing policy changes" [1], and no comparable WGS industry landscape existed at the time. The landscape is not merely a directory; it is designed to connect company characteristics to the policy domains that the Delphi panel will subsequently address.

Aim 2 contributes a prioritized, stakeholder-informed list of policy questions and detailed, legally grounded policy alternatives for three selected high-priority issues in the domains of analytical validity and testing quality, patents and licensing, and reimbursement and payment [1]. The five-survey Delphi process over approximately 20 months is explicitly designed to clarify "where positions converge, where they diverge, and what would require negotiation or compromise" [1], rather than to impose a premature consensus. This commitment to articulating the structure of disagreement—rather than suppressing it—is a meaningful scientific output in its own right, particularly for a policy domain where the relevant actors hold different knowledge, responsibilities, and interests [1].

Aim 3 contributes practical dissemination products designed for policymakers, including white papers on current policy, concise issue briefs, webcasts, electronic landscape materials, and a methodological account of the Policy Delphi process itself [1]. The staged dissemination—a year-two Capitol Hill briefing on priorities and a year-three briefing on policy approaches—ensures that findings reach policy audiences during the project period [1].

**Honest scope boundaries strengthen the contribution.** The proposal is appropriately candid about its scope. It acknowledges that other consequential WGS issues, including privacy, ownership and storage of data, workforce needs, and equitable access, will be identified by stakeholders and will remain visible in the published priorities, but will not receive detailed policy development within this study [1]. This is an honest acknowledgment rather than a weakness: the three-domain focus reflects the collaborating teams' specific expertise and maintains a tractable scope for a three-year project.

**Conditional knowledge, proportionate value.** The attainable knowledge is conditional on the panel's composition and the analytical methods, but this conditionality is intrinsic to the Policy Delphi method and does not diminish the project's value. The proposal is explicit that complete stakeholder consensus is not the intended endpoint and that the panel is not a statistically representative population sample [1]. What the project can legitimately produce—a structured representation of how a purposively selected, diverse expert panel prioritizes WGS policy questions and evaluates alternative approaches, with documented reasons—is precisely the type of knowledge that policymakers need when confronting a rapidly evolving technology with limited empirical evidence [1]. This contribution is proportionate to the project's scale and its methodological constraints, and it supplies an integrated knowledge base that no prior single study had provided.

## Innovation

**The project makes a genuine knowledge contribution whose core innovation lies in the integrated design rather than in any single component.** No prior study had combined a systematic WGS industry landscape, a structured multi-stakeholder Policy Delphi across three interconnected policy domains, and detailed legal and policy analysis within a single project.

**Prior work addressed these domains in isolation.** Cook-Deegan and colleagues had examined DNA sequencing technologies, patents, and intellectual-property issues in multi-allele testing [1] [2]. Chandrasekharan and Cook-Deegan (2009) analyzed gene patents and personalized medicine, noting that "prudent policy will depend on careful analysis and foresight" [3]. Deverka and Tunis had studied coverage and reimbursement for molecular diagnostics and pharmacogenomics [1] [4] [5], and McGuire and colleagues had addressed regulation of direct-to-consumer testing [1]. The Genetics and Public Policy Center had conducted prior landscape work on genetic-testing oversight and laboratory quality [1], which the proposal correctly describes as providing a methodological model rather than answering the WGS question. These were essential foundations, but each addressed a single domain, and none connected industry description, stakeholder priority-setting, and legal analysis in an integrated framework.

**The landscape fills a recognized documentary gap.** At the time of the proposal, no published WGS industry landscape existed that systematically linked company business models, technologies, and services to the policy domains of analytical validity, patenting, and reimbursement. The subsequent Curnutte et al. (2016) correspondence in *Nature Biotechnology*, which mapped 43 NGS providers across seven clinical segments, was funded by the same NHGRI grant and represents an output of this project rather than prior work [6]. The prior Hogarth et al. (2008) landscape surveyed the direct-to-consumer genetic testing market, not the broader WGS clinical-integration ecosystem [7].

**The Delphi application is novel in scope.** While the Policy Delphi method itself is established (Turoff 1970, cited in ref. 9) and the proposal draws on systematic reviews of Delphi methods for healthcare quality indicators (Boulkedid et al. 2011, ref. 6) [1], no prior Policy Delphi had been applied to prioritizing WGS clinical-integration policy questions across the three domains of analytical validity, patents, and reimbursement. The Messner et al. (2016) Delphi study on barriers to clinical adoption of NGS—which included Cook-Deegan, Deverka, McGuire, and Messner as co-authors—covered the same three policy domains with a 48-expert panel across four Delphi rounds [8]. However, the current proposal is larger in scope: it adds the landscape analysis as a foundational input, selects one question per domain for detailed alternative development including legal analysis, and includes a structured dissemination plan with Hill briefings. The Messner et al. study identified proprietary variant databases and payer policy inconsistency as key concerns [8]; the proposed project would build on these findings within a more comprehensive analytical architecture.

**The differentiation could be more explicit, but the contribution is real.** The proposal's background and rationale section describes the general need—that existing policy frameworks built around individual genetic tests may not adequately accommodate WGS [1]—rather than systematically reviewing prior work and specifying what each prior study omitted. The reference list (11 items) is notably thin for a project of this scope, lacking citations to some directly relevant work. A more explicit mapping of prior landscapes, Delphi studies, and domain-specific analyses against the proposed integrated design would have strengthened the case for novelty. However, the gap the project addresses was real, and the integrated architecture itself—a descriptive landscape feeding a multi-round stakeholder Delphi that grounds legal and policy analysis—remains its distinctive contribution even if the priority-setting claims are modest. The proposal explicitly limits its claims: "complete stakeholder consensus is not the intended endpoint" [1], and it acknowledges that important questions outside the three domains will remain visible in the published priorities rather than being treated as answered [1]. This honest acknowledgment of scope, combined with the integrated approach, constitutes a meaningful advance beyond prior single-domain analyses.

## Rigor

**The modified Policy Delphi design is methodologically appropriate for its stated goals, and the proposal is candid about its inferential limits, but several specific aspects of the research design have recognizable limitations that constrain the depth and transparency of the project's expected knowledge.**

**A coherent and well-defended core architecture.** The core design is a multi-method, three-phase architecture: a landscape analysis and industry interviews (Aim 1) provide an evidence base that feeds into a five-survey Policy Delphi process with approximately 40 stakeholders (Aim 2), which in turn grounds detailed legal and policy analysis of three selected priority issues [1]. This dependency chain is logically coherent and well-suited to the project's goal of producing a structured, stakeholder-informed account of policy priorities and alternatives rather than a statistical consensus. The proposal explicitly frames the Delphi's purpose as clarifying "options and the reasons for agreement and disagreement," acknowledging that "complete stakeholder consensus is not the intended endpoint" [1]. The panel is not treated as a statistically representative population sample, and the non-representative sampling is correctly positioned as a feature of the Policy Delphi method, not a flaw [1]. This candor strengthens the design by correctly bounding the inferences that the outputs can support.

**Panel composition and process design are defensible.** The planned ≈40 panelists, drawn from three broad constituencies (industry, healthcare, and other participants including regulatory agencies, patient groups, and intellectual-property experts), exceed the median of 17 found in a systematic review of 80 Delphi studies selecting healthcare quality indicators [9] and fall within the established range for policy Delphi studies requiring coverage of multiple stakeholder categories. The iterative five-survey structure, with its progression from question identification through alternative development to evaluation, is consistent with established Policy Delphi methodology (Turoff 1970, cited in ref. 9). The use of anonymous survey rounds before a final face-to-face meeting appropriately gives participants opportunities to introduce unconventional options and challenge arguments without an open meeting immediately favoring the most influential voices [1]. The two-letter coding system, the initial group-building exercise that shares names and affiliations while keeping individual responses coded, and the plan to document minority views are thoughtful measures that enhance both transparency and the integrity of the anonymous process [1].

**Limitation 1: landscape analysis is exploratory, not systematic.** The landscape analysis search strategy, while reasonable for an exploratory industry scan, is not systematic by formal review standards. It relies on organizations self-identifying with keywords in publicly accessible sources, and the initial organization list in Table 1 is visibly weighted toward sequencing technology and service providers: of 34 listed organizations, only a handful represent the interpretation and clinical-annotation sector [1]. This sector is central to the analytical validity and reimbursement policy domains—the bioinformatics pipeline that generates variant calls and clinical interpretations is where many of the most challenging quality and coverage questions arise [10]. An incomplete map of the interpretation sector could shape which policy questions are initially surfaced in survey 1. The Delphi process's openness to panelist additions in survey 1 [1] can partially correct this, but the framing effect of the initial question list is not fully neutralized by the ability to add items. The 20 planned semi-structured interviews are sufficient to identify the range of business models and clinical-integration pathways across the three sectors (code saturation), but sit at the lower bound for developing richly textured understanding within any single sector (meaning saturation) [11].

**Limitation 2: composite scoring lacks necessary specification.** The composite scoring method for question prioritization is transparent but lacks necessary specification. The four five-point Likert items (importance, validity, technical feasibility, political feasibility) are combined into three composites: Priority (importance + validity, range 0–8), Tractability (technical + political feasibility, 0–8), and Overall (all four items, 0–16) [1]. While the equal-weight default is the simplest and most transparent choice, the proposal does not discuss sensitivity to alternative weighting assumptions—rankings could shift meaningfully if, for instance, importance were double-weighted relative to validity. More practically, with ≈40 panelists rating potentially dozens of questions on a 1–5 scale, many items may cluster at similar median values. The proposal does not specify a tie-breaking rule or an agreement-threshold criterion, a gap that matters because 35% of Delphi studies in the Boulkedid et al. review used a combination of median threshold plus percentage agreement, while 16% used median alone [9]. The ultimate selection of the three issues for detailed policy development rests with the investigators and advisory board, making the composites advisory rather than determinative [1]—this is appropriate, but readers of the output need to understand the hybrid nature of the selection process.

**Limitation 3: legal analysis is described only in outline.** The legal analysis component is described only in outline. The proposal states that GPPC, Duke, and CMTP will each examine relevant laws, regulations, professional guidelines, and industry standards, considering their intended purposes, scope, enforcement, and conflicts [1]. These four elements are a sensible starting framework, but the proposal provides no operational protocol: no description of how "sufficiently broad and precise" will be assessed against a benchmark, no common analytical template to ensure comparability of white papers across the three institutions, and no specification of how the Patton and Sawicki policy-analysis framework will be integrated with the Delphi panel's own alternative-generation exercises [1]. The distribution of dedicated legal expertise is asymmetric: the reimbursement domain has deep expertise through Deverka and Tunis at CMTP, the patent domain has strong expertise through Cook-Deegan at Duke, but analytical validity and testing quality draws primarily on GPPC's broader regulatory expertise without an equivalently specialized legal scholar in laboratory regulation. Regulatory coverage also has identifiable gaps: the proposal does not specify how it will address the structural gap between CLIA (which covers analytical validity but not clinical validity) and FDA (whose authority over LDTs was in active policy flux), nor does it detail attention to state-level variation in laboratory oversight (New York and Washington's CLIA-exempt programs, state genetic testing consent laws) or international patent dimensions.

**Limitations of specification, not fatal flaws.** The landscape's incompleteness is partially mitigated by the Delphi panel's ability to add issues, the ongoing monitoring of new entrants, and the advisory board's review. The scoring method's equal-weight default is defensible as the most transparent starting point. The legal analysis's four-element framework provides a reasonable structure that the collaborating institutions, with their demonstrated domain expertise, are capable of executing. Together, however, these limitations mean that the project's knowledge claims—particularly the comprehensiveness of the initial landscape and the rigor of the legal comparisons across domains—will be more conditional and less deeply grounded than a fully specified protocol would support. The integrated design remains the project's strength; the individual components are adequate but not exceptional.

## Feasibility

**The project is feasible as planned, with a logical dependency chain, a realistic schedule, and an experienced team whose expertise matches the project's demands.** The three-year timeline is ambitious but achievable for the scope of work described, and the critical dependencies—from landscape to prioritization to policy analysis to dissemination—are correctly identified and sequenced.

**Logical dependency structure.** Year 1 concentrates on the landscape analysis and industry interviews, which supply the Delphi panel with its foundational briefing document [1]. Year 2 moves through panel recruitment and preparation, the first two surveys that identify and rank policy questions, and the selection of three priority issues for detailed development [1]. The remaining three surveys, the two-day stakeholder meeting, and the final analyses extend into year 3. Dissemination is staged: a year-two Capitol Hill briefing presents priorities, and a year-three briefing presents policy approaches, both followed by meetings with relevant federal officials [1]. Each handoff—landscape to panel, survey outputs to issue selection, legal analyses to alternative-development surveys, survey findings to the final meeting's verification exercise—is specified [1].

**The timeline is consistent with published precedents.** The 20-month Delphi window for five surveys plus a final meeting is consistent with published precedents. The Boulkedid et al. systematic review of 80 Delphi studies found a median procedure duration of 20 weeks (Q1: 8; Q3: 28) [9], with each round potentially requiring up to eight weeks for questionnaire completion, non-respondent follow-up, analysis, and feedback preparation [9]. Studies in that review typically involved two to three rounds, not five, so a proportionally longer timeline is expected. A directly relevant precedent comes from the Guerrini et al. (2025) modified Policy Delphi on investigative genetic genealogy, in which McGuire served as a co-author: four months passed between rounds 1 and 2 due to the time required for data analysis and detailed survey development, and the authors considered this reasonable given the methodological constraints [12]. With ≈20 months available for five survey rounds and a final meeting, the proposed schedule allocates approximately four months per survey-meeting cycle, which aligns with this operational experience.

**Demonstrated team capability.** The team's capacity to execute the work is supported by demonstrated prior experience. The Genetics and Public Policy Center has conducted landscape analyses of genetic testing companies and used interviews, surveys, focus groups, and discrete-choice methods to examine genetic testing and biobank participation [1]. The investigators have convened a national summit with more than 60 participants from regulatory agencies, professional organizations, industry, and laboratories [1], providing direct evidence of their ability to recruit and manage a large multi-stakeholder event. The four-institution structure maps expertise onto the three policy domains: GPPC on regulatory and oversight questions, Duke (Cook-Deegan) on intellectual property and patents, and CMTP (Tunis, Deverka) on coverage and reimbursement [1]. Messner at GPPC brings specific experience implementing Delphi processes [1], and the eight-member advisory board—meeting five times by video conference—provides guidance across clinical genetics, sequencing technology, regulation, economics, intellectual property, and consumer protection [1].

**Recognized risks with proportionate mitigations.** The proposal acknowledges and addresses the two principal feasibility risks: rapid industry change and panel attrition. The landscape includes ongoing monitoring of new entrants and changes in services [1], and the team will "monitor developments and reassess the research strategy," prioritizing "broader questions that cut across particular technologies" [1]. This is a reasonable mitigation: the regulatory, IP, and reimbursement questions the panel addresses are structural and less vulnerable to particular market entries or exits. Panel attrition is addressed through upfront explanation of the five-survey, two-day meeting, ≈20-month commitment during recruitment [1], and the initial group-building exercise that may foster panel cohesion [1]. The empirical evidence on Delphi attrition provides context: the Boulkedid review found that among studies reporting response rates, the median first-round rate was 90% (Q1: 80%–Q3: 100%) and the median last-round rate was 88% (Q1: 69%–Q3: 96%) [9]. The wide interquartile range on the last round—from 69% to 96%—indicates that substantial attrition is not uncommon. The proposed panel size of ≈40, larger than the median of 17 in comparable studies [9], provides a buffer, and the Policy Delphi's purpose—clarifying options and reasons for disagreement rather than producing statistical consensus [1]—means that the loss of some panelists does not invalidate the exercise. The proposal does not, however, specify a quantitative retention target, an explicit plan for replacing dropouts, or a minimum panel size below which domain coverage would be compromised. These gaps do not undermine feasibility but represent points where additional operational planning would strengthen the robustness of the later survey rounds, which depend on maintaining expertise across the three selected policy issues.

## Ethics and safety

**The ethics and data management plan is thorough, proportionate to the minimal-risk nature of the research, and well-specified across each activity involving human participants.** The project involves two categories of human participation: semi-structured telephone interviews with up to 20 industry leaders (Aim 1), and the five-survey Delphi process with a two-day meeting involving approximately 40 stakeholders (Aim 2) [1]. Neither activity involves experimental intervention, clinical procedures, or collection of biospecimens. The primary risks relate to privacy, confidentiality, and the potential disclosure of commercially or legally sensitive information—risks that the proposal identifies and addresses with a coherent set of procedural safeguards.

**Proportionate consent for professional participants.** The consent and information provisions are appropriately tailored to each activity. For the industry interviews, the consent process explicitly acknowledges the small possibility that commercially or legally sensitive information could be disclosed, including information potentially subject to subpoena [1]. The investigators will request a Certificate of Confidentiality and inform participants about it and its purpose, which is the correct mechanism for protecting research data against compelled disclosure. Interviews and the summit will use verbal consent after advance provision and discussion of study information [1], a proportionate approach for professional participants engaged in policy discussion rather than personal or clinical research. Panel recruitment for the Delphi process explains the five surveys, the two-day meeting, and the approximate 20-month commitment, along with confidentiality arrangements [1], ensuring that participants understand the scope of their involvement before consenting.

**Comprehensive data protection integrated into the research workflow.** Interview recordings are made with two devices, transcribed, and de-identified before analysis [1]. Identifying information is held securely and separately from numbered interview recordings and transcripts [1]. For the Delphi process, each panel member receives a two-letter code, and survey responses are encrypted and password-protected [1]. An initial group-building exercise shares participants' names, affiliations, and expertise so that panel members know the relevant experience represented on the panel, while individual survey responses remain coded [1]. This approach thoughtfully balances two competing ethical commitments: transparency about who is deliberating (so that the basis of the panel's judgments can be assessed) and confidentiality of individual positions (so that participants can express unconventional views without attribution). The summit is closed to outside participants and the press; transcripts are de-identified before analysis; comments are reported in aggregate; and the meeting recording is destroyed after transcription [1]. These measures are appropriate for a setting where participants need to discuss potentially contentious policy positions with commercial and regulatory implications.

**Honest treatment of inclusion and equity.** The recruitment plan does not exclude participants on the basis of sex, race, or ethnicity [1]. The proposal acknowledges transparently that the demographic composition of the professional populations from which participants are drawn—senior executives of sequencing and informatics companies, payer medical directors, clinical laboratory directors, and policy officials—is not sufficiently defined to specify demographic enrollment targets [1]. This is an honest recognition of the structural demographics of these professional sectors rather than an ethical lapse. The three-constituency panel structure (industry, healthcare, and other participants including regulatory agencies and patient groups) [1] ensures that a range of institutional perspectives is represented, including patient group voices in the reimbursement and access discussions, even though the panel is purposively sampled for expertise rather than demographic representativeness.

**Confidential dissemination and appropriate benefit framing.** Public products—the landscape, priorities, policy alternatives, and methodological findings—will present aggregate findings [1]. The proposal correctly notes there is no expected direct benefit to participants beyond ensuring that their perspectives inform policy development [1], which is the appropriate framing for research that engages professionals in their capacity as informed stakeholders. No material risks to communities, animals, the environment, or research operations are present in this study design. The ethics and safety arrangements are proportionate, well-specified, and align with the minimal-risk nature of the research.

## Management

**The management plan is adequate and well-aligned with the project's multi-institutional structure and scientific goals, drawing on demonstrated team expertise and a logical division of responsibilities, though it lacks explicit quantitative targets for panel retention and replacement.**

**Sensible division of leadership and clear handoffs.** Co-leadership by Kaufman and McGuire is sensibly divided: Kaufman oversees the Delphi process and dissemination, McGuire primarily oversees development of policy approaches, and both share responsibility for the landscape analysis [1]. Weekly communication between the co-leaders and regular cross-institution project meetings are specified [1], providing a standard coordination mechanism for a four-institution collaboration. The dependency sequencing—landscape, panel recruitment and preparation, question prioritization, policy development, integration and dissemination—is clearly mapped onto a three-year schedule with specific deliverables at each handoff: the landscape report is supplied to the Delphi panel as background before survey 1 [1], the first two surveys' outputs determine which three issues are selected for detailed policy development [1], and legal analyses prepared by GPPC, Duke, and CMTP inform the alternative-development surveys [1].

**Appropriate advisory structure and integrated dissemination.** The eight-member advisory board, distinct from the 40-person stakeholder panel, is appropriately constituted and scheduled. Its expertise encompasses clinical genetics, sequencing technology, regulation, economics, intellectual property, and consumer protection [1]. Five video-conference meetings are timed to critical decision points: clarifying study goals, discussing the landscape, advising on the selection of three issues, reviewing developing policy approaches, and guiding dissemination and future research [1]. This cadence provides external oversight without burdening the board with operational responsibilities. The dissemination plan is integrated into the research workflow rather than appended: Burness Communications assists with stakeholder identification and events, and the staged Capitol Hill briefings in years two and three ensure that findings reach policy audiences during the project period [1].

**Directly relevant team experience.** The Genetics and Public Policy Center has conducted landscape analyses, used multiple stakeholder engagement methods, and convened a national summit with more than 60 participants [1]. Messner brings specific experience implementing Delphi processes [1]. Most directly relevant, McGuire is a co-author on a recently published modified Policy Delphi on investigative genetic genealogy [12] that engaged 31 participants in a four-round process using purposive sampling, iterative online surveys, and an explicit framing of identifying policy options and reasons rather than achieving consensus [12]—design features that closely parallel the proposed project. The four-month interval between rounds 1 and 2 in that study provides a realistic operational benchmark for the proposed ≈20-month, five-survey timeline [12].

**Panel retention targets remain unspecified.** The principal gap is the absence of quantitative operational targets for the Delphi panel. The proposal explains the commitment to panelists upfront and uses an initial group-building exercise to foster cohesion [1], and the ≈40 panel size exceeds the median of 17 in comparable studies [9], providing a buffer against attrition. However, empirical evidence shows that last-round response rates in Delphi studies range from 69% to 96% [9], indicating that substantial attrition is not uncommon. The plan does not specify a minimum acceptable retention rate, an explicit protocol for replacing dropouts, or a minimum panel size below which domain coverage would be compromised. These are specifications that would strengthen operational robustness in the later survey rounds, where maintaining expertise across the three selected policy domains becomes critical. They do not, however, undermine the fundamental feasibility of the project, given the team's demonstrated experience, the logical dependency structure, and the Policy Delphi's purpose of clarifying options and disagreement rather than producing statistical consensus.

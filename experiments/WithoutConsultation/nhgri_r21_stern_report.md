# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project proposes to conduct the first systematic, population-based analysis of the demographic patterns of eugenic sterilization in California. By digitizing and coding approximately 15,000 institutional sterilization recommendation records from 1921–1952 and linking them to census microdata, the team will estimate rates of sterilization recommendation and test associations with ethnicity (identified via Spanish surname), gender, age, nativity, and institutional context. The work is designed to distinguish disproportionate institutionalization from disproportionate selection for sterilization within institutions. A parallel qualitative analysis will examine consent documents and patient correspondence to understand how coercion operated and was resisted. The most valuable expected knowledge is a rigorous, quantitative account of the disparities in a major state-sponsored program of reproductive control, combined with an interpretive historical analysis of the lived experience within that system.

The proposal is supported by a strong team combining historical and epidemiological expertise, a well-justified research question, and substantial preliminary work including completed digitization, database construction, and a successful pilot analysis showing marked disparities. The quantitative plan to link recommendation records with census denominators to estimate relative rates is feasible and the designated statistical methods (Poisson regression) are appropriate for the core question. Published work by the same team subsequently confirmed the productivity of this approach. The main limitations are: (1) the quantitative design is not a true case-cohort study and cannot estimate absolute risks; it relies on untested assumptions for intercensal population interpolation and cannot simultaneously adjust for key confounders like age and institution due to data sparsity, a limitation the proposal does not address; and (2) the qualitative methodology for Aim 3 is substantially underspecified, lacking a defined analytical framework or procedure for integrating qualitative and quantitative findings, which limits confidence that its interpretive claims can be systematically supported.

This is a well-motivated project that addresses questions of profound historical and contemporary importance with a creative combination of epidemiological and historical methods. The pilot work is compelling, and the data access and team expertise are genuine strengths. When weighed, the project's substantial scientific value and the feasibility of its core quantitative aims—both strongly supported by published outcomes—outweigh the methodological limitations in design and the under-specification of the qualitative plan. These are real but manageable weaknesses that should be addressed to ensure that the historical interpretations are as rigorous as the statistical ones. The current plan presents a worthwhile direction with solvable problems.

## Scientific value

**Scientific value is good, reflecting a question of profound historical and contemporary importance that the project is well-positioned to answer in its core dimensions.** The California eugenic sterilization program was the largest in the United States, accounting for approximately 20,000 of the roughly 60,000 documented procedures nationwide [1]. The broad outlines of this history are known, but the proposal correctly identifies a genuine gap: "detailed empirical accounts of the populations affected remain limited" and no prior work had combined systematic quantitative analysis of the full corpus of individual recommendation records with census-based population denominators to estimate rates and test demographic associations across multiple institutions and time periods [1]. Filling this gap would matter not only for historical understanding but for contemporary ethical, legal, and social implications (ELSI) discussions in genetics and public health, where patterns of reproductive coercion against racialized and vulnerable groups continue to resonate [1].

The project's attainable knowledge falls into three connected tiers. First, creation of the analytic infrastructure: digitizing approximately 15,000 sterilization recommendation records, each coded for 212 variables covering demographics, diagnosis, family structure, and institutional allegations, into a HIPAA-compliant REDCap database [1]. This dataset, covering nine institutions from 1921–1952, would itself be an enduring scholarly resource regardless of any particular analysis, and it represents approximately one quarter of all documented US eugenic sterilizations [1].

Second, population-based rate estimation: by constructing institutional population denominators from census microdata for 1920, 1930, and 1940—anticipating information on more than 100,000 residents of sterilizing institutions—the project can move decisively beyond simple frequency counts [1]. The design permits estimation of whether Spanish-surnamed people, women, those under age 18, and immigrants had higher risks of sterilization recommendation than comparison groups, and it distinguishes disproportionate institutionalization from disproportionate selection for sterilization within institutions [1]. The Pacific Colony pilot analysis demonstrates the productivity of this approach, showing elevated adjusted odds ratios for Spanish-surnamed patients (2.07, 95% CI 1.65–2.60) and those under age 18 (3.21, 95% CI 2.67–3.86) compared with the institutional population [1].

Third, qualitative analysis of consent and resistance: the proposal identifies a distinctive corpus of correspondence, protest letters, and non-consent forms—including approximately 50 handwritten patient letters from Patton State Hospital containing objections to their own sterilization—that can illuminate how consent operated in practice when the law did not require it, how families contested the procedure, and how institutional language changed over time [1]. This tier promises an interpretive depth that quantitative rate estimation cannot supply.

The published record from this same investigative team confirms that the combination is productive. The 2018 paper "Disproportionate Sterilization of Latinos Under California's Eugenic Sterilization Program, 1920–1945" demonstrated that Latino men faced 23% higher sterilization rates than non-Latino men (IRR 1.23, 95% CI 1.15–1.31) and Latina women 59% higher rates (IRR 1.59, 95% CI 1.48–1.70) [2]. The 2023 paper extended this to Asian immigrants, finding IRR of 2.00 for Asian-born women compared with US-born women [3]. These outputs demonstrate that the proposed census-linked rate estimation has generated knowledge—about disproportionate sterilization of specific racialized groups, about institutional and temporal variation, and about the scale of the program—that did not exist before this research program. The qualitative contribution remains less fully realized in the published trace, but the research questions it addresses—the meaning of consent under coercive institutional conditions—are central to the project's historical and ethical significance.

## Innovation

**Innovation is good, residing in the unprecedented assembly and linkage of archival data sources rather than in novel theory or method.** The proposal does not advance a new theoretical framework, statistical technique, or causal model. Its originality is infrastructural and empirical: it creates, for the first time, a comprehensive, analyzable dataset from California's surviving sterilization recommendation records and connects it to census microdata in a way that enables population-based estimation of sterilization disparities. This combination—the systematic digitization of approximately 15,000 individual records from nine institutions, each coded for 212 variables, and their linkage with decennial census enumerations of institutional residents and catchment populations—had no clear precedent in the historiography of American eugenics at the time of submission [1].

### Prior work and the gap it leaves

Prior scholarship had established the broad contours of California's program and provided qualitative or narrative accounts of particular institutions or groups. Stern's own 2005 monograph *Eugenic Nation*, Braslow's *Mental Ills and Bodily Cures* (1997), the Bashford and Levine *Oxford Handbook of the History of Eugenics* (2010), and Wellerstein's 2011 chapter on California each contributed historical analysis with varying amounts of quantitative description [1]. Schoen's *Choice and Coercion* (2005) and Dyck's *Facing Eugenics* (2013) examined related programs in North Carolina and Canada through qualitative and historical lenses. Chávez-García's *States of Delinquency* (2012) analyzed race and science in California's juvenile justice system. The Lira and Stern (2014) article in *Aztlán* examined Mexican-American resistance to sterilization but was a preliminary qualitative analysis. None of these works combined the full corpus of individual recommendation records with census-based denominators to estimate rates and test demographic associations across multiple institutions and time periods.

### Three levels of addition

The innovation operates at three levels. First is the construction of the dataset itself: abstracting 212 variables from heterogeneous historical records spanning 1921–1952, including demographic characteristics, diagnostic classifications, family circumstances, and institutional allegations about criminality and sexual behavior [1]. This resource, once completed, becomes available for subsequent analyses beyond the immediate aims.

Second is the census-linkage strategy: using individual-level microdata from 1920, 1930, and 1940 to reconstruct institutional populations and, separately, catchment-area populations as denominators for rate estimation [1]. This permits the project to address a question that simple description of recommendations cannot answer—whether particular groups were disproportionately institutionalized, disproportionately selected for sterilization within institutions, or both [1]. The Pacific Colony pilot demonstrated this move from description to rate comparison, showing that Spanish-surnamed residents constituted 29.2% of recommendations but only 14.6% of the institutional population (unadjusted OR 2.41) [1].

Third is the qualitative complement: systematic analysis of consent documents, refusal correspondence, and patient protest letters that can reveal how coercion operated and was contested in practice [1]. The proposal's framing of consent as a practice embedded in specific institutional arrangements—signature-seeking from family members, conditional release, and the absence of legal requirement—provides a productive analytical distinction between a signature, acquiescence, refusal, and meaningful choice [1].

The published record confirms the productivity of this combination. Novak et al. (2018) used 17,362 forms with census-linked denominators and Spanish-surname identification to produce the first population-based sterilization rate estimates for Latinos in California [4]. Kaniecki et al. (2023) extended the same infrastructure to Asian immigrants, documenting disproportionate sterilization of Asian-born women (IRR 2.00) and time-varying risk for Asian-born men [3]. These findings, disseminated in high-impact journals, represent knowledge that could not have been produced without the infrastructural innovation of dataset construction and census linkage. The innovation is incremental rather than disruptive—it extends established historical and epidemiological methods to a new, rich corpus—but the increment is large and consequential.

## Rigor

**Rigor is moderate: the quantitative design is largely sound for the core within-institution rate comparisons, but it has important structural limitations and unexamined assumptions that constrain its inferential scope, while the qualitative methodology is substantially underspecified for the interpretive claims it is meant to support.** The proposal combines two distinct methodological traditions—historical epidemiology and qualitative historical analysis—and their integration is more gestured at than operationalized.

### Quantitative design: strengths and structural constraints

The quantitative Aims 1 and 2 rest on a solid foundation. The REDCap instrument, developed during the preliminary phase, captures 212 variables from approximately 15,000 recommendation records across nine institutions [1]. The Pacific Colony pilot analysis demonstrates that the approach can detect meaningful disparities: adjusted logistic regression on 2,071 observations yielded odds ratios of 2.07 for Spanish surname and 3.21 for age under 18, both statistically significant at p\<0.001 [1]. The plan to apply consistent Spanish-surname identification across recommendation and census records, using standardized Census Bureau lists with manual review for misspellings, is methodologically defensible [1]. The published validation literature supports acceptable sensitivity and specificity for this population: in 1950, 88% of Spanish-surnamed individuals in California were of Mexican descent [2]. The known limitations of the surname method—gender-differential misclassification due to intermarriage, temporal mismatch between late-20th-century surname lists and early-20th-century populations—are unlikely to reverse the direction of the large disparities observed in pilot data, and they apply with comparable force across the data sources being compared.

However, three structural limitations constrain the inferences the quantitative design can support, and the proposal does not adequately address them.

**The design is not a formal case-cohort study.** The proposal describes a "case-cohort design" with Poisson regression [1], but what is actually proposed is a historical rate comparison using decennial census snapshots as denominators without individual-level follow-up or person-time. A formal case-cohort design requires a random subcohort sampled from a defined baseline cohort, with all cohort members followed for events. The approach here treats census headcounts as the offset representing person-time, which requires the steady-state assumption that institutional populations remain approximately constant between census years. The published work from this team explicitly relied on linear interpolation between census years to generate intercensal denominators, and extended 1940 counts forward to 1945 [3]. The proposal's study period extends through 1952—twelve years past the last individual-level census—yet it does not discuss the uncertainty this introduces.

**The design cannot simultaneously adjust for key confounders.** The published Kaniecki et al. (2023) paper, which used identical methods, acknowledged that "we could not adjust models for age and institution because small cell counts prevented reliable interpolation between census years when incorporating additional variables" and that "statistically significant demographic differences between nativity groups and the population recommended for sterilization versus the institutionalized population suggest age and institution influence sterilization patterns, confounding the results" [3]. The proposal's Aim 2 promises to examine variation "within and across institutions" [1], but stratifying the denominator simultaneously by institution, age group, gender, and surname or ethnicity will produce many empty or sparse cells, making interpolation unreliable and Poisson models unstable. The proposal is silent on how it will overcome this limitation.

**Temporal coverage has a substantial gap after 1940.** For the period 1941–1952—which contains a substantial fraction of all California sterilizations—no individual-level census microdata exist; only tract-level 1950 reports are available [1]. The rate estimates for the later period will therefore be substantially more uncertain. Annual institutional reports can supplement population estimates, but these typically provide aggregate counts rather than the individual-level demographic cross-tabulations needed for stratified Poisson models [1].

What the design can validly estimate is relative rates of sterilization recommendation comparing demographic groups among institutional residents, provided the interpolation assumptions and confounding limitations are transparently reported. It cannot estimate absolute risks, population-based rates, or causally separated effects of institutionalization versus sterilization selection. The proposal's primary value may lie in its descriptive and historical contribution rather than in precise causal estimation—a contribution the qualitative and archival components substantially support.

### Qualitative methodology: important questions without operational specification

The qualitative Aim 3 addresses questions of genuine importance: how consent was requested and refused, how institutional language changed over time, and how patients and families contested sterilization. The corpus—including all documented refusal cases and approximately 50 handwritten patient letters from Patton State Hospital—is distinctive and valuable [1]. The investigators appropriately commit to preserving "the distinction between institutional claims about a person and that person's own responses" [1], which is the correct analytical posture for documents produced within asymmetric power relationships.

However, the methodology is described in only four paragraphs that identify *what* will be examined but not *how* [1]. Several substantial gaps remain unaddressed.

**No analytical framework is specified.** The proposal invokes "coding" and "themes" without indicating whether the approach draws on grounded theory, thematic analysis, qualitative content analysis, discourse analysis, or historical-critical methods—each of which carries distinct assumptions about how meaning is generated from texts and what kind of knowledge claims can be made.

**No coding procedures are described.** Qualitative research convention typically requires some account of whether coding will be inductive or deductive, how a coding scheme will be developed, whether multiple coders will be used and how reliability or interpretive consensus will be assessed, and how negative or disconfirming cases will be handled. None of these is addressed. The statement that letters will be "manually coded to identify themes" [1] is a description of activity rather than a specification of method.

**The integration strategy is stated but not operationalized.** The proposal envisions that "numerical associations and close reading of the documents will jointly support interpretation, rather than reduce all records to demographic counts" [1]. This is a defensible epistemological stance, but it leaves entirely open how integration will occur in practice. Will quantitative patterns guide the selection of cases for qualitative analysis? Will qualitative themes generate hypotheses tested in the quantitative data? Mixed-methods research requires explicit integration procedures to avoid the two strands simply confirming each other through selective presentation.

These limitations do not negate the project's value. The quantitative work can validly estimate relative disparities among institutional residents, and the published record confirms that meaningful findings emerged. The qualitative component identifies important questions and promising materials. But the proposal claims more than its quantitative design can deliver without acknowledging the constraints, and the qualitative component requires substantially more methodological specification to support its interpretive ambitions.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility is good for the core quantitative aims but carries schedule risk at the intersection of census linkage and qualitative analysis, where staffing and methodological specification are thinnest.** The 24-month plan can deliver the principal descriptive statistics and Poisson regression models for the primary demographic comparisons; the critical dependencies are data entry completion and census dataset construction, both of which rest on substantial preliminary work already accomplished.

### Critical dependencies and their status

The most important gate—completion of data entry for approximately 15,000 records, each with 212 variables, into the REDCap database—is assigned to a preparatory phase ending by summer 2015 [1]. The proposal reports that digitization has occurred, the REDCap instrument has been developed, and pilot studies have already used subsets of the data [1]. These pilot outputs demonstrate not only that the infrastructure exists but that the team has successfully navigated the full analytical pipeline from historical record to rate estimate. Data entry completion before the analytical phase is assumed rather than documented in staffing detail, which introduces a residual uncertainty, but the evident progress makes this a manageable rather than high-risk dependency.

Census companion dataset construction—identifying residents of nine institutions within complete-count microdata for 1920, 1930, and 1940—is placed in Year 1 alongside descriptive statistics and initial hypothesis testing [1]. The team anticipates census information on more than 100,000 institutional residents over the study period [1], and access to microdata through the University of Michigan's Institute for Social Research provides a concrete institutional advantage [1]. The subsequent tasks—intercensal interpolation, cross-walking of racial and ethnic categories across census years [1], and construction of tract-level denominators for 1950—add complexity but are standard operations in historical demography.

### Schedule risk at the Aim 2–Aim 3 interface

The schedule envisions Year 1 producing the census dataset, descriptive statistics, initial hypothesis tests, and beginning qualitative coding simultaneously; Year 2 expands hypothesis testing, examines inter-institutional differences, develops analysis of refusals and consent, and prepares publications—all while dissemination is ongoing [1]. The qualitative work—manual coding of refusal letters, analysis of approximately 50 handwritten patient appeals, and tracing of diagnostic terminology changes—competes for investigator attention with the quantitative modeling [1].

The proposal states that quantitative and qualitative work will proceed in parallel [1], which is methodologically desirable but assumes sufficient personnel. The implementation section identifies three named investigators—Stern, Kardia, and Harlow—but does not detail the allocation of research assistants or a dedicated qualitative analyst beyond mention of a project manager for data sharing [1]. The simultaneous pursuit of Poisson modeling, qualitative coding, and visualization production may strain available effort unless substantial staff support exists outside the grant.

The companion archive (Aim 4) is correctly positioned as a parallel project with separate humanities support [1], which limits its claim on R21 resources and reduces the risk that it diverts effort from the analytical aims. The most likely outcome if schedule pressures materialize is that Aim 3 is reduced to a smaller set of illustrative cases and Aim 2 narrowed to its primary ethnic and gender comparisons. This would still constitute a meaningful and publishable contribution, as the team's subsequent publication record in high-impact journals confirms. The 24-month plan can deliver the principal quantitative findings. The main uncertainty—whether qualitative analysis can be completed to an equivalent standard within the same timeline—reflects the methodological under-specification noted under Rigor rather than a prohibitive resource constraint.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety are good, supported by a well-structured data protection plan, dual IRB approval, and explicit commitments to confidentiality, though the proposal does not fully address the ethical dimensions of working with records of a vulnerable, historically wronged population.** The project involves retrospective research on sensitive historical records without direct contact with the people represented; the ethical obligations center on protecting the information of those subjected to coercive state sterilization and on responsible stewardship of data that document a profound injustice.

### Data protection measures

The proposal describes a layered approach to data protection. The analytical dataset will be de-identified, with the identifier used during data entry excluded from exported data and reports [1]. The source files—digitized authorizations, correspondence, and forms—will be accessed through a password-protected server and stored on a password-protected drive kept in a locked safe [1]. Data extraction uses HIPAA-compliant REDCap instruments, and coded data remain on protected computers [1]. Any transport of records will use a secure carrier [1]. These are appropriate and proportionate measures for records that contain identifiable information about individuals who include adults and children, with recorded ages spanning approximately 7–58, and people with intellectual or psychiatric diagnoses [1].

### Institutional oversight

The project has received institutional review board approval from both the California Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects and the University of Michigan biomedical IRB [1]. This dual approval, covering the state that holds the records and the institution conducting the research, is a meaningful safeguard. The proposal specifies that team members must be included on the IRB protocol and complete research-responsibility, confidentiality, and REDCap training before accessing data [1]. These are standard but appropriate access controls for sensitive historical data.

### Dissemination and data sharing

The dissemination plan explicitly commits to not disclosing individual identification through research outputs [1]. The companion digital archive (Aim 4), being developed with digital-humanities collaborators using Omeka, will incorporate findings and visualizations subject to these confidentiality requirements [1]. Publications and presentations to public-health, history, ethics, and ELSI audiences will follow the same constraints [1].

The resource-sharing plan provides for release of suitable data after acceptance of the principal findings, with identifiers removed and disclosure risk minimized, using institutional data-use agreements as appropriate [1]. This commitment to sharing is appropriately subject to the privacy restrictions on the historical records. The plan identifies a project manager to coordinate and document requests and distributions [1], which provides a mechanism for accountable data stewardship.

### Underdeveloped dimensions

A gap in the proposal is the absence of explicit discussion of ethical obligations to descendant communities or the potential for harm through public dissemination of sensitive historical information about a racialized and vulnerable population. The people whose records form the study corpus were subjected to a coercive state program that targeted them on the basis of disability, ethnicity, gender, and institutional status. Their descendants may have legitimate interests in how these records are used, interpreted, and made public. The proposal's ELSI framing—situating the research in relation to contemporary ethical, legal, and social questions in genetics [1]—suggests awareness of these dimensions, but the ethics section focuses narrowly on data security and IRB compliance. A more thorough consideration of community engagement, the ethics of public history, or the potential for re-traumatization through dissemination would strengthen the plan, particularly given that the companion archive aims to reach public audiences [1]. This gap does not constitute a material deficiency in data protection—the protocols described are appropriate and proportionate for the direct risks of data breach or re-identification—but it represents an underdeveloped dimension of the project's ethical framework that could be addressed without altering the research design.

## Management

**Management is good: the combined expertise of the investigative team and the logical phasing of the work support delivery of the core quantitative aims, though the proposal is thin on staffing allocation and the mechanisms for coordinating the quantitative and qualitative streams.** The project draws on established institutional resources, a completed preparatory phase, and a 24-month schedule that correctly sequences its major tasks.

### Team expertise

The investigative team combines the three kinds of expertise the project requires. Stern, the principal investigator, contributes deep historical knowledge of California eugenics, including the institutional landscape, the archival sources, and the historiographical context; her 2005 monograph *Eugenic Nation* and prior work on Mexican-American resistance to sterilization establish her command of the subject [1]. Kardia and Harlow contribute epidemiologic and quantitative expertise, including experience with population-based rate estimation, Poisson regression, and the design of studies using linked administrative and census data [1]. This combination—a historian who understands the records and an epidemiology team that can construct denominators and model rates—is precisely what the project's dual quantitative–qualitative design requires. The team has already collaborated successfully on the preliminary phase, developing the REDCap instrument, conducting the Pacific Colony pilot analysis, and publishing preliminary qualitative work [1].

### Institutional resources and schedule

The project leverages concrete institutional infrastructure at the University of Michigan. The data-entry environment, secure computing, and HIPAA-compliant REDCap platform are housed in the School of Public Health [1]. Census microdata access is provided through the Institute for Social Research [1]. The existing IRB approvals, defined protocols for data access, training, and secure storage reduce the administrative burden during the analytical phase [1].

The 24-month schedule is logically phased. The preparatory phase—data entry completion by summer 2015—separates the labor-intensive abstraction work from the analytical work funded by the grant [1]. Year 1 focuses on building the census companion dataset, producing descriptive statistics, beginning population-based analyses and hypothesis tests, and initiating qualitative coding [1]. Year 2 expands hypothesis testing, examines inter-institutional differences, develops the analysis of refusals and consent, and prepares publications and public-facing materials [1]. The plan for parallel quantitative and qualitative work, with emerging findings informing contextual investigation in both directions, is methodologically sound [1]. The companion archive (Aim 4) is correctly positioned as a separate project with parallel humanities support [1].

### Staffing specification

The principal weakness in management specification is staffing. The proposal identifies three named investigators and mentions a project manager responsible for coordinating data-sharing requests [1], but it does not detail the allocation of research assistants, graduate students, or a dedicated qualitative analyst.

| Role | Named in proposal | Comment |

| Historical expertise, qualitative analysis | Stern [1] | Demonstrated; qualitative methodology unspecified |
| Epidemiologic and quantitative design | Kardia, Harlow [1] | Appropriate; pilot analysis confirms capability |

| Qualitative coding (manual, all refusal cases) | Not specified | Labor-intensive; no dedicated analyst identified |
| Project coordination, data sharing | Project manager mentioned [1] | Responsibilities limited to data sharing in text |

These gaps do not prevent delivery of the core quantitative aims. The pilot analysis demonstrates that the team can execute the full analytical pipeline from REDCap data to adjusted rate estimates. But the proposal would be stronger with a clearer account of how the qualitative coding will be staffed and how the two analytical streams will be coordinated beyond the general statement that they will proceed in parallel. The risk is that the qualitative work, already under-specified in method, is also under-resourced in practice, and is consequently reduced to a smaller set of illustrative cases. This would still produce a meaningful contribution, but it would fall short of the systematic analysis of consent and resistance the proposal promises.

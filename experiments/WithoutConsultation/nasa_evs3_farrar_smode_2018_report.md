# Project review

## Overall assessment

S-MODE proposes a coordinated airborne and oceanographic investigation off the coast of San Francisco to test the hypothesis that submesoscale ocean dynamics, specifically kilometer-scale fronts and eddies, make an important contribution to the vertical exchange of heat, carbon, and biological variables. The project uniquely combines daily DopplerScatt maps of surface currents and winds with coincident airborne measurements of ocean color (PRISM), sea surface temperature (MOSES), and directional wave spectra (MASS). This synoptic remote sensing is tightly integrated with a nested *in situ* array of a research vessel, Lagrangian floats, Wave Gliders, Seagliders, and drifters over two 25-day intensive periods in contrasting seasons. The most valuable expected knowledge is a first-of-its-kind benchmark dataset that can directly differentiate between conflicting high-resolution model predictions, characterize the 3D structure of submesoscale features and their associated vertical velocities, and develop an exploratory pathway for estimating vertical flux from future remote sensing missions.

The project is exceptionally well-supported by the proven track record of the instruments and the scientific leadership team. All key airborne sensors have been demonstrated in prior campaigns, and the *in situ* platforms are standard tools expertly deployed by the PIs in earlier submesoscale studies like LatMix and the pioneering D’Asaro et al. (2018) frontal subduction experiment. The proposal’s science traceability matrix provides a clear and logical link from instrument performance to science goals. The primary limitations, identified by expert analysis, are operational and conceptual. The Wave Glider formation for the critical 1 km divergence array faces station-keeping challenges not fully addressed in the operations plan, which could weaken one branch of the vertical velocity cross-validation. Furthermore, the flux proxy plan (Goal 4) is correctly identified as exploratory; its validation strategy is underspecified, particularly concerning the vertical length scale *h*, which currently prevents a high-confidence path to a validated proxy within the proposal’s scope. These are well-recognized risks in cutting-edge fieldwork that bound, but do not undermine, the core value of the proposed measurements.

The proposal’s great strengths are its scientific importance, genuine observational innovation, and thorough planning by a world-leading team. The most valuable expected contribution—a unique multi-season, multi-variable benchmark dataset to test model physics—is strongly supported by the demonstrated instrument capabilities and the well-structured nested sampling design. The main trade-off concerns the exploratory flux proxy in Goal 4: its validation pathway is a sketch, not a protocol, and this limits the certainty of achieving a definitive, validated proxy within the project’s scope. This is not a fatal flaw, as the proposal explicitly identifies this goal as exploratory and the threshold mission, focused on the 3D structure and the surface-to-subsurface velocity relationship, is robust. On balance, the project’s foundational goals are secure and the potential for transformative insights into submesoscale dynamics is high. **The current plan provides a compelling and proportionate route to a suite of valuable, benchmark-quality results.**

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**The question S-MODE addresses sits at a recognised frontier in physical oceanography, and the attainable knowledge would constitute a substantive advance.** The ocean surface boundary layer mediates exchanges of heat, carbon, nutrients, and momentum with the atmosphere, yet the contribution of submesoscale (0.2–25 km) fronts and eddies to vertical transport remains a critical uncertainty. The proposal’s own scientific background identifies the core difficulty: at grid spacings approaching 100 m, explicitly simulated submesoscale structures overlap the scales represented by turbulence parameterizations, and existing models only partially represent this coupling [1]. Simulations of the California Current system from ROMS and MITgcm both generate submesoscale features yet differ systematically in the relative prominence of eddies versus fronts and in the seasonal contrast between spring and autumn [1]. Su et al. (2018) subsequently showed that resolving submesoscales alters annual-mean air–sea heat flux by 4–10 W m⁻², enough to change sea surface temperature by up to 0.3 °C [2]. Dong et al. (2024) demonstrated that geostrophic shear production at fronts contributes approximately 34% of total turbulent dissipation in the winter boundary layer and roughly 17% in summer—a source entirely absent from conventional boundary-layer schemes [3]. These are not marginal corrections to existing understanding; they indicate fundamental deficiencies in how the ocean boundary layer is represented in the climate models that inform projections of future warming and carbon uptake.

**The project’s primary deliverable is a benchmark observational dataset whose descriptive value is secure and whose comparative value for model discrimination is high.** No existing dataset simultaneously maps surface currents, sea surface temperature, ocean colour, and directional wave spectra from aircraft while measuring three-dimensional density, velocity, and direct vertical velocity from a nested *in situ* array—let alone across multiple frontal features in two contrasting seasons. The Shcherbina et al. (2013) LatMix experiment provided the first *in situ* estimates of the full velocity gradient tensor at approximately 1 km scale, but as a single-season snapshot from two ships with no airborne context, it could not address seasonal modulation, air–sea interaction, or biogeochemical coupling [4]. The OSMOSIS mooring array revealed a seasonal cycle in submesoscale vorticity skewness but was limited to Eulerian point measurements [5]. The D’Asaro et al. (2018) experiment produced a compelling single-feature demonstration of frontal subduction—one Lagrangian float measuring vertical velocities of 1–2 cm s⁻¹—but lacked the synoptic spatial mapping needed to place that measurement in a statistical ensemble [6]. S-MODE advances beyond each of these: the baseline mission targets approximately six feature realisations per season with the full instrument suite, moving the field from single-realisation existence proofs toward a systematic characterisation of submesoscale structure and its seasonal dependence [1].

**The practical and conceptual contributions extend well beyond the immediate study region.** The benchmark dataset is explicitly designed to discriminate between competing high-resolution model predictions—the proposal states that “the intended product is a benchmark observational data set that can discriminate between high-resolution model predictions and improve the interpretation of surface measurements from aircraft and satellites” [1]. This ambition is supported by the measurement capability: the published S-MODE results from Torres et al. (2024), using DopplerScatt data from the campaign, already reveal that velocity gradients at 2 km scale reach root-mean-square values of order *f* (the local Coriolis parameter), that the magnitude of divergence and its associated vertical velocities are roughly 2.5 times larger than those produced by a 500 m numerical model, and that the modelled submesoscale structures exhibit principally elongated patterns that differ from the dipole structures revealed by the observations [7]. This is precisely the kind of model-discriminating evidence the project was designed to produce. Goal 4’s attempt to relate remotely sensed surface velocity divergence and tracers to vertical flux—while appropriately labelled “the most exploratory goal” [1]—carries the longer-term promise of a framework for inferring vertical transport from future satellite missions, which remains a major gap in the global observing system. The threshold mission, focused on quantifying three-dimensional submesoscale structures and relating surface radar currents to boundary-layer currents, provides a well-defined and independently valuable fallback that does not depend on the success of the flux proxy development [1].

## Innovation

**S-MODE’s observational design is genuinely unprecedented and represents a qualitative advance over all prior submesoscale field campaigns.** The innovation lies not in a single new instrument or a wholly new theory, but in the deliberate, simultaneous combination of mature measurement techniques into a cohesive, multi-platform system that addresses questions no previous experiment could answer. Prior campaigns have demonstrated individual pieces—the two-ship ADCP gradient survey, the Lagrangian float following subduction, the airborne Doppler current map—but S-MODE integrates them with a completeness and synopticity that transforms the scientific reach of the dataset.

The following table locates the innovation precisely against the three most relevant prior experiments:

| Capability | LatMix 2013 | D’Asaro et al. 2018 | OSMOSIS 2016 | S-MODE (proposed baseline) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Airborne surface current mapping (synoptic, \~1 km) | None | None | None | DopplerScatt, daily, \~100 km domain |
| Coincident airborne ocean colour, SST, waves | None | None | None | PRISM + MOSES + MASS, ≤1-hour coincidence |
| Direct vertical velocity (Lagrangian float) | None | One float | None | Multiple floats per IOP |
| Gradient array for divergence (w estimate) | Two ships, 1 km separation, single survey | None | None | Four Wave Gliders, \~1 km square, sustained |
| Mesoscale context (gliders, maps) | None (single transect) | None | Eulerian moorings, gliders | Seaglider surveys, altimetry, broad DopplerScatt |
| Seasonal contrast (identical instruments) | Winter only | Single event | One year, fixed moorings | Spring and autumn IOPs, full suite |
| Biogeochemical flux exploration | None | None | None | Goal 4 W\_D proxy, POC/Chl, water samples |

Three aspects of this integration merit particular emphasis. **First, the airborne component alone is a first**: no prior campaign has simultaneously mapped surface currents (DopplerScatt), hyperspectral ocean colour and POC (PRISM), sub-pixel SST (MOSES), and directional wave spectra with wave-breaking statistics (MASS) from coordinated aircraft within a one-hour coincidence window [1]. The Torres et al. (2024) published results confirm that this combination reveals dynamical signatures—dipole structures of vorticity and divergence between interacting eddies, cross-scale kinetic energy transfers of order 2 × 10⁻⁷ m² s⁻³—that are simply invisible to *in situ* measurements alone and that differ qualitatively from the elongated structures produced by current numerical models [7].

**Second, the nested in situ design provides two independent and complementary estimates of vertical velocity within the same feature-scale context.** The Lagrangian float measures the vertical motion of a specific water parcel with approximately 1 mm s⁻¹ accuracy; the four-Wave Glider divergence array integrates horizontal divergence to estimate areal-mean vertical velocity over a \~1 km footprint [1]. D’Asaro et al. (2018) demonstrated the float method but could not compare it with an independent technique [6]; LatMix estimated divergence but had no direct vertical velocity measurement [4]. The two methods sense different spatial filters—the float captures concentrated subduction at a front while the array averages over a kilometre—so their comparison is complementary rather than strictly cross-validating, but this contrast is itself scientifically valuable: agreement would indicate that vertical velocity is coherent over kilometre scales, while systematic differences would quantify the spatial heterogeneity of submesoscale exchange. The full implications of this complementary design are examined under Rigor below.

**Third, Goal 4’s flux proxy concept, while exploratory, is genuinely original in its observational ambition.** Attempting to relate the covariance of DopplerScatt surface divergence and remotely sensed tracers (SST, chlorophyll, POC) to vertical flux has no precedent in a field campaign. The proposal states that “this is the most exploratory goal” and that “complete flux quantification is difficult, but even partial progress would advance understanding of vertical transport” [1]. The concept is physically grounded in continuity and is timely given the planned SWOT and future Doppler-scatterometer satellite missions. Even partial validation of the scaling hypothesis W\_D = D·h, and a characterisation of its limitations, would influence how the next generation of satellite ocean-current measurements is interpreted—a practical pathway that extends well beyond the immediate study region.

## Rigor

**The design logic connecting measurements to science goals is carefully articulated and largely appropriate, but the proposal’s validation pathway for its most ambitious aim—the flux proxy in Goal 4—contains structural gaps that the proposal itself partially acknowledges. These gaps are consistent with an exploratory goal at the current stage, but they limit the certainty with which specific inferences can be drawn from the dataset within the project’s scope.**

### Traceability matrix and nested sampling

The matrix maps each observable to the required space–time sampling, accuracy, and the demonstrated performance of the assigned instrument [1]. This is not a cosmetic exercise: it shows, for example, that DopplerScatt’s 0.0086 m s⁻¹ precision over 2 km amply meets the 0.08 m s⁻¹ requirement for gradient estimation, that PRISM’s ±30% chlorophyll uncertainty over 0.01–10 mg m⁻³ matches the 0.05 mg m⁻³ discrimination target, and that the Wave Glider ADCP configuration targets the divergence precision of 2 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹ needed to resolve vertical motion against a typical submesoscale signal of order 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹ [1]. The nested sampling—DopplerScatt guiding the ship, the ship surveying around a Lagrangian float, Wave Gliders maintaining a gradient array within the survey, Seagliders providing the surrounding mesoscale context—is a logical and efficient solution to the problem of observing features that evolve on inertial time scales (\~20 hours at the site latitude) while requiring kilometre-scale spatial resolution [1].

External evidence strengthens confidence in the gradient-measurement approach. The Shcherbina et al. (2013) LatMix experiment demonstrated that synchronous two-ship ADCP measurements at 1 km separation can capture the full velocity gradient tensor, with observed divergence standard deviations of approximately 0.47*f* in the mixed layer [4]. The Bhuyan et al. (2024) analysis of S-MODE Saildrone data subsequently confirmed that velocity gradients at 2 km scale can be obtained with approximately 0.1*f* uncertainty using four platforms in formation [8]. However, an important qualification applies to the Wave Glider component: the four Wave Gliders in a nominal 1 km square [1] face station-keeping challenges not addressed in the operations plan. Wave Gliders are wave-propelled at approximately 1–2 knots and cannot actively hold position; their formation geometry will evolve, affecting the conditioning of the gradient fit. The Bhuyan et al. study explicitly addressed this by discarding formations with aspect ratio below 0.2, but this required active piloting of Saildrones and yielded only four frontal crossings in 36 hours of maintained quad formation [8]. The proposal references neither these controls nor a comparable Wave Glider protocol, leaving an unresolved operational risk to one of the two independent vertical-velocity estimates. The implications for feasibility are discussed in the corresponding section below.

### Vertical-velocity complementary design

The vertical-velocity cross-comparison itself is well-conceived as a complementary rather than a cross-validating measurement. The Lagrangian float measures the vertical velocity of a specific water parcel; the Wave Glider divergence array estimates areal-mean vertical velocity over a kilometre footprint. Johnson et al. (2020), using a closely related approach, found that divergence-based vertical velocity “greatly underestimated the vertical velocities experienced by the float” and attributed this to “a highly localized region of downwelling at the front that could not be resolved by the 5 km distances used to calculate d” [9]. Even with the proposal’s tighter 1 km array, a float following a narrow (tens of metres) subduction filament while the array averages near-zero vertical motion over 1 km represents a physically informative contrast, not a measurement failure. The proposal’s description of this comparison as contextual placement rather than strict validation is therefore appropriate [1].

The ship survey itself carries an acknowledged temporal-aliasing trade-off: the Johnson et al. study explicitly noted that “the cross-frontal extent of the Lagrangian survey is an example of the trade-off between spatial coverage and temporal aliasing” [9]. At submesoscale time scales (hours to the inertial period), ship surveys that take 1–2 hours to complete a 5 km pattern may alias the very gradients they seek to measure. The OSSE is intended to bound this effect.

### OSSE scope and model limitations

The OSSE plan is proportionate for its stated purpose of testing sampling patterns, array dimensions, repeated coverage, and potential aliasing across the nested 2 km, 250 m, and 30 m ROMS-MITgcm grids [1]. Acknowledged model limitations—the turbulence parameterisation gap at \~100 m scales, the absence of coupled atmosphere–ocean feedback in the air–sea flux estimates—define the boundary of what the OSSE can test [1]. At grid spacings approaching 100 m, explicitly simulated submesoscale structures overlap the scales represented by turbulence parameterizations, and the interaction is two-way: turbulence modifies fronts, and frontal gradients modify turbulence. The proposal explicitly identifies these limitations and correctly frames them as the scientific motivation for the field campaign. The OSSE will test whether the planned sampling captures the scales the models produce; it cannot validate whether the models produce the correct physics.

### Goal 4 flux proxy: structural limitations

The flux proxy W\_D = D·h is physically grounded in continuity—for ∂w/∂z = −D, under the assumption of uniform divergence to depth h, one obtains w(h) ≈ −D·h—and the proposal’s characterisation of this goal as “the most exploratory” [1] is appropriate. The published Torres et al. (2024) approach already illustrates a simpler route: deriving vertical velocities by “assuming the divergence is uniform in the upper half of the mixed-layer” [7], i.e., taking h as half the mixed-layer depth without *in situ* calibration. The proposal envisions a more ambitious calibration but, as the expert analysis makes clear, three structural gaps separate the proposed measurements from a validated flux estimate.

**First, the vertical structure of divergence is unknown.** The continuity equation requires knowing D(z) to integrate to w(z). DopplerScatt measures surface divergence tuned to \~4 m depth [7]. The proposal provides no observational or modelling evidence for how divergence decays with depth in the upper ocean at submesoscale. If divergence is surface-intensified, h is smaller than the mixed-layer depth. If divergence changes sign within the mixed layer—which can occur when wind-driven Ekman convergence coexists with frontal secondary circulations—the D·h proxy can give the wrong sign.

**Second, the method for determining h is underspecified.** The proposal states that h “will be inferred from in situ vertical velocity and radar divergence, and separately assessed in numerical models” [1], but this is described as an intention rather than a protocol, with no statistical approach, no expected number of coincident measurements, and no error propagation. A direct regression approach (h ≈ w\_in\_situ / D\_radar) requires coincident Lagrangian float or Wave-Glider-array vertical velocity and DopplerScatt divergence, yet the proposal does not estimate how many such coincidences the campaign design will yield.

**Third, the proposed validation comparisons target different physical scales.** The proposal states that W\_D estimates will be compared with “mesoscale Omega-equation estimates, Seaglider turbulent-flux estimates, and the contributions of different horizontal scales” [1]. The Omega equation reconstructs w on scales typically down to \~5–10 km [10]; Seaglider microstructure measures small-scale turbulent mixing, not the organised submesoscale vertical velocities that W\_D targets. These are useful consistency checks rather than validations of the same quantity, a distinction the proposal does not make explicit.

These gaps do not render Goal 4 unworthy of pursuit—the threshold mission preserves the core structural and velocity-relationship goals independently—but they mean the project’s endpoint for Goal 4 is properly characterised as a tested proxy concept with quantified assumptions and limitations, rather than a validated flux estimate.

## Feasibility

**The proposed work can be carried out with the stated methods, access, and resources, and the team’s demonstrated expertise with every major platform substantially reduces implementation risk.** The key feasibility considerations are the maturity of the instruments, the operational logistics of the multi-platform field campaign, and the critical dependency on Wave Glider station-keeping for one of the two vertical-velocity estimates.

### Instruments and aircraft

All airborne sensors are at Technology Readiness Level 6 or higher and have been deployed in the intended aircraft configurations. PRISM has operated on the ER-2 and other platforms; MASS has participated in 16 campaigns over seven years; MOSES has been used in two Gulf of Mexico campaigns; and DopplerScatt demonstrated wide-swath current and wind retrievals during the 2017 Gulf of Mexico experiment [1]. The aircraft accommodation margins are comfortable: DopplerScatt and MOSES fit within 55% of the King Air’s mass budget, MASS has 127% margin on the Twin Otter, and the ER-2 provides more than adequate altitude, speed, and power for PRISM [1]. The required flight profiles—10–15 flights per intensive operations period, with repeated coverage of a 50–100 km region in under an hour for the fast-sampling mode—fit within the stated aircraft endurance and speed capabilities [1].

### Ocean platforms and vessel access

The oceanographic component draws on equally mature platforms. The team has extensive experience with Seagliders, Lagrangian floats, instrumented Wave Gliders, and underway CTD surveys [1]. The Lagrangian float technology, in particular, was proven in the D’Asaro et al. (2018) experiment, where a single float measured subduction at 1–2 cm s⁻¹ with independent confirmation from its upward-looking ADCP [6]. A UNOLS ocean-class vessel, nominally R/V Sally Ride, is requested for 79 days including transit, mobilisation, and demobilisation [1]. The explicit provision for a pilot campaign in April 2020—with September 2020 as backup—to test platform interoperability, space–time sampling, and agreement between velocity and gradient measurements before committing to the main intensive periods provides a structured de-risking step [1].

### Site and risk management

The campaign site offshore of San Francisco is well chosen for practical access as well as scientific context. It is reachable from California ports and aircraft bases, overlaps a planned SWOT fast-repeat crossover, and has abundant prior observational and modelling coverage [1]. The risk register identifies the main operational threats—fog, clouds, rain, weak winds, airspace restrictions, instrument anomalies, and autonomous platform loss—with proportionate mitigations including seasonal scheduling, a 200% campaign-duration margin, early air-traffic coordination, test flights, spare components, and reserve platforms [1].

### Wave Glider formation-keeping

The critical feasibility question, as the Rigor section notes, is the Wave Glider formation-keeping for the \~1 km divergence array. Wave Gliders are wave-propelled at roughly 1–2 knots and cannot actively hold position against currents and wave drift; their formation geometry will inevitably evolve, and poorly conditioned formations degrade the gradient fit. The Bhuyan et al. (2024) study, using actively piloted Saildrones, found that formations with aspect ratio below 0.2 had to be discarded, and that even with active piloting only four frontal crossings were achieved in 36 hours of sustained quad formation [8]. The proposal does not specify a comparable formation-control protocol for the Wave Gliders. This does not make the array unworkable—useful gradient estimates can still be obtained with a larger effective footprint or over longer averaging periods—but it means the tight 1 km square and the 2 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹ divergence precision should be treated as design targets subject to operational refinement, rather than as guaranteed outcomes. The pilot campaign and the OSSE refinement phase are the appropriate vehicles for this refinement.

### Threshold-mission fallback

The explicit threshold-mission descope option—reducing the intensive operations period to 14 days and lowering flight and ship time by roughly half—provides a credible contingency if weather, platform losses, or other factors constrain the baseline plan [1]. This preserves the core Goals 1 and 3 even under adverse conditions, which substantially strengthens the overall feasibility assessment.

## Ethics and safety

**S-MODE is a physical oceanographic field campaign that does not engage human or animal subjects and does not involve invasive procedures, genetic resources, or interventions in protected areas; the ethical profile is straightforward and the safety framework is proportionate and well-documented.** The principal material risks—aircraft operations, oceanographic vessel operations, autonomous platform deployment, and the introduction of equipment into the marine environment—are managed through established procedures, mature instruments, and a structured risk-mitigation plan.

**Environmental responsibility.** The proposal makes explicit provision for environmental responsibility in its choice of surface drifters. The CARTHE-style drifters are described as biodegradable [1], and the field program uses approximately 100 drifters per intensive operations period [1], deployed in arrays of more than 30 in approximately 10 km patterns [1]. The use of biodegradable materials for expendable surface instrumentation is the recognised best practice for minimising long-term marine debris. Beyond the drifters, the environmental footprint consists of temporary deployment and recovery of Wave Gliders, Seagliders, and Lagrangian floats—all retrieved platforms—and standard ship operations on a UNOLS ocean-class vessel for 79 days including transit, mobilisation, and demobilisation [1]. No seafloor installations, seabed disturbance, or acoustic sources likely to affect marine mammals are described.

**Aircraft and vessel safety.** The operations area, offshore of San Francisco extending roughly 300 km from the coast, is selected partly to avoid nearby warning areas and to simplify air-traffic coordination [1]. Two of the three aircraft—the NASA King Air B200 and the NASA ER-2—are NASA-operated platforms that fly under the agency’s airworthiness and safety management systems. The Twin Otter DHC-6 carrying MASS is a chartered research aircraft with 16 prior campaigns [1]; its instrument payload of 265 lb fits within a 127% mass margin, consistent with a mature and well-characterised installation [1]. The risk register explicitly identifies fog, clouds, rain, airspace closure, and airborne instrument anomalies as operational threats, each assigned a likelihood and consequence rating with documented mitigations including seasonal scheduling, a 200% campaign-duration margin to avoid pressured go/no-go decisions, early air-traffic coordination, ground testing, test flights, and the availability of spare components [1]. The residual risk levels are consistently scored as low (consequence 1, minimal or small impact) after mitigation.

**Ethical approvals and biological sampling.** The proposal does not mention institutional ethics review, animal ethics approval, or specific permits for operations in the California Current region. For a physical oceanography campaign of this type—no human subjects, no protected-area interventions, no genetic or biological sampling beyond standard seawater collection for chlorophyll, POC, oxygen, and nutrients—such requirements would not ordinarily be triggered under US federal regulations or the institutional policies of the participating organisations. The seawater sampling [1] uses standard Niskin-bottle collections from the research vessel and serves calibration and proxy validation rather than biological experimentation; it does not introduce additional ethical or safety considerations beyond those already covered by standard shipboard scientific protocols and the vessel’s standing operating procedures.

**Autonomous platform loss.** The autonomous platform loss risk is addressed directly: after a Glider or Wave Glider loss, the remaining array and ship tracks can be reorganised, and early-campaign losses may be replaced from reserve [1]. This mitigates both the scientific impact and the environmental concern of unrecovered equipment. The pilot campaign, scheduled for April 2020 with a September 2020 backup, provides a dedicated opportunity to test platform performance and inter-platform coordination before the main intensive operations periods [1].

In summary, the proposal identifies the material risks inherent in a multi-aircraft, multi-vessel, multi-autonomous-platform field campaign, adopts standard and appropriate mitigations, and makes a specific, environmentally sound choice of biodegradable drifters. No material ethical issues arise from the project’s design or execution.

## Management

**S-MODE’s management framework is well-conceived for a multi-platform field campaign of this complexity, combining clear leadership responsibilities, a team with demonstrated expertise in every major observational component, a realistic schedule with structured decision points, and a risk register that identifies the principal threats with credible mitigations.**

### Leadership and organisational structure

The management structure divides responsibility across three domains under the PI (J. Thomas Farrar, WHOI) and a Science Investigation Manager (Paul Matthias): airborne observations led by Ernesto Rodriguez (NASA-JPL), *in situ* observations led by Andrey Shcherbina (UW-APL), and analysis/modelling led by Eric D’Asaro (UW-APL) [1]. This partition matches the natural operational divisions of the campaign and places each component under a recognised leader with direct prior experience. Rodriguez led the DopplerScatt development and its 2017 Gulf of Mexico demonstration [1]; Shcherbina was the lead author of the LatMix velocity gradient analysis [4] and a co-author on the Johnson et al. (2020) restratification study; D’Asaro led the Lagrangian float programme that produced the frontal subduction measurements in D’Asaro et al. (2018) [6]. The deployment support from NASA Ames Earth Science Project Office (ESPO) [1] provides an established logistical framework for the airborne component and has been used successfully in prior NASA airborne campaigns.

### Staffing, integration, and data delivery

The staffing plan allocates six postdoctoral researchers across two-year appointments, with staggered start dates tied to the campaign phases: early appointments for modelling and OSSE development, followed by appointments focused on air–sea interaction, Wave Glider and wave data analysis, and integrated frontal structure [1]. Two science workshops per year during the main analysis phase support cross-team integration. This sequencing is well-matched to a project in which the field data arrive in pulses (pilot, two intensive operations periods) and the analysis phase outlasts the field deployments.

The data delivery schedule reinforces this structure: Level 0 *in situ* data are due within two months of each campaign, Level 1–3 products within six months, and MASS Level 3 directional spectra and breaking statistics within 6–12 months of the relevant campaign [1]. The PI and Science Investigation Manager oversee delivery to the designated NASA data centre (PO.DAAC), with explicit milestones in the project timeline [1].

### Schedule and descope logic

The milestone plan incorporates the descoping logic built into the threshold-mission concept [1]. The pilot campaign (April 2020, with September 2020 backup) occurs nine months after the project start, providing a structured window for instrument integration, confirmation reviews, and an initial test of platform interoperability before the main intensive periods in April and September 2021 [1]. The six-month buffer between the pilot and the first main campaign is sufficient for rigorous data intercomparison and any necessary corrections. The backup scheduling (September 2020 for the pilot, September 2021 and April 2022 for the main campaigns) preserves the seasonal comparison even under a six-month overall delay [1].

### Risk management

The risk register identifies five threat categories—weather, airspace, airborne instrument anomalies, airborne instrument performance, and autonomous platform loss—each with an initial consequence/likelihood rating, documented mitigations, and a residual rating [1]. The mitigations are operational rather than aspirational: seasonal scheduling informed by weather predictions, a 200% campaign-duration margin that avoids pressured go/no-go decisions, early air-traffic coordination, ground testing and test flights, spare components procured six months before campaigns, and the ability to reorganise remaining platforms around ship tracks after a loss. The residual risk levels are scored as low (consequence 1) after mitigation, with a quarterly review cycle during campaigns [1]. This is a proportionate approach for a campaign using mature instruments and established platforms; the main unaddressed item—a protocol for Wave Glider formation-keeping—is a refinement that belongs in the pilot analysis and OSSE phase rather than in the risk register proper.

### Modelling integration and public data

The science–implementation link is strengthened by the modelling programme’s dual role: OSSE simulations before the campaigns to test sampling patterns, and concurrent data-assimilating simulations during the analysis phase to compare model output with observations [1]. The use of two model frameworks (ROMS and MITgcm) provides cross-model robustness for the OSSE tests. The project’s explicit public-data policy—Level-2 data available to the team and public within six months of each campaign, with NASA archive deposition—supports the benchmark ambition and the broader community use of the dataset [1].

### Integration challenge

The primary management uncertainty, intrinsic to any campaign of this scope, is the integration of data streams from three aircraft, four Wave Gliders, five Seagliders, Lagrangian floats, surface drifters, and a research vessel into consistent, cross-calibrated products within the analysis timeline. The data delivery schedule partitions this into manageable segments, and the assignment of product leads to specific named investigators provides accountability [1]. While the proposal does not describe a data-management system architecture or a dedicated data manager, this is consistent with NASA EVS-3 practice in which instrument teams are responsible for their own processing pipelines and the PI provides coordination. No deficiency threatens the delivery of the core Goals 1–3 dataset.

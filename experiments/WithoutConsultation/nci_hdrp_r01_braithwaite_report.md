# Project review

## Overall assessment

This proposal, led by Dr. Braithwaite and a multidisciplinary team, seeks to address a critical gap in lung cancer screening (LCS) by determining how multimorbidity—particularly co-existing chronic conditions, functional limitations, and impaired pulmonary function—modifies the balance of benefits and harms from low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening in real-world clinical practice. The core insight is that patients at highest risk for lung cancer also face elevated risks of competing mortality and treatment-related complications, meaning they may not derive the greatest net benefit from screening. The planned work combines a large, multi-site observational cohort of approximately 34,000 screened adults with the team's validated CISNET microsimulation model (UM-LCS) to achieve its most valuable expected contribution: quantitative, comorbidity-specific evidence that can be used to individualize screening decisions, moving beyond current generic ‘healthy enough’ guidelines.

The project is supported by a strong rationale, a well-characterized evidence gap formalized in an ATS statement co-authored by the team, and significant preliminary work. This includes a validated NLP algorithm for nodule identification, an established multi-system data infrastructure from the Watch the Spot study, and the existing, validated UM-LCS microsimulation model. The three-site design (KPSC, MUSC, VA Portland) provides crucial diversity.

However, several material limitations affect the judgement. The observational design, while appropriate, is restricted to the screened population and cannot directly address selection into screening. The planned functional limitation (FRI) data are systematically unavailable at MUSC, reducing effective power for a key analytic dimension, and the power calculations are insufficient for the precise, multi-dimensional subgroup estimates that are the project’s most ambitious goal. Finally, the microsimulation’s lifetime projections will depend on significant extrapolation of competing mortality from a 6-year observation window.

The proposal tackles a question of high clinical importance with a well-reasoned design that builds on a strong foundation of prior work. Its main strength is the innovative and pragmatic plan to generate real-world, comorbidity-stratified evidence and directly incorporate it into a policy-relevant microsimulation model. This trade-off is ultimately positive; the project’s most likely products—a rich, harmonized dataset and a model that meaningfully advances risk stratification beyond a one-size-fits-all approach—represent a worthwhile and supportable contribution, even if some of the most granular, multi-dimensional subgroup thresholds prove elusive. The project’s strengths in scientific value and feasibility outweigh the noted methodological constraints, which can be managed through appropriate sensitivity analyses and a realistic interpretation of the findings.

**Recommendation:** Support

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

The project addresses a clinically urgent and well-recognized knowledge gap whose resolution would directly improve the quality of lung cancer screening decisions for millions of patients. The core problem is compelling and clearly stated: smoking increases lung cancer risk but simultaneously increases chronic respiratory and cardiovascular disease, competing mortality, and complications of invasive procedures, so that patients at greatest cancer risk need not obtain the greatest net benefit from screening [1]. The conceptual model—that net benefit may peak at intermediate risk and decline when competing mortality and treatment-related harms dominate—is clinically intuitive and well articulated [1]. Current guideline language invoking “reasonably good health” or the ability to “tolerate treatment” lacks transparent quantitative selection criteria, leaving clinicians without evidence-based tools to distinguish patients likely to benefit from those for whom screening may cause net harm [1].

The importance of this gap is confirmed by the 2018 American Thoracic Society Research Statement, which was co-authored by several members of the present team (Braithwaite, Gould, Silvestri, and Caverly) and concluded that there is “an urgent need for further research that can help guide clinical decision-making with patients who may not benefit from LCS owing to coexisting chronic illness” [2]. The NLST, which established the mortality benefit of LDCT screening, enrolled participants who were substantially healthier and younger than the community population now eligible for screening: only 25% were at least 65 years old, none were older than 74, and estimated life expectancy was 21.2 years versus 14 years for a Medicare screening-eligible subgroup [1]. Comparisons with screening-eligible respondents in the Health and Retirement Study reveal markedly higher comorbidity, more current smoking, and shorter life expectancy in the real-world population [1]. The project is therefore not merely extending existing evidence but addressing a qualitative shift in the population to which trial findings must be applied.

The project’s three aims are logically sequenced: Aim 1 characterizes the distribution of multimorbidity, functional limitations, and impaired pulmonary function in the real-world screened population; Aim 2 quantifies how these factors associate with short-to-intermediate-term screening outcomes including false-positive findings, procedure-related complications, and cancer detection; Aim 3 feeds these comorbidity-stratified, real-world estimates into the validated UM-LCS microsimulation model to project lifetime net benefit across subgroups [1]. The intended products include subgroup-specific estimates of screening harms and benefits, a refined simulation model with empirically grounded inputs, and evidence to support screening discussions and future guideline development [1].

The most realistic and valuable contribution is the identification of groups where net benefit is clearly favorable versus clearly unfavorable—for instance, distinguishing patients with mild comorbidity who remain strong screening candidates from those with severe multimorbidity in whom competing mortality and procedural harms dominate. This level of stratification would already represent a meaningful advance over current generic eligibility criteria. The more ambitious goal of precisely estimating multidimensional thresholds across the full space of comorbidity × functional limitation × pulmonary function combinations faces acknowledged constraints in data completeness (FRI available at approximately 80% of the cohort [1], spirometry on a subset [1]) and statistical power for intersectional subgroups, as detailed in the Rigor assessment. Nonetheless, the knowledge that can be attained—a harmonized, richly phenotyped multi-system cohort linked to a comorbidity-calibrated simulation model—would represent a durable resource for the field, supporting both the immediate analyses and subsequent clinical and policy applications.

## Innovation

The proposal’s innovation lies in integrating three elements that no prior study has combined: a multi-system real-world screening cohort with racial, socioeconomic, and care-delivery diversity; multidimensional patient phenotyping that extends beyond comorbidity counts to functional limitations and quantitative pulmonary function; and the direct feeding of these empirically estimated, comorbidity-stratified parameters into a validated CISNET microsimulation model for lifetime projections. Each element individually exists in prior work; their systematic integration in a single prospective design is the novel contribution.

**Prior work and remaining gap.** Caverly et al. (2018) used the UM-LCS microsimulation model to identify life expectancy and lung cancer risk thresholds where screening becomes preference-sensitive, a conceptual advance demonstrating that moderate differences in patient preferences could change screening appropriateness for persons with annual lung cancer risk below 0.3% or life expectancy below 10.5 years [3]. That analysis, however, relied entirely on trial-based inputs (NLST, PLCO, SEER) that reflected a healthier population. It did not incorporate comorbidity-specific false-positive rates, biopsy rates, complication rates, or competing mortality measured from real-world screened patients—precisely the parameters the current proposal will estimate and feed into the same model [1]. The Gould et al. (2017) Cancer Research Network study demonstrated the feasibility of multi-site data collection from EHRs for LCS monitoring but did not measure functional limitations or pulmonary function and did not analyze comorbidity-stratified outcomes [4]. The Gould et al. (2017) latent-class analysis identified comorbidity profiles associated with treatment selection and survival, but in diagnosed lung cancer patients rather than a screening cohort [1] [5].

**Incremental novelty relative to subsequent work.** The distinctive contribution has narrowed somewhat since the proposal's conception. Kale et al. (2024) extended a validated lung cancer screening microsimulation model (the Lung Cancer Policy Model, not UM-LCS) to incorporate comorbidity-specific effects on complications, quality of life, and mortality, finding that comorbidities reduce life-years gained from screening [6]. More importantly, Rager et al. (2026), co-authored by the UM-LCS team (Meza, Cao, Hayward, Caverly), already used that model to estimate individualized quality-adjusted life-year gains for 59 million ever-smokers and identify net-benefit thresholds for encouraging, discouraging, or offering neutral shared decision-making about screening [7]. This work operationalizes the individualized net-benefit estimation that the current proposal targets, though without using the proposal’s real-world cohort for calibration.

**What remains distinctive is the empirical grounding.** The Rager et al. analysis, like Caverly et al., parameterizes the model using trial and registry data; the current proposal will replace those generic inputs with comorbidity-stratified estimates of competing mortality, Lung-RADS adherence, biopsy and complication rates, and false-positive rates measured directly from approximately 34,000 patients screened across three diverse health systems [1]. The three-system design—KPSC (integrated care, ethnically diverse), MUSC (safety-net hospital), and VA Portland (veterans with dedicated screening coordination)—provides variation in care delivery, socioeconomic context, and patient demographics that no single-system study can offer [1]. The addition of claims-based Function-Related Indicators (FRI) on a 0–13 scale and structured spirometry with GOLD classification extends characterization beyond the Charlson/Elixhauser scores used in all prior work, capturing the mechanistic pathways—functional decline, airflow limitation—through which comorbidity plausibly translates to screening-related harm [1].

Whether these empirical refinements yield meaningfully different policy conclusions than the trial-calibrated model remains to be demonstrated, but the proposal provides the direct test. The principal uncertainty is not whether the combination is novel—it is—but whether the partial availability of FRI (approximately 80% of the cohort) and spirometry data will permit the full multidimensional integration that would maximize the project’s originality. This is addressed under Rigor.

## Rigor

The study design is methodologically competent for its core descriptive and associative goals but has material limitations—systematic missingness of functional limitation data, absence of explicit competing-risk methods, over-reliance on AIC-driven variable selection, and insufficient power for the most ambitious intersectional subgroup estimates—that constrain the strength of causal inference and the precision of the project’s most distinctive contribution.

**Confounding control and the restriction to screened patients.** The use of generalized propensity scores (GPS) with multinomial logistic models, incorporating demographics, smoking, clinical information, and geographic location, is a reasonable strategy for addressing confounding by measured variables within the screened cohort [1]. However, all participants have already undergone screening—the cohort excludes eligible individuals who were never screened. Because greater comorbidity burden strongly reduces screening uptake, those with the most severe multimorbidity may be underrepresented in ways the GPS cannot address. The resulting estimates will be conditional on having been screened, and generalizing to the broader screening-eligible population would require external assumptions not supplied by the design. This is a recognized constraint of screening cohort studies and does not invalidate the project, but the associative rather than causal interpretation of comorbidity–outcome relationships should be explicit throughout the reporting.

**Systematic missingness of functional limitation data.** The FRI is derived from claims and EHR indicators and will be obtained “primarily at KPSC and VA, which together represent nearly 80% of the cohort” [1]. At MUSC, a state safety-net hospital serving a socioeconomically distinct population, FRI data are expected to be unavailable or of substantially lower quality. This is structured, site-level missingness—not random—and the proposal’s plan to address missing data using logistic models, inverse-probability weighting, and multiple imputation under a missing-at-random (MAR) assumption [1] does not adequately address it. When data are absent due to study design, the foundational assumptions of most imputation algorithms are violated and imputation quality deteriorates when overlap between sites is limited. Pattern-mixture models or other missing-not-at-random (MNAR) methods would be more appropriate but are not proposed. The practical consequence is that FRI-stratified analyses are effectively restricted to approximately 27,000 patients at KPSC and VA, reducing power for the central functional-limitation dimension. The proposal would be strengthened by committing to complete-case analyses restricted to KPSC and VA for FRI models as the primary analysis, with MUSC included only in sensitivity analyses.

**Competing mortality and cumulative risk estimation.** The discrete-time survival framework, with time indexed by examination number and time-varying covariates updated at each screening round, is well-suited to the episodic nature of screening risk [1]. However, individuals with the highest comorbidity burden face elevated competing mortality and are therefore less likely to survive to contribute observations to later rounds. The proposal describes cumulative risks “over the proposed six-year screening course” [1] but does not specify whether these are cumulative incidence functions accounting for competing risk. Treating death as independent censoring in this context likely overstates screening harms in the high-comorbidity groups most depleted by mortality. A Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model or cause-specific cumulative incidence approach would provide more appropriate estimates and should be adopted.

**Variable selection and model specification.** Backward selection using AIC is proposed “to balance predictive performance and model complexity” [1]. While preferable to p-value-based stepwise methods, AIC-driven selection is known to be suboptimal for causal inference because it can exclude confounders weakly associated with the outcome but strongly associated with the exposure, or retain mediators and colliders. A more principled approach would pre-specify a core set of adjustment variables based on directed acyclic graphs, including all three multimorbidity dimensions (Charlson/Elixhauser score, FRI, pulmonary function) and their two-way interactions as the minimum model, reserving backward selection only for additional covariates. This is especially important because the interaction between comorbidity dimensions is the study’s central scientific target.

**Power for intersectional subgroups.** The power calculations project 34,039 screened people and 902 incident cancers, with detectable risk differences of 5% for complications and 1.3%–1.9% for false positives [1] [8]. These are main-effect comparisons, not estimates for cross-classified subgroups. Using PROSPR-Lung consortium data as the best available comparator, Charlson ≥2 is expected in approximately 38% of the cohort [9], and COPD in 35–40% [9] [10]. The critical intersection that the conceptual model targets—patients with both substantial comorbidity (Charlson ≥2) and functional limitation (FRI ≥2) and severe COPD—may number only a few hundred, with perhaps 15–30 cancer events and even fewer complications (only 2.8% of screened patients undergo invasive diagnostic procedures, with 30.6% experiencing complications [11]). At those levels, cancer detection rate estimates have confidence intervals of ±30–50% relative width, and complication comparisons between subgroups are unreliable.

The proposal would benefit from shifting from hypothesis testing toward estimation with explicit precision targets, pre-specifying broader combined categories (e.g., any two of three high-risk indicators rather than all three simultaneously) as the primary subgroup analysis, and acknowledging that the Aim 3 microsimulation—which can interpolate across the comorbidity space—will carry more of the inferential burden for the narrowest subgroups than Aim 2’s direct estimates.

## Feasibility

The project is overall feasible, leveraging an established team with demonstrated multi-site data infrastructure, a validated NLP pipeline, and a mature microsimulation model, but its feasibility for delivering the full multidimensional stratification depends critically on data completeness for functional and pulmonary measures that are only partially available across sites.

**Data infrastructure and cohort assembly are supported by substantial prior investment.** The Watch the Spot (WTS) study provides a common vocabulary and data infrastructure covering 13 groups of elements—eligibility, demographics, smoking, nodules, utilization, comorbidity, and vital status—that will be reused for the proposed cohort [1]. The team's validated NLP algorithm for identifying pulmonary nodules in radiology reports achieves 96% sensitivity and 92% specificity, with an expanded version capturing laterality, lobe, size, attenuation, edge, and calcification at sensitivity and specificity above 95% [1]. The three participating health systems—KPSC, MUSC, and VA Portland—each have established LCS programs with structured EHR order sets, clinical reminders, and dedicated screening coordination that facilitate distinguishing screening from diagnostic scans, a known challenge in prior work [1] [4]. Data sources include EHR structured fields, NLP, text mining, administrative and membership records, ICD-9/10 diagnoses and procedures, CPT and HCPCS codes, radiology reports, pathology, tumor registries, mortality data, census linkage, and Medicare linkage for care outside participating systems [1]. This combination of existing infrastructure, validated phenotyping tools, and established clinical programs makes cohort assembly of approximately 34,039 screened individuals across 2016–2022 realistically achievable within the first three years, as the timeline proposes [1].

**The critical feasibility constraint is the partial availability of functional limitation and pulmonary function data.** FRI indicators from claims and EHR will be obtained primarily at KPSC and VA, representing approximately 80% of the cohort; MUSC data are expected to be unavailable or of lower quality [1]. Structured spirometry for COPD severity classification is available only on a subset of participants at KPSC and, in the published protocol, at OneFlorida [1] [8]. The full three-way stratification at the heart of the project's distinctive contribution—comorbidity (Charlson/Elixhauser) × functional limitation (FRI) × pulmonary function (GOLD classification)—will therefore be feasible on a substantially reduced subset of the total cohort. This does not prevent execution of Aims 1 and 2, which can estimate associations for each dimension separately and for pairwise combinations where data permit, but it means the most granular intersectional estimates will carry wide uncertainty. Site-level systematic unavailability is inherently not recoverable through imputation from other sites with different populations and care models. The feasibility judgement acknowledges that this partial data availability constrains the attainable contribution but does not preclude generating useful comorbidity-stratified estimates and a more empirically grounded simulation model.

**The timeline and analytic sequence are realistic.** Regulatory approvals and cohort development begin in the first year, with three years devoted to assembling the real-world cohort and generating Aim 1 and Aim 2 estimates, and two years for model refinement and Aim 3 simulation [1]. The UM-LCS model is already calibrated to NLST and PLCO outcomes [1] [12] and the team has extensive experience using it for comparative screening analyses [8]. The model inputs to be refined from the cohort—comorbidity-stratified competing mortality, Lung-RADS adherence, biopsy and complication rates, and screening outcomes [1]—are parameters for which the 6-year observation window is informative, even though long-term competing mortality will require parametric extrapolation beyond the observed period. Manuscript preparation and dissemination are integrated into the later years [1], and the expected products—a harmonized clinical cohort, subgroup-specific estimates, and a refined simulation model [1]—are commensurate with the planned resources.

**The three-site design provides both strength and complexity.** Geographic, socioeconomic, racial/ethnic, and care-delivery variation across KPSC, MUSC, and VA Portland [1] enhances generalizability but also introduces data harmonization challenges. The reuse of WTS common data elements, a central data coordinating center at KPSC, and a common data dictionary implemented at each institution [1] provides a concrete plan for managing this complexity. The team's prior success combining EHR and claims information across institutions and studying pulmonary nodule management [1] [4] provides direct evidence of capability for multi-site data integration, though differences in EHR systems and clinical documentation practices across an integrated health plan, a safety-net hospital, and a VA facility will require sustained attention during cohort development.

## Ethics and safety

The project is a retrospective and prospective observational study using existing clinical and administrative data; it involves no new patient interventions, specimen collection, or direct participant contact, and the associated risks are therefore limited to informational privacy and data confidentiality. These risks are appropriately managed through a proportionate, multi-layered data governance plan that is clearly described and consistent with current standards for multi-site research using electronic health records.

The study analyzes LDCT screening outcomes, comorbidity, functional status, and pulmonary function from EHR, claims, cancer registries, and mortality records for approximately 34,039 adults across three health systems [1]. Because the data are generated during routine clinical care and are analyzed retrospectively for the 2016–2019 period and prospectively for 2020–2022, the project does not alter patients’ screening schedules, diagnostic pathways, or treatment. It therefore poses no direct physical, psychological, or clinical management risk to the individuals whose records are included. This places it in the category of secondary data research where the primary ethical obligations concern privacy protection, data security, and appropriate regulatory oversight.

The proposal’s data governance architecture addresses these obligations through several reinforcing layers. First, regulatory approvals—explicitly including IRB review—will be sought at all three study sites and at the data coordinating center [1]. Second, the data separation and de-identification procedures are well specified: each institution will maintain its own secure patient database, and names, medical record numbers, and dates of birth will be linked to unique study IDs and kept separately from the analytical datasets [1]. Third, the data transfer and access controls are explicitly addressed: the coordinating center at KPSC will use a common HIPAA-compliant structure, and REDCap will support encrypted data transmission from MUSC and VA Portland to KPSC, with authenticated and authorized access controls [1]. REDCap is widely used in clinical research and provides audit trails, user-level permissions, and encrypted data storage, making it an appropriate platform for multi-site studies of this scale and sensitivity. Fourth, the scope of data collection—demographics, smoking history, comorbidity diagnoses, procedure codes, radiology reports, spirometry values, tumor registry records, and mortality data—is necessary for the stated scientific aims and does not extend beyond what is required to characterize multimorbidity and quantify screening outcomes [1]. Census linkage for area-level socioeconomic measures and Medicare linkage for care received outside participating systems are standard and proportionate supplements.

One consideration worth noting is that the proposal does not explicitly discuss whether individual consent will be sought or waived. In US practice, retrospective EHR-based research of this type routinely qualifies for a waiver of informed consent under the Common Rule when the research involves no more than minimal risk, could not practicably be conducted with consent, and does not adversely affect subjects’ rights or welfare. The approximately 34,000-person cohort across multiple states, spanning seven years of clinical data, meets the practicability criterion. The commitment to IRB review [1] provides the appropriate mechanism for determining waiver eligibility at each site, and the absence of a detailed consent discussion in the proposal narrative does not indicate an ethical deficiency.

## Management

The project is led by a multidisciplinary team whose expertise spans all the domains required to execute the study, and the management structure—centralized data coordination, regular communication cycles, and a realistic five-year schedule—provides proportionate oversight for a three-site observational study with integrated microsimulation.

**Team expertise is well matched to the project’s requirements.** PI Braithwaite contributes epidemiology and cancer screening research; co-investigators Gould (thoracic oncology, health services research, comorbidity profiling) and Silvestri (thoracic oncology, screening program leadership) bring the clinical and research expertise represented in their prior collaborative work—the Gould et al. (2017) Cancer Research Network LCS monitoring study, the Gould et al. (2017) latent-class comorbidity analysis, and the 2018 ATS Research Statement [1]. The microsimulation component is supported by the UM-LCS modeling team (Meza, Jeon, and colleagues), whose CISNET work has been externally validated against NLST and PLCO outcomes and who have published multiple comparative screening analyses, including the Caverly et al. (2018) preference-sensitivity study and the recent Rager et al. (2026) individualized net-benefit analysis [1] [7] [12]. Statistical leadership is provided through the data coordinating center at KPSC led by Gould and Shen, with additional statistical input from Fan [1]. Patient advocates are included to inform interpretation and dissemination for patient-centered decision support [1]. This team has a publication record that traces directly from the ATS research statement—which defined the knowledge gap—through the methods development and feasibility work to the current full-scale proposal, indicating a coherent, long-planned research program rather than an ad hoc assembly.

**The reuse of Watch the Spot data infrastructure reduces start-up risk.** The WTS study has already established a common vocabulary and data infrastructure covering 13 groups of elements (eligibility, demographics, smoking, nodules, utilization, comorbidity, vital status) [1]. Applying this existing framework to the current screening-focused cohort avoids building data harmonization de novo and provides a concrete basis for cross-site consistency. Site-level data extraction uses familiar methods—structured EHR exports, NLP, text mining—that the team has validated in prior work [1].

**The schedule and coordination structure are proportionate.** The five-year timeline places regulatory approvals in the first half of Year 1, cohort development from the second quarter of Year 1 through Year 3, Aims 1–2 analysis from Year 1 through Year 4, and Aim 3 simulation refinement from late Year 1 through Year 5, with manuscript preparation in Years 3–5 [1]. The overlapping sequencing of data assembly and analysis is realistic given that retrospective data (2016–2019) are available early while prospective data (2020–2022) accumulate. Monthly team calls and annual in-person meetings provide regular communication across three geographically distributed sites [1]. The data coordinating center at KPSC provides a single point of responsibility for data capture, mapping, transfer, and management [1].

**Risk mitigation for data harmonization could be more explicit.** The three sites—an integrated health plan (KPSC), a safety-net hospital (MUSC), and a VA facility (VA Portland)—use different EHR systems and serve distinct populations [1]. While the WTS common data dictionary and central coordination provide a structural framework, differences in clinical documentation practices, variable completeness of structured fields, and site-specific coding conventions can generate unanticipated harmonization challenges during cohort assembly. The proposal describes the planned data elements and sources in detail [1] but does not specify a formal process for identifying and resolving discrepancies during the cohort-building phase—for example, through early data-quality audits comparing key variables across sites or pre-specified decision rules for handling site-level differences in variable definitions. The team’s demonstrated experience with multi-site EHR integration [1] [4] suggests these challenges will be manageable, but an explicit quality-assurance protocol would strengthen confidence that the harmonized dataset will support the intended cross-site analyses on schedule. The experienced team, the reuse of proven infrastructure, and the realistic timeline together support a judgement that the management arrangements are fit for purpose, even if the risk-mitigation detail could be fuller.

# Project review

## Overall assessment

This study evaluates screening performance when synthesized two-dimensional mammography replaces conventional digital mammography in a digital breast tomosynthesis (s2D/DBT) examination. It leverages a natural experiment at seven radiology facilities (academic and community) that uniformly switched from combined DM/DBT to s2D/DBT, using prospectively collected records from the PROSPR Research Centers at the University of Vermont and the University of Pennsylvania. Approximately 185,700 screening examinations from 2011–2017 and 12-month cancer-registry outcomes will be analyzed. The project addresses three specific aims: (1) clinical screening performance (recall, cancer detection, sensitivity, specificity), (2) lesion-specific detection including histopathologic subtyping of benign biopsies and molecular characterization of invasive cancers, and (3) the distribution of BI-RADS breast density assessments. The most valuable expected knowledge is the first multicenter characterization of how s2D affects the types of benign lesions biopsied and the systematic comparison of density classification between the two DBT acquisition modes—both directly informing clinical practice, patient notification requirements, and supplemental screening decisions.

The before/after design with the same 22 radiologists, common Hologic equipment, uniform facility-level adoption, and 12-month cancer-registry outcome ascertainment provides stronger internal validity than cross-sectional modality comparisons. Feasibility is excellent—the data already exist, the centers have pooled data before, and the team has deep expertise in this exact research area. External evidence on DBT learning curves and density stability across modalities bolsters the plausibility that observed differences reflect the modality change. The two novel aims (lesion-specific analyses and formal density comparison) address genuine, practice-relevant gaps not resolved by prior multicenter work.

Material limitations require acknowledgement. The proposal's Aim 1 substantially overlaps with the team's own published 2020 multicenter study (Zuckerman, Sprague et al., *Radiology*, 151,363 exams), which already reported all Aim 1 metrics for the same comparison at the same centers; the knowledge increment for core performance is therefore modest. The analytical plan, while correctly adopting the Miglioretti-Heagerty GEE approach for non-nested clustering, lacks specification of the working correlation structure, the three-source variance extension, and the design-effect assumptions underlying the power calculations. The absence of concurrent controls and the reliance on covariate adjustment alone without interrupted time-series analysis leaves temporal confounding partially unaddressed. Power is limited for rare outcomes such as DCIS and benign lesion subtypes, which the proposal appropriately labels exploratory. Ethical and management arrangements are sound and proportionate. These limitations affect the confidence and novelty of specific inferences without undermining the study's practical contribution.

The study will deliver a useful, practice-relevant multicenter evaluation that extends existing evidence on s2D/DBT screening performance. Its strongest contributions—benign lesion subtyping and a rigorous density classification comparison—fill genuine knowledge gaps with practical implications for biopsy management and supplemental screening policy. The design is largely appropriate for the question, and feasibility is high given the existing data and team expertise. However, the core Aim 1 findings largely replicate already-published multicenter work from the same investigators, which limits the study's broader scientific impact. The analytical plan, while based on a sound GEE framework, omits specifics that would strengthen confidence in the power calculations and inference, and it does not fully exploit the staggered transition timing to address temporal confounding. These are correctable limitations that do not nullify the value of the proposed work, but they do constrain the overall contribution to a moderate increment on an already established evidence base. On balance, the investigation is well-supported and feasible, and it will produce practical knowledge for screening practice, though its incremental nature and analytical under-specification temper its overall strength.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

The proposed study addresses a question of genuine clinical and policy relevance with practical implications for routine breast cancer screening. The central issue is whether replacing the conventional digital mammography (DM) acquisition in a digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT) examination with a synthesized two-dimensional image (s2D/DBT) maintains screening performance while allowing the dose reduction—estimated at approximately 45% compared with combined DM/DBT—that motivates the technology's adoption [1]. The question matters at scale: millions of women undergo DBT screening annually, and evidence on s2D's effects is needed by patients choosing screening modalities, providers considering technology adoption, payers making coverage decisions, and screening policy-makers [1].

The attainable knowledge spans three domains. Aim 1 will deliver multicenter estimates of recall rate, cancer detection, sensitivity, specificity, false-negative rate, and positive predictive value for s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT, with 12-month cancer-registry outcome ascertainment enabling calculation of sensitivity and specificity that cross-sectional studies without follow-up cannot provide [1]. Aim 2 will characterize screen-detected lesions by histopathologic type, including benign biopsies stratified into non-proliferative changes, fibroepithelial proliferations, proliferative lesions without atypia, and atypical lesions, together with invasive cancers by ER/PR/HER2 status [1]. This lesion-level detail has direct clinical utility: if s2D/DBT reduces biopsies of low-risk proliferative lesions while maintaining detection of atypical lesions and invasive cancers, the evidence would strengthen the case for s2D as a replacement for conventional DM in the DBT acquisition. Aim 3 will determine whether s2D images systematically shift BI-RADS breast density classifications [1]. This is policy-relevant because density notification laws in many jurisdictions require informing women of their density status and may trigger recommendations for supplemental imaging; if s2D changes perceived density, women could receive different risk assessments and management recommendations without a corresponding biological change [1].

The value of this knowledge is moderated by the fact that the core performance comparison has already been substantially addressed by the team's own published multicenter study. The Zuckerman, Sprague et al. 2020 *Radiology* paper reported all Aim 1 metrics for s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT using 151,363 examinations from the same two PROSPR centers, finding no significant difference in cancer detection (5.6 vs 5.9 per 1,000, P = 0.55), sensitivity (adjusted OR 1.27, P = 0.33), false-negative rate (P = 0.38), or invasive versus DCIS detection (P = 0.52 and P = 0.98) [2]. Two meta-analyses confirm that cancer detection is comparable and recall is lower or similar for s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT [3] [4]. The proposed study extends this evidence across a larger sample (approximately 185,700 versus 151,363 examinations) and adds four community-hospital facilities in Vermont beyond the academic centers previously studied [1]. The knowledge increment for Aim 1 is therefore refinement rather than discovery.

The novel contributions reside in Aims 2 and 3. No prior multicenter study of this comparison has reported benign biopsy findings stratified by histopathologic subtype, nor has any study examined invasive cancer detection by receptor status. The density classification comparison, while partially addressed by single-site work showing 80.3% agreement between s2D and DM (κ = 0.73) [5], has not been formally powered in a multicenter, within-facility, within-radiologist design. The proposed detectable absolute difference of 0.6 percentage points in dense classifications at 80% power [1] would provide the most rigorous evaluation of this question to date. The diverse populations served—predominantly white rural women in Vermont and a racially diverse urban population in Pennsylvania [1]—support the generalizability of the findings.

The overall scientific value represents a meaningful but incremental contribution: the existing evidence base already supports comparable s2D/DBT screening performance, and the proposed study provides validated replication in expanded community settings plus genuinely new lesion-specific and density information that directly informs biopsy management and supplemental screening policy.

## Innovation

The proposal refines an already well-characterized comparison rather than opening a new question, but the lesion-specific and density aims add genuinely new information not available from prior multicenter work.

The central comparison—s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT screening performance—has been substantially addressed by existing evidence. As noted above, the team's own 2020 multicenter study reported all Aim 1 metrics using the same before/after design, the same Hologic equipment, the same 12-month cancer-registry outcome ascertainment, and the same GEE-based adjustment strategy the current proposal describes [2]. Two comprehensive meta-analyses confirm these findings: Zeng 2021 (13 studies, 1.37 million participants) reported no difference in cancer detection rate (RD = −0.1 per 1,000, 95% CI −0.4 to 0.2, I² = 0%) and lower recall for s2D/DBT [3]; Hamad 2024 (17 studies) confirmed these results and additionally demonstrated higher specificity for s2D/DBT (difference 1.08%, 95% CI 0.59–1.56) [4]. The proposal's incremental contribution to Aim 1—larger sample, four additional community-hospital facilities, greater population diversity—represents valuable replication and generalizability testing rather than new discovery.

The novel contribution is concentrated in Aims 2 and 3. Benign lesion subtyping by histopathologic category (non-proliferative, fibroepithelial, proliferative without atypia, atypical) has not been reported in any prior s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT study. This addresses a clinically important question raised by the altered image appearance of s2D: the proposal notes that reconstructed architectural distortion can become more conspicuous while normal dense structures may resemble calcifications [1], and these changes could shift which benign lesions are biopsied. If s2D/DBT reduces biopsies of low-risk lesions while maintaining detection of atypical proliferations and invasive cancers, the evidence would directly inform biopsy management decisions. Similarly, invasive cancer characterization by ER/PR/HER2 status for this comparison is novel. The proposal appropriately labels these analyses exploratory given limited statistical power [1], but they represent a clear increment beyond what has been published.

The density classification aim fills a gap that prior work has touched but not resolved. The Alshafeiy 2017 single-site study showed comparable overall agreement between s2D and DM density assignments but documented reader-level variability in the direction of shifts [5]. The Sprague 2019 BCSC analysis demonstrated stable density distributions between the BI-RADS 4th and 5th editions and between DBT and DM at the population level [6], but did not examine the specific s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT comparison within the same radiologists and facilities. The proposal's within-radiologist, within-facility design with formal power to detect a 0.6 percentage-point difference will provide the most rigorous evaluation of this policy-relevant question to date.

The study's contribution is best characterized as a modest but useful increment: replication with expanded generalizability for the core performance question, plus genuinely novel lesion-specific and density evidence that directly informs clinical practice and policy. The innovation grade reflects the predominance of replication in Aim 1, moderated by the clear originality of the lesion-specific and density aims.

## Rigor

**Design strengths.** The within-facility before/after design with uniform adoption, common equipment, and 12-month cancer-registry outcome ascertainment provides substantially better protection against confounding than the cross-sectional modality comparisons common in the early s2D literature. Seven facilities that uniformly transitioned from DM/DBT to s2D/DBT serve as their own controls, with the same 22 radiologists interpreting in both periods [1]. Uniform facility-level adoption eliminates patient-level selection into modality—the dominant source of bias in observational modality comparisons—and shared equipment (Hologic Selenia Dimensions with C-View reconstruction) removes between-manufacturer variation in image quality and reconstruction algorithms [1]. Linkage to pathology and cancer registries provides 12-month outcome ascertainment for cancers diagnosed outside the screening facility, enabling calculation of sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rates [1]. External evidence from the Miglioretti et al. 2019 learning-curve study—which included co-author Sprague—demonstrates that DBT recall reduction is immediate and sustained, suggesting that any further recall change when switching from DM/DBT to s2D/DBT is attributable to the synthesized 2D image characteristics rather than general DBT learning [7]. The Tice et al. 2021 BCSC analysis similarly showed no meaningful difference in density assessment between DBT and DM [8], supporting the plausibility that observed density differences reflect the s2D reconstruction rather than the tomosynthesis acquisition.

**Statistical approach.** The proposal correctly adopts the Miglioretti-Heagerty GEE variance estimator for non-nested clustering of examinations within women, radiologists, and facilities [1]. This approach is specifically designed for settings where clustering sources are crossed rather than nested [9], and has been validated in BCSC practice and used in the Sprague et al. 2023 BCSC paper [10]. The use of GEE with a log link and Poisson distribution for binary outcomes, with adjustment for age, race/ethnicity, breast density, family history, and screening interval, follows established practice in this literature. All facilities adopt the BI-RADS fifth-edition performance definitions with a consistent 12-month cancer-outcome window [1].

**Power calculations and analytical under-specification.** Several material limitations constrain confidence in the rigor of the intended inferences. The power calculations lack transparency: the proposal states 80% power to detect absolute differences of 0.4 percentage points in recall, 0.8 per 1,000 in cancer detection, and 5.6 percentage points in sensitivity [1], but does not specify the working correlation structure, the intraclass correlation coefficients assumed, or how within-woman correlation across time periods—acknowledged to affect most women in the sample [1]—was incorporated into these calculations. The Miglioretti-Heagerty approach requires fitting separate GEE models with independent working correlation for each source of clustering and combining their variance estimates as V₁ + V₂ − V₃ [9]; the extension from two to three clustering sources (women, radiologists, facilities) is not described. Without these specifications, the projected detectable differences cannot be fully evaluated.

For rare outcomes, the concern is more acute. The detectable difference of 0.5 per 1,000 for DCIS is based on approximately 197 expected DCIS cases across both arms and represents a 29% relative change from the 1.7 per 1,000 baseline [1]. Detecting such a difference at 80% power with limited events strains credibility without explicit documentation of the variance assumptions. The proposal appropriately labels the lesion-specific sub-analyses by molecular subtype and benign histopathologic subcategory as exploratory [1], which is appropriate given the event counts.

**Temporal confounding.** The absence of concurrent controls—all facilities switched, with no comparison group continuing DM/DBT throughout the study period—leaves temporal confounding partially unaddressed. The analytical plan relies on covariate adjustment alone rather than modeling the temporal dimension explicitly [1]. Calendar year is not included as a variable, and there is no plan to model pre-existing temporal trends or test whether the transition represents a deviation from those trends. The staggered transition timing across seven facilities—Pennsylvania adopted in 2015, Vermont facilities at various dates [1]—is an underutilized asset that could support interrupted time-series analysis. External precedent exists: the migration from film to digital mammography in Australian screening was evaluated using segmented regression to separate the intervention effect from the underlying secular trend [11]. Incorporating such an approach, or at minimum including calendar time as a covariate and conducting falsification tests with pseudo-transition dates, would materially strengthen causal inference without requiring additional data collection.

On balance, the design and analytical framework are sound for the primary inference, but the under-specification of the statistical analysis plan and the reliance on covariate adjustment without temporal modeling represent correctable limitations that reduce confidence in the precision of the projected effect estimates and the study's ability to distinguish modality effects from secular trends.

## Feasibility

**The study is essentially ready to execute, with no critical technical, data-access, or resource dependency remaining unresolved.** The project uses existing, prospectively collected screening registries and linked pathology and cancer registry data; no new imaging acquisition, patient recruitment, or prospective data collection is required [1]. The before/after natural experiment is already completed: the seven participating facilities performed approximately 98,850 DM/DBT and 86,850 s2D/DBT examinations during 2011–2017 [1], and the workflow change—uniform adoption of s2D/DBT at each facility—occurred without research intervention [1].

The feasibility assessment rests on several concrete factors. Both centers have operated standardized data collection under the PROSPR consortium since 2011, with shared common data elements and established linkage infrastructure [1]. The Vermont pathology facilities provide standardized abstraction forms and reports for benign and malignant specimens; Pennsylvania obtains pathology electronically through Penn Medicine [1]. Both centers link to their respective state cancer registries for validated cancer diagnoses and stage, including cancers diagnosed outside the screening facility. The exam-level data align with BI-RADS, College of American Pathologists, and central cancer registry standards [1]. These are operational registries that have already supported multiple published analyses within this exact research group.

The team has already demonstrated the specific workflow required. The Zuckerman, Sprague et al. 2020 *Radiology* paper pooled and analyzed 151,363 screening examinations from the same two centers using the same before/after design, the same exposure definitions, the same Hologic equipment, the same 12-month outcome window, and the same GEE-based analytic approach [2]. The current proposal extends this pipeline to a slightly larger dataset (approximately 185,700 examinations) and adds four community-hospital facilities in Vermont that were not included in the 2020 analysis [1]. The incremental data assembly and analysis are well within the demonstrated capacity of this team.

The protocol includes appropriate data handling arrangements. The Pennsylvania exam-level dataset will be securely transferred to Vermont for pooling [1]. The exclusion criteria—women with a personal history of breast cancer and transitional examinations that combined conventional DM, DBT, and s2D—are clearly defined and can be applied programmatically to the existing data [1]. The exposure classification is binary and unambiguous: DM/DBT before the facility transition date and s2D/DBT after, with the transitional period excluded.

No critical dependency remains unaddressed. The data exist, the linkage systems are in place, the exposure classification requires no adjudication, and the analytical methods, while under-specified in some respects, are well-established and have been used by this team in prior publications. The study does not depend on external collaborators, new data-sharing agreements, or access to proprietary algorithms. The project is feasible as proposed and can be completed within the R03 timeframe and resources.

## Ethics and safety

The proposed study uses only existing clinical imaging data and linked registry records; it introduces no additional research radiation exposure and no direct participant contact, placing it well within the minimal-risk category for retrospective records-based research. The ethical and data-protection arrangements described are proportionate to the study's activities.

The study draws on routine clinical screening examinations already obtained for care, not research-acquired imaging. The proposal explicitly states that it does not introduce additional research radiation exposure [1], eliminating the primary physical risk that would require justification in a prospective imaging study. The remaining ethical considerations concern privacy, consent, and data security.

Vermont's PROSPR registry uses passive consent on its imaging questionnaire, with an opt-out checkbox; approximately 95% of eligible patients participate [1]. This approach satisfies the ethical requirement to inform participants and respect their autonomy while achieving near-complete population coverage, which minimizes selection bias in registry-based screening research. The University of Pennsylvania secured a waiver of informed consent for inclusion of women screened in its network [1]. This is ethically defensible for a retrospective records review where the research poses minimal risk, does not affect clinical care, and could not practicably be conducted if consent were required—conditions met here. The screening examinations already occurred, no patient is re-contacted, and the research question could not be answered without near-complete ascertainment of the screened population.

The data-security arrangements involve secure transfer of exam-level Pennsylvania data to Vermont for pooled analysis [1]. The two centers have an established collaboration under the PROSPR consortium with a track record of pooled data analysis [1]. The data elements are clinical and administrative (patient demographics, radiology reports, BI-RADS assessments, pathology results, cancer registry diagnoses) and do not appear to involve genomic data or other uniquely identifiable information that would elevate privacy risk beyond what is routinely managed in clinical data registries.

No animal subjects, environmental hazards, or biosafety concerns are implicated. The exclusion of women with a personal history of breast cancer [1] removes a group with distinct imaging protocols and clinical needs, which is methodologically appropriate. The study's findings will be relevant to the populations served by the participating centers [1], and the inclusion of both academic and community settings supports external validity without raising equity concerns.

The consent and data-governance approach is proportionate to the minimal risk, the retrospective design, and the public health value of the evidence. No material ethical or safety concern remains unresolved at the level of detail supplied in the proposal.

## Management

**The team combines all necessary scientific and organizational expertise, and the work plan is well-matched to the focused scope of an R03 mechanism. The proposal would benefit from clearer specification of the project timeline and individual responsibilities for the multi-site analytic workflow.**

The study's management strengths rest on three foundations. First, the team's expertise spans radiology, pathology, epidemiology, biostatistics, and multicenter PROSPR consortium operations [1]. The PI, Brian Sprague, is a co-author on the Miglioretti 2019 learning-curve paper [7], the Conant 2016 PROSPR DBT study [12], the Sprague 2016 radiologist density-variation study [13], and the Sprague 2019 BCSC density-trend paper [6]—all directly pertinent to the proposed analyses. This is a team that has executed closely analogous work and published it in high-impact journals.

Second, the data infrastructure already exists and has been used for pooled analysis. Both centers have operated standardized data collection under the PROSPR consortium since 2011, with shared common data elements [1]. The Zuckerman, Sprague et al. 2020 *Radiology* paper demonstrates that the exact workflow—pooling exam-level data from these two centers, applying uniform exclusion criteria, ascertaining 12-month outcomes, and running GEE models—has been successfully completed [2]. The current proposal extends this pipeline to a larger sample and adds four community-hospital facilities [1], but the operational challenge is one the team has already solved.

Third, the project's tasks are well-defined and limited. The data already exist [1]; no recruitment, imaging acquisition, or prospective follow-up is needed. The work consists of assembling eligible exam-level records, applying the exclusion criteria [1], pooling common data elements, establishing 12-month outcomes via existing linkage pathways [1], and completing three coordinated analyses [1]. These are focused analytic tasks appropriate to an R03 mechanism.

The management assessment is tempered by the absence of explicit detail about project coordination. The proposal describes the work to be done but does not specify a timeline with milestones, the division of analytic responsibilities between Vermont and Pennsylvania team members, or the procedures for quality control during data pooling and harmonization. The secure data transfer mechanism is mentioned but not described in operational terms. While these omissions are not decisive—the R03 mechanism implies a small, focused team where informal coordination is feasible, and the two centers have a track record of successful collaboration—more explicit specification would strengthen confidence in efficient multi-site workflow. The prior 2020 publication demonstrates that this team can deliver, which substantially mitigates the concern in practice.

Overall, the assembled expertise, existing data infrastructure, and clearly bounded tasks provide a strong basis for successful completion. The management limitations concern specification rather than capacity and do not threaten delivery of the stated aims.

# Project review

## Overall assessment

This R01 proposal from Drs. Gifford and Camarata aims to evaluate image-guided cochlear implant programming (IGCIP) in children aged 6–12 with prelingual deafness. IGCIP uses each child's cochlear anatomy and electrode location from CT imaging to selectively deactivate electrodes predicted to provide redundant or extracochlear stimulation, hypothetically reducing channel interaction and improving spatial selectivity. The most valuable expected contribution is the first randomized controlled evidence on whether this anatomically informed programming yields auditory benefit beyond optimized clinical care, and whether individual differences in auditory change relate to downstream growth in speech recognition, phonological awareness, language, and reading. The proposed double-blind, waitlist-control design with comprehensive longitudinal measurement across auditory, speech, and developmental domains would produce a uniquely rich dataset on the relationships between peripheral auditory processing and developmental outcomes in pediatric cochlear implant users.

The proposal's main strengths are its strong team with deep expertise in IGCIP and pediatric hearing, and the methodological rigor of a double-blind randomized design. The measurement battery—encompassing auditory resolution, speech recognition, language, phonological awareness, and reading—is among the most comprehensive ever proposed in pediatric CI research. The main limitations are substantial and, collectively, constrain the attainable contribution. First, converging evidence from this team's own published research shows that spectral resolution does not predict speech recognition in children with CIs, in contrast to the strong adult relationship, breaking the hypothesized mechanistic pathway from IGCIP to functional outcomes. Second, the study's preliminary data show that only 16–28% of children improved with IGCIP, with the majority unchanged, suggesting a small average effect that is realistically below the study's detectable d ≈ 0.67. Third, developmental outcomes are measured at only three time points, limiting the temporal resolution of the intended cross-lagged and mediation analyses. Fourth, the stated plan not to impute data expected to be missing nonrandomly introduces an unquantified risk of bias.

Weighing these factors, the proposal addresses a genuine evidence gap with methodological rigor and a uniquely qualified team. The RCT design is defensible for establishing IGCIP efficacy beyond optimized clinical programming. The comprehensive measurement battery will generate a valuable dataset on auditory-development relationships regardless of the primary outcome. However, the mismatch between the study's power and the realistically expected effect size, combined with the critical limitation that spectral resolution does not predict speech recognition in this population, leads to the conclusion that **the current proposal is insufficient for support**. A more targeted design—perhaps a smaller within-subject optimization study focused on identifying predictors of individual IGCIP response before committing to a fully powered RCT—would yield more definitive knowledge at this stage.

**Overall conclusion: The current proposal does not yet justify support**

## Scientific value

**The project addresses a clinically and scientifically important question, but the attainable knowledge is substantially constrained by converging evidence—much of it from the investigators’ own published work—that the core mechanistic pathway from IGCIP to improved developmental outcomes is not supported in children with cochlear implants.** This limitation narrows the contribution that the proposed design can realistically deliver.

**The question is well-motivated and the evidence gap is genuine.** Many early-implanted children continue to show persistent language and reading difficulties despite restoration of auditory access [1]. The quality of the electrical signal delivered by the implant may constrain subsequent development [1], and IGCIP offers a principled, anatomy-based method for altering the electrode–neural interface [1]. Establishing whether this intervention produces benefit beyond optimized clinical programming, and characterizing the relationships between peripheral auditory change and higher-order developmental outcomes, would fill an important gap: no randomized trial of IGCIP has been conducted in children, and the existing evidence base consists entirely of single-group pre-post or within-subject crossover studies [2] [3]. A positive result from a rigorous RCT would motivate clinical adoption; even a null result on the primary auditory outcome, combined with the proposed pathway analyses, would generate a uniquely rich longitudinal dataset on auditory–developmental relationships in this population.

**However, the value of that dataset depends on the interpretability of the pathway analyses, and here the proposal faces a critical limitation.** The hypothesized chain is: IGCIP → improved spectral/spatial selectivity → improved speech recognition → improved language/literacy. The investigators’ own published research has shown that spectral modulation detection does not predict speech recognition in children with CIs, in contrast to the strong, consistent relationship in adults. In Gifford et al. (2018), the correlation between spectral modulation detection and word recognition was r = 0.30 (p = 0.07) in 36 pediatric CI users, compared with r = 0.52 (p \< 0.0001) in 477 postlingual adults and r = 0.51 (p \< 0.0001) in 65 prelingual adults [4]; for sentence recognition in quiet, the pediatric correlation was r = 0.09 (p = 0.61) [4]. The authors concluded that “pediatric CI recipients might not depend upon spectral resolution for speech understanding in the same manner as adult CI recipients” and “may be making use of different cues, such as those contained within the temporal envelope” [4].

DeFreese et al. (2024), from the same team with a larger cohort of 47 children, confirmed this null finding after Bonferroni correction: neither spectral nor temporal resolution was significantly correlated with any speech recognition measure [5]. More recently, Camarata et al. (2026) studied 47 prelingually deaf children with CIs and found that phonological processing abilities fell significantly below age-expected levels (CTOPP-2 Blending Words M = −1.55 SD; Nonword Repetition M = −2.33 SD), yet receptive language and reading comprehension were often within the typical range and significantly above phonological processing levels [6]; language ability alone, not phonological processing, was a significant predictor of reading comprehension [6]. The authors concluded that these children may use “alternate pathways of skill acquisition” and that “spectral differences inherent to CI signal processing did not prevent many of these children from acquiring typical language and reading comprehension levels” [6].

**These findings do not render the proposed study valueless, but they substantially constrain the attainable contribution.** The comprehensive measurement battery—encompassing auditory resolution, speech recognition, language, phonological awareness, and reading [1]—will generate descriptive data on developmental trajectories in pediatric CI users that are of genuine scientific interest. The inclusion of temporal modulation tasks at multiple rates [1] is an appropriate response to the uncertainty about which auditory dimensions matter for children. But if spectral resolution does not predict speech recognition in this population, then IGCIP-driven improvements in spectral resolution cannot be expected to translate through the hypothesized pathway to language and literacy benefits. The pathway analyses, in the likely event of a null or small between-group effect on auditory outcomes, would describe associational relationships among variables rather than test the causal mechanism that the proposal’s conceptual framework posits. Such descriptive contributions are legitimate but more modest than the proposal’s stated aims.

## Innovation

**The proposal’s main innovations are the first double-blind randomized trial of IGCIP in children and a uniquely comprehensive longitudinal measurement framework linking auditory resolution to developmental outcomes. A further conceptual innovation lies in testing whether children rely on temporal rather than spectral cues for speech recognition—a hypothesis that challenges the adult-derived mechanistic model. These original elements are genuine, but their impact is constrained because the core mechanism the trial is designed to test has already been undermined by the investigators’ own published findings.**

**The most straightforward contribution is the controlled experimental evidence.** All prior IGCIP studies in children are single-group pre-post or within-subject crossover designs. Noble et al. (2016) tested 21 ears of 18 children aged 5–17 and found statistically significant group-level improvement on word and sentence recognition with the IGCIP map, but the magnitude was not reported in percentage points [2]. The 2022 meta-analysis by Yang et al. identified only seven IGCIP studies, none randomized, and reported nominal effect sizes whose confidence intervals all included zero: CNC words d = 0.15 (95% CI −0.12 to 0.42), AzBio quiet d = 0.09 (−0.05 to 0.22), BKB-SIN d = −0.11 (−0.35 to 0.12) [7]. The broader 2025 systematic review of electrode-neural interface-informed remapping by Lien et al. found only one RCT among 30 included studies [3]; the site-selection subgroup—IGCIP’s closest analogue—yielded a moderate pooled effect of d = 0.59 (95% CI 0.18 to 1, p = 0.005) but with substantial heterogeneity [3]. The proposed double-blind, waitlist-control design with 72 children randomly assigned to immediate versus 12-month-deferred IGCIP [1] would therefore provide the first controlled estimate of whether this anatomically guided approach offers benefit beyond optimized clinical programming. That is a genuine advance in the evidence base.

**A second innovation is the analytic framework that treats the intervention as a source of controlled variation for probing auditory–developmental pathways.** Rather than stopping at whether IGCIP works, the design uses within-subject change in spectral, temporal, and spectrotemporal resolution to predict downstream growth in phonological awareness, language, and reading, via cross-lagged and path models [1]. This integration of a randomized intervention with a developmental cascade framework—measuring auditory resolution, speech recognition, language, PA, and literacy simultaneously across 24 months—has not previously been attempted in this population. The measurement battery itself is among the most comprehensive ever assembled in a pediatric CI study: psychoacoustic tasks spanning spectral modulation detection (0.5, 1.0 cyc/oct), temporal modulation detection (4, 32, 128 Hz), and spectrotemporal combinations [1]; speech recognition in quiet, fixed-noise, and adaptive-noise conditions using multiple instruments and nonword repetition [1]; standardized receptive and expressive language measures (ROWPVT-4, PPVT-4, TACL-4, CELF-4, EOWPVT-4, SPELT-3) [1]; PA measures (CTOPP-2, TAPS-3 plus a developmentally ordered supplementary battery) [1]; reading assessment (WRMT-III, GORT-5) [1]; working memory (Woodcock-Johnson IV Numbers Reversed and study-specific touchscreen tasks) [1]; acoustic speech-production analyses [1]; and quality-of-life questionnaires [1].

**A further conceptual contribution is the explicit test of whether children’s auditory processing relies on temporal-envelope cues rather than the spectral resolution that drives adult performance.** The proposal’s own preliminary data show that spectral modulation detection was not associated with speech recognition in 41 pediatric participants, unlike findings in 542 adults [1]; a small temporal-modulation pilot (n = 14) suggested associations with word and sentence recognition, and a five-child pre/post dataset suggested an association between temporal and word-recognition benefit. The inclusion of temporal modulation detection at multiple rates and spectrotemporal tasks [1] is designed to determine which auditory dimensions support children’s speech understanding and whether IGCIP alters them. This hypothesis—that the effective auditory cue differs between children and adults—is original and clinically consequential.

**However, the proposal’s own published work substantially constrains the impact of these innovations.** The null relationship between spectral resolution and speech recognition in children [4], confirmed in a larger sample [5], directly challenges the mechanistic chain that motivates the trial. Similarly, the finding that phonological processing deficits do not align with language and reading outcomes as predicted by standard developmental models [6] weakens the rationale for expecting IGCIP-driven auditory improvements to cascade through these domains. The proposal is therefore innovative in an ironic sense: it may demonstrate that the very mechanism it was designed to leverage does not operate in children as it does in adults. That is a legitimate scientific contribution—establishing the absence of an expected relationship can redirect the field—but it means the trial’s primary conceptual framework is being tested against evidence that, from this team’s own laboratory, already points toward its rejection. A smaller, more intensive study designed explicitly to characterize which auditory dimensions predict children’s outcomes and which children benefit from IGCIP might, at this stage, yield more definitive mechanistic knowledge.

## Rigor

**The double-blind, randomized waitlist-control design is a methodological strength that provides the first controlled estimate of IGCIP efficacy in children, but three interconnected limitations—power for a realistically small average effect, measurement density for the developmental outcomes, and the missing-data strategy—collectively constrain the causal and mechanistic inferences the study can deliver.**

**The randomized design is well-conceived.** Children are assigned to immediate IGCIP versus IGCIP deferred for 12 months, with both arms receiving identical imaging, IGCIP plan generation, and optimized clinical maps at baseline [1]. The group-by-time interaction over months 2–12 estimates the effect of IGCIP relative to optimized clinical programming—the clinically relevant comparison. The assessor-blind allocation [1], baseline map optimization to verifiable CI-aided thresholds of 20–25 dB HL from 250–6,000 Hz with a minimum two-month stabilization period [1], and equipment checks at every visit [1] strengthen internal validity. The deferred arm's pre-post contrast after crossover at 12 months provides a useful descriptive supplement, though the proposal correctly does not present it as a substitute for the between-group comparison [1].

The waitlist-control design does, however, produce a pragmatic treatment effect that confounds the specific physiological mechanism of electrode deactivation with expectancy, motivation, or disappointment effects. Because the deferred arm knows it is waiting, the between-group contrast estimates “IGCIP plus knowing you received it” versus “no IGCIP plus knowing you are waiting.” Meta-analytic evidence from psychotherapy research demonstrates that waitlist-controlled trials produce systematically larger effects than care-as-usual designs, driven by suppressed improvement in the waitlist arm rather than inflated treatment response [8]. In the present context, the deferred arm continues using an optimized clinical map—itself a high-quality active intervention—so the waitlist contrast estimates the *incremental* benefit of IGCIP, which may be substantially smaller than an effect against suboptimal baseline programming.

**The power calculation presents a significant tension.** With 30 analyzed participants per arm, the study is powered at 80% (two-sided α = 0.05) to detect a between-group effect of d ≈ 0.67–0.74 [1]. These figures appear to be derived from a conventional two-group comparison with baseline adjustment; they do not specify how power was calculated for the intended multilevel models with group-by-time interactions, which depend on the number and spacing of time points, within-subject correlation, and the shape of the hypothesized trajectory. The proposal’s own preliminary data in 41 children show that only 16% improved significantly in quiet speech recognition, 79% showed no change, and 5% worsened; in noise, 28% improved, 69% were unchanged, and 3% worsened [1]. For the 19 children with BKB-SIN data, the mean improvement was approximately 1.2 dB SNR in the experimental ear and 1.4 dB bilateral [1]. This distribution—a minority of responders, modest mean shifts—is consistent with the meta-analytic evidence showing nominal effect sizes near zero for speech recognition outcomes [7]. The empirical distribution of effect sizes in hearing intervention trials places d = 0.67 well above the 75th percentile of published effects (Hedges’ g = 0.48) [9]. The study is therefore realistically powered to detect an average treatment effect that the proposal’s own data suggest is unlikely to materialize.

**The fallback position—that pathway analyses are “not contingent on a significant average intervention effect” [1]—is logically coherent but carries interpretive consequences.** With a significant between-group effect, the randomized assignment serves as an instrumental variable: group assignment causes auditory change, and downstream associations can be attributed to that auditory pathway. Without one, the pathway analyses become observational: children who happen to show more auditory improvement, for whatever reason (IGCIP responsiveness, maturation, motivation, measurement error), may also show more language growth, but those individual differences are confounded by all stable child characteristics that jointly influence both domains [1]. The cross-lagged models then estimate Granger-causal associations—whether past auditory change predicts future language change above and beyond past language—but not causal effects [10].

**A further constraining factor is the measurement density for the developmental outcomes.** Language, phonological awareness, and literacy are assessed at only three time points: baseline, 12 months, and 24 months [1]. This is the minimum for a cross-lagged panel model and permits estimation of autoregressive and cross-lagged paths between adjacent waves, but only across a single pair of 12-month intervals. The design cannot test whether hypothesized temporal precedence is stable across different developmental windows or is an artifact of the specific spacing chosen. With only three waves, the random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM)—now recognized as necessary to separate stable between-person differences from within-person dynamics—is just-identified for the within-person parameters, leaving no degrees of freedom to test model fit for the dynamic portion [11]. The proposal does not distinguish between-person from within-person effects, and the standard cross-lagged panel model it plans to use conflates these sources of variance, potentially inflating cross-lagged estimates [12]. Mediation analyses requiring temporal ordering of mediator and outcome are similarly constrained: with PA, language, and reading assessed simultaneously at each wave, the design cannot establish whether PA changes precede reading changes within the same 12-month interval. For the path analyses, N = 60 permits detection of a path correlation near 0.35 [1], but in a model with multiple endogenous variables and covariates, the effective sample size for model estimation is reduced; a model with 15–20 freely estimated parameters yields a subject-to-parameter ratio of only 3:1 to 4:1. Bootstrapping provides valid confidence intervals but does not increase power.

**The stated approach to missing data introduces an unquantified risk of bias.** The proposal expects most missing assessments to be nonrandom and does not plan imputation for these cases [1]. Attrition of roughly 17% is projected (72 enrolled to 60 analyzed) [1]. Nonrandom missingness—for example, children with poorer auditory outcomes being differentially lost to follow-up, or families with lower socioeconomic status being harder to retain—cannot be remedied by bootstrapping, which provides valid standard errors only under the assumption that the observed data are representative. The specific risk is that children who benefit less from IGCIP, or who find the experimental map unpleasant and restore their clinical map [1], may be less likely to attend subsequent visits. If these children are differentially removed from the analytic sample, the estimated treatment effect and path coefficients would be biased upward. The proposal’s plan to examine patterns of missingness descriptively [1] is a useful step but does not constitute a sensitivity analysis for the primary estimands under missing-not-at-random assumptions.

## Feasibility

**The project is largely feasible given the team’s established infrastructure, extensive preliminary work, and access to a large clinical population, but the feasibility of the primary between-group comparison is undermined by the mismatch between the study’s power and the realistically expected effect size.**

**The feasibility argument rests on several strong foundations.** The investigators have already completed a single-group IGCIP study in 41 pediatric CI users aged 4.4–17.9 years [1], demonstrating that they can recruit, image, generate IGCIP plans, and collect auditory and speech recognition outcomes in this population. Of 23 children followed for one year, 16 retained the map and showed continued group-level improvement [1]. The image-processing pipeline—atlas-based co-registration of pre- and post-operative CT scans using ten micro-CT scans of human cadaveric cochleae, electrode localization, and distance-versus-frequency analysis [1]—is operational and has been used across multiple published studies from this group [2] [13]. The clinical infrastructure at Vanderbilt provides access to a large pediatric implant program, and the proposal’s staggered recruitment plan targeting summer and winter breaks [1] is a practical accommodation to family schedules. The projected enrollment of 72 children to yield 60 analyzed participants [1]—approximately 17% attrition over 24 months—is optimistic but within the range achievable by an experienced team at a major center with dedicated research staff and a project manager [1].

**The measurement battery, while ambitious, is supported by established procedures.** The auditory resolution tasks use child-friendly three-interval forced-choice procedures with cartoon images and touchscreen responses [1]. The speech recognition battery employs instruments with established pediatric norms and multiple test lists to permit repeated administration without item-list repetition [1]. The language, PA, and literacy measures are standardized instruments already used by this team in published work [1]. The home word-recognition check via smartphone [1] adds a practical monitoring component without requiring an additional clinic visit.

**The critical feasibility concern is that the between-group comparison is realistically underpowered to detect the average treatment effect that is likely to materialize.** The preliminary data show that 79% of children were unchanged in quiet and 69% unchanged in noise after IGCIP [1]; the mean BKB-SIN improvement was approximately 1.2–1.4 dB SNR [1]. These modest mean shifts, combined with the meta-analytic evidence showing nominal effect sizes near zero for IGCIP on speech recognition outcomes [7], suggest an average effect well below the d ≈ 0.67 the study is powered to detect. The 2025 meta-analysis of electrode-neural interface-informed remapping found a pooled d = 0.59 for site-selection interventions, but this estimate comes predominantly from within-subject crossover studies, not randomized comparisons [3]; randomized effects are typically smaller.

This mismatch does not render the study infeasible—the planned path analyses can proceed regardless of the primary outcome, and the dataset will retain descriptive value—but it means the project’s central confirmatory test is unlikely to yield a statistically significant result. The proposal’s fallback of relying on individual-difference analyses [1] is a realistic acknowledgment of this constraint, but it also means the study’s scientific contribution will shift from a causal test of IGCIP efficacy toward an observational characterization of auditory–developmental relationships, with the interpretive limitations that observational analyses entail at N = 60.

## Ethics and safety

**The ethics and safety framework is well-considered and proportionate to the risks involved, though two procedural details merit institutional review before enrollment.**

**Postoperative imaging.** The proposal adopts a tiered approach that prioritizes existing clinically obtained CT scans; only children whose postoperative imaging is unavailable receive research imaging through a linked, separately approved low-dose protocol [1]. Published dosimetry confirms that modern pediatric temporal bone CT involves very low radiation exposure. Steiniger et al. (2022) measured effective doses of approximately 0.2 mSv for a high-end low-dose CT protocol and 0.6 mSv for a mid-range scanner in a 1-year-old anthropomorphic phantom, against an annual natural background of roughly 3 mSv [14]. Spahn et al. (2025) further demonstrated that photon-counting detector CT with dedicated low-dose protocols achieves 82–87% dose reduction (to 3.43 mGy CTDI) while maintaining accuracy for pre- and postoperative cochlear analysis [15]. The proposal’s statement that “radiation oversight and institutional review apply” [1] appropriately delegates the verification of specific dose parameters. The risk–benefit calculus is favorable: children have already undergone cochlear implant surgery with its attendant imaging, and the incremental radiation from a single low-dose temporal bone CT is very small in exchange for the anatomical information essential to generating the IGCIP plan.

**Risk of reduced speech recognition.** The available evidence indicates this risk is real but modest and manageable. The proposal’s own preliminary data showed that in the experimental ear, 5% of children worsened in quiet and 3% in noise; in the bilateral aided condition—the listening mode used in daily life—no child showed a significant decrement [1]. In the adult prelingual IGCIP study, 76–90% of subjects demonstrated equivocal or significant improvement at the individual level [13]. The Lien et al. (2025) meta-analysis of 30 electrode-neural interface-informed remapping studies found that speech-in-noise and speech-in-quiet performance improve concurrently (r = 0.89, p \< 0.001), not at each other’s expense [3]; site-selection interventions showed a positive pooled effect for both speech in noise (d = 0.59, p = 0.005) and speech in quiet (d = 0.29, p = 0.036) [3].

The monitoring plan contains several well-designed safeguards. Baseline clinical maps are optimized to CI-aided thresholds of 20–25 dB HL from 250–6,000 Hz before study entry, with a minimum two-month wait after any optimization, ensuring subsequent changes are attributable to IGCIP rather than to a suboptimal clinical map [1]. Equipment and listening checks occur at every study visit [1]. Smartphone-based word-recognition testing is performed in clinic at baseline and 12 months, with caregivers repeating testing at home one month later; a decline exceeding the 95% test-retest interval triggers the option to restore the prior clinical map or continue for one additional month until the next scheduled visit [1]. The IGCIP plan aims to retain more than eight active electrodes [1], placing a floor on the extent of deactivation and ensuring a minimally functional number of channels remains active.

Two aspects warrant IRB attention. First, the one-month interval between the programming change and the home word-recognition check means a child experiencing a decline could have a suboptimal map for up to one month. The IRB should weigh this duration against the child’s dependence on the CI for classroom communication and safety. Second, the proposal does not specify whether caregivers can request map restoration between scheduled monitoring points if they observe concerning changes. Both are solvable matters of protocol detail rather than fundamental ethical flaws.

**Waitlist control and consent.** The waitlist design is ethically justified. All participants in both arms receive an optimized clinical map meeting verified audibility thresholds before baseline [1]; the deferred arm continues with high-quality standard care, not an untreated condition. Genuine equipoise exists: only 16–28% of children showed measurable benefit in preliminary testing [1], and in the adult study only 72% chose to retain the IGCIP map [13]. The 12-month deferral period is comparable to waitlist designs used in other pediatric habilitation trials [16]. The knowledge gained from the controlled comparison—distinguishing IGCIP effects from maturation and repeated testing—cannot be obtained from an uncontrolled design. Parents provide consent and children assent under institutional procedures [1]; the commitment to provide families with reports of their children’s assessments [1] supports the principle of respect for participants.

## Management

**The project’s management is well-structured, drawing on an established interdisciplinary team, a realistic operational schedule, and the resources of a major pediatric implant center. The main coordination challenge lies in the breadth of the scientific agenda rather than in any deficiency of leadership, expertise, or institutional support.**

**Leadership and team.** The dual-PI leadership pairs Gifford (auditory perception and recruitment) with Camarata (speech, language, and literacy), and the team further comprises otology, image analysis and engineering, biostatistics, and dedicated consultants in phonological processing and speech production [1]. This group has a demonstrated track record of collaborative publication spanning the precise domains the proposal integrates—IGCIP development, pediatric audiological outcomes, and developmental language and reading research [2] [6] [4]. A project manager and trained research staff will coordinate the seven-visit, 24-month schedule and data management, with monthly team meetings throughout the study [1]. The division of oversight responsibilities is clearly specified, and the inclusion of a statistician responsible for allocation generation and blinded analyses reinforces methodological integrity.

**Schedule and recruitment.** The five-year schedule is realistic for the intended scope [1]. Year 1 covers staff hiring, assessment acquisition, trial registration, randomization generation, and recruitment of approximately 12–14 children during summer and 5–6 during winter break. The staggered cohort design—with recruitment concentrated around school holidays—is a practical accommodation to family availability in a pediatric longitudinal study. Subsequent years interleave recruitment of new cohorts with continuing follow-up of earlier entrants, allowing the team to accumulate the target 72 enrolled participants while completing the full two-year sequence for each child. The timeline correctly builds in periods for blinded analysis, consultant review, and manuscript preparation during years 3–4, with integrated cross-aim analyses and dissemination concentrated in year 5. Vanderbilt’s large pediatric implant program provides an adequate recruitment base, and the investigators’ preliminary work confirms their ability to enroll, image, and retain children with CIs in IGCIP research—41 participants in the published single-group study, with 23 followed for one year [1].

**Coordination risk.** The principal management risk is the project’s ambitious interdisciplinary scope, which requires tight integration across auditory psychoacoustics, speech recognition, standardized language and literacy assessment, acoustic speech-production analysis, and advanced longitudinal statistical modeling. The assembled expertise covers each domain, but the success of the integrated pathway analyses depends on sustained coordination across these specialties over five years, with data streams that must remain aligned across multiple cohorts and assessment waves. The monthly meeting schedule and the project manager role are appropriate mechanisms for maintaining this integration, though the proposal’s documentation of specific data-flow and quality-control procedures across the auditory and developmental arms is limited to general statements about REDCap and a centralized repository [1]. This does not indicate incapacity, but it means the management assessment rests primarily on the team’s established collaborative record rather than on a detailed operational data-management plan. Given that record, confidence in the team’s ability to execute the proposed work is reasonably high.

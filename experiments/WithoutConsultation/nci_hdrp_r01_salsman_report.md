# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project uses the Multiphase Optimization Strategy (MOST) to decompose EMPOWER—a web-based positive-emotion regulation intervention—into five components: noticing positive events and gratitude, mindfulness, positive reappraisal, personal strengths and achievable goals, and acts of kindness. In a full factorial experiment, 352 young adult cancer survivors within five years of treatment will be randomized to one of 32 combinations, each component present or absent, allowing independent estimation of component main effects on positive affect, life satisfaction, depression, and anxiety. The study also explores mediation through coping self-efficacy and social support and moderation by age and gender. The most valuable expected knowledge is an empirically identified set of effective components, enabling assembly of a leaner, better-understood intervention package tailored to an underserved population with documented unmet psychosocial needs.

The strongest supporting evidence includes the extensive prior package-level trials showing multicomponent positive-emotion interventions can improve well-being across clinical populations, establishing the intervention family's promise; the team's pilot work adapting EMPOWER for young adult survivors, demonstrating feasibility and acceptability in small single-arm waves; published precedent for factorial optimization trials (e.g., IMPROVE-2) using similar designs, alpha levels, and analytic approaches; and the clear theoretical grounding in Broaden-and-Build and Revised Stress and Coping theories. The proposal acknowledges that the study may miss smaller interactions and that inert control content could attenuate effect estimates. The most material limitation is recruitment risk: pilot yields of 9.7–23.2% and external benchmarks of 7–10% for hospital-based AYA recruitment suggest the 13% conservative rate may be optimistic; if realized at the pilot rate, enrollment would fall short of the 352 target.

Weighing these elements together, the current proposal provides a defensible route to valuable scientific knowledge. The factorial optimization strategy is well-suited to the question and addresses a gap that package-level trials cannot fill. The primary risks are operational: recruitment and retention may fall short of the levels needed to distinguish moderately effective components with confidence. The conservative design choices protect against over-interpretation but may leave the optimization less decisive than expected. On balance, the scientific question, the methodological fit, and the team's qualifications outweigh these manageable risks. With close attention to recruitment performance and an explicit adaptive plan, the project can deliver an optimized intervention profile that advances both AYA survivorship care and the broader methodology of behavioral intervention optimization.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

**The project addresses a valuable and well-motivated question: which specific emotion‑regulation skills contribute to psychological well‑being in young adult cancer survivors, an underserved group with documented unmet psychosocial needs.** The attainable knowledge goes beyond confirming that bundled positive‑emotion interventions work—a point already established across HIV, diabetes, dementia‑caregiving and metastatic breast‑cancer samples [1]—by isolating the independent contribution of each of five components (positive events/gratitude, mindfulness, reappraisal, strengths/goals, and acts of kindness) [1]. This component‑level evidence is precisely the gap identified in prior work: the Vivibot trial in young adult cancer survivors explicitly called for understanding “the effects of individual positive emotion intervention skills” after finding no clear effect of the full bundle on depression or positive affect [2]. The EMPOWER project therefore supplies knowledge that none of the completed package‑level trials can produce.

Three features of the design strengthen the scientific value. First, the 2⁵ full factorial experiment enables estimation of each component’s main effect by comparing all 176 participants assigned the component with all 176 not assigned it [1] [3], so the study efficiently screens a five‑component space with a single cohort. Second, the proposal measures four primary outcomes (positive affect, life satisfaction, depression, anxiety) using PROMIS and NIH Toolbox computer‑adaptive tests [1], providing sensitive, standardised detection of change across a diagnostically heterogeneous population. Third, the mediator analyses—coping self‑efficacy and social support, grounded in Broaden‑and‑Build Theory [1]—offer a plausible mechanistic account that, while constrained by the study’s power for mediation and limited to components judged effective [1], can guide theory refinement and later tailoring. The LEAF dementia‑caregiving trial demonstrated that increases in positive emotion mediated depression reduction [4], and the current project extends that logic to specific component‑by‑mediator pathways in an AYA cancer context where no such evidence yet exists.

The value of the knowledge is practical as well as conceptual. By applying the MOST framework, the study will produce an empirically selected component set that can be shorter, reducing participant burden; potentially more effective, by removing inert or antagonistic components; and transparent about which skills matter—all features that strengthen subsequent implementation and training [1]. The deferred confirmatory trial, planned for a later R01 [1], is the correct evaluation phase for the assembled package and does not diminish the knowledge gained at this optimization stage.

The contribution is bounded in two main ways. First, the detectable interaction threshold of d = 0.62 [1] means the study will miss all but the largest synergistic or antagonistic combinations; the IMPROVE‑2 factorial trial similarly found no significant interactions among seven CBT components [5], so the practical loss may be modest, but the project will not fully characterise how components work together. Second, the core skills themselves are drawn from established positive‑psychology interventions already tested in other populations [1]; the advance lies in component‑level decomposition and AYA‑survivor application rather than in wholly new therapeutic techniques. These limits are consistent with a rigorous optimization phase and do not undermine the genuine increment the project offers over the existing literature.

## Innovation

**The project offers a meaningful increment over the existing evidence base, principally by shifting the question from “does the package work?” to “which components contribute, and for whom?”** The five skills—noticing positive events/gratitude, mindfulness, positive reappraisal, personal strengths/achievable goals, and acts of kindness—are drawn from established positive‑psychology techniques that have been combined in prior interventions [1]. What is novel is not the skills themselves but the systematic decomposition: no prior study in any clinical population has used a full factorial design to estimate the independent main effects of these five components on well‑being outcomes.

Prior trials tested bundled packages. Across HIV (IRISS), type‑2 diabetes (DAHLIA), dementia caregiving (LEAF), and metastatic breast cancer (LILAC), the designs could determine whether the whole intervention outperformed a control but could not identify which skills drove the effect, which were inert, or which combinations were synergistic or antagonistic [1]. The Vivibot chatbot trial delivered eight positive‑psychology skills as a bundle to young adult cancer survivors and found anxiety reduction but no significant effects on depression or positive affect; the authors concluded that “additional work should be done to understand the effects of individual positive emotion intervention skills” [2]. The EMPOWER project directly addresses that call.

The population application contributes a second layer of originality. Young adults diagnosed during ages 15–39 face cancer during education, career entry, relationship formation, and family planning—developmental tasks distinct from those of the older adults, dementia caregivers, and newly diagnosed HIV participants studied previously [1]. Recent work confirms that AYA cancer survivors are more likely to experience elevated depressive symptoms than older survivors and healthy age‑matched peers, yet digital interventions tailored to their needs remain scarce [6] [7]. The proposal’s adaptation process—revising language, examples, and images with survivor and clinical stakeholder input [1]—moves beyond simple repackaging. The co‑design literature in this population reinforces the value of such tailoring: AYA survivors prioritise authentic peer stories, brief sessions, and an encouraging tone [7], features that EMPOWER’s web‑based, five‑week, diary‑style format is designed to accommodate [1].

A third element of originality lies in the exploratory mediator analyses. Although positive emotion has been shown to mediate depression reduction in LEAF [4], the proposed pathway through coping self‑efficacy and social support—examined separately for each retained component—has not been tested in AYA cancer survivors. The moderator analyses by age and gender extend the IRISS finding that participant characteristics moderated intervention effects on antidepressant use [8], applying that logic to a younger, post‑treatment oncology population.

The boundaries of the innovation are also clear. The core skills are well‑established in the broader positive‑psychology literature [1], and the web‑based delivery format follows precedents including LILAC [1] and Vivibot [2]. The MOST framework itself is a published methodology with successful applications such as IMPROVE‑2 [5] [9], and the use of α = 0.10 at the screening phase is standard practice [3]. The contribution therefore lies in the novel combination of question, design, and population—the first systematic decomposition of a positive‑emotion skills intervention in young adult cancer survivors—rather than in fundamentally new therapeutic techniques or methods. This is a well‑judged scope for an R01‑level optimization study: the originality is sufficient to produce knowledge that the field does not yet have, while the established methodological and empirical foundations make the work credible and deliverable.

## Rigor

**The factorial optimization design is methodologically appropriate for a MOST screening phase, but the gap between the 0.31 detectable effect and the 0.50 retention criterion creates a consequential ambiguity, and the underpowered interaction detection limits characterisation of combined component effects.**

The 2⁵ full factorial design allocates 352 participants across 32 combinations (11 per condition), yet each component’s main effect is estimated by comparing all 176 participants who receive it with all 176 who do not [1]. This is the core efficiency highlighted by Watkins and Newbold (2020): “all participants are involved in every effect estimate—it effectively recycles each participant” [3]. The IMPROVE‑2 trial used the same logic to test seven CBT components in 767 participants [5], and the MOST framework has been extensively validated for intervention optimization [9].

The analytic approach is sound. An ANCOVA model adjusts each follow‑up score for baseline and includes the five component indicators, with longitudinal mixed‑effects models employing person‑level random effects and an autoregressive covariance structure for repeated observations [1]. PROMIS and NIH Toolbox computer‑adaptive tests provide sensitive, standardised primary outcomes [1]. The choice of α = 0.10 is well‑justified for a screening phase: as Watkins and Newbold note, it is “recommended for component selection experiments to decrease the relative risk of Type II to Type I error when selecting treatment components; i.e., to avoid prematurely ruling out potentially active treatment components” [3].

**The central analytic tension lies between the detectable and retainable effects.** With the stated 72% retention (n = 256 at one month), the design detects a main effect of d = 0.31 at 80% power (95% interval 0.06–0.56) [1]. The retention criterion, however, is d = 0.50. This creates a zone where a component with a true effect of d = 0.35–0.45—comparable to the LEAF positive‑affect effect of d = 0.41 or IRISS at d = 0.30 [1]—could be detected as statistically significant at α = 0.10 yet fail the retention threshold and be discarded. The proposal does not specify whether the 0.50 criterion is a hard cut‑off or whether a statistically significant but modest effect might still be retained on grounds of low burden, positive interactions, or strong mechanistic evidence. Without such guidance, the optimization risks an inconclusive profile where few or no components clearly exceed the threshold.

**Interaction detection is substantially underpowered.** The minimum detectable interaction is d = 0.62 (95% interval 0.37–0.87), approximately twice the main‑effect threshold [1]. The IMPROVE‑2 trial, with N = 767, found no significant interactions among seven CBT components [5], suggesting that interactions in behavioural interventions may often be small. The current study would miss most plausible synergistic or antagonistic effects, a limitation the proposal acknowledges [1] but which nevertheless constrains the ambition to identify which components work best together.

**The inert control content entails acknowledged risk of attenuation.** Absent components are replaced with “factual journaling and reading about nutrition or stress responses” [1]. The proposal transparently acknowledges that “if this content itself improves well‑being, estimated EMPOWER effects would be smaller” [1]. The direction of bias is toward the null, making the design conservative—the correct direction for a screening phase, since components that survive attenuation are likely robust. However, combined with the gap between the detectable effect and the retention criterion, this attenuation further increases the risk of discarding genuinely useful components with modest but meaningful effects.

**The 72% retention assumption warrants caution.** Pilot evidence is mixed: Wave 1 at Northwestern achieved 72% retention (13 of 18 enrolled) [1]; Wave 2 at Wake Forest reported 66.7% with 11 participants still on study [1]; the LILAC pilot achieved 79% at one‑month follow‑up [1]. All three estimates rest on very small samples, and Wave 2’s 23.2% enrollment rate suggests a selective sample that could inflate retention relative to a larger, more representative trial. If retention falls to 60% instead of 72%, the analytic sample drops from 256 to approximately 211, reducing power below 80% for the stated detectable effect.

Taken together, these features produce a conservatively designed optimization that is methodologically defensible but faces a real risk of emerging with an ambiguous component profile—where few components unambiguously exceed the 0.50 threshold and interactions remain uncharacterised. For a screening phase that feeds a subsequent confirmatory trial, this conservatism is preferable to the opposite error of retaining ineffective components, but the combination of the retention‑criterion gap, underpowered interactions, potential control‑content attenuation, and uncertain retention means the informativeness of the final component selection is not guaranteed.

## Feasibility

**The web‑based delivery format is a structural strength for feasibility, and the two‑site infrastructure is adequate, but recruitment projections are optimistic relative to the pilot evidence and external benchmarks, creating a material risk that enrollment will fall short of the 352 target.**

The Wake Forest Research Information Systems Unit will host the intervention, and the two comprehensive cancer centers (Wake Forest Baptist and Northwestern’s Lurie) provide established recruitment infrastructure with dedicated coordinators and EHR‑based registry screening [1]. The intervention itself has been piloted in two single‑arm waves, demonstrating that the adapted content, weekly structure, and online platform function as designed—participants completed lessons, reported home practice, and provided assessment data [1]. The LILAC pilot similarly confirmed that online delivery produces trajectories comparable to in‑person delivery for this intervention family [1]. These operational elements are credible.

**Recruitment yield is the principal concern.** The proposal estimates approximately 2,850 potentially eligible survivors over three years based on roughly 950 post‑treatment survivors seen annually at the two sites, and applies a 13% recruitment rate to reach the 352 target [1]. Three lines of evidence suggest this rate is optimistic.

Pilot performance sets a lower benchmark. Wave 1 at Northwestern enrolled 18 of 185 potentially eligible survivors—a 9.7% yield, not 13% [1]. Wave 2 at Wake Forest enrolled 23 from 99 contacted (23.2%), but this denominator reflects contacted individuals from mixed community and online sources, not the broader potentially eligible pool that registry‑based recruitment would identify [1]. Neither wave used the factorial design, 32‑condition randomization, or five‑week intervention now proposed; each adds participant burden that may further suppress enrollment willingness.

External benchmarks reinforce the concern. Benedict et al. (2019) reported a 7.3% enrollment rate from EMR‑identified patients through hospital‑based AYA recruitment (21 enrolled from 289 identified as eligible), compared to 37% from social media where participants self‑identified [10]. Clinton‑McHarg et al. (2011) achieved an overall consent rate of 7.8% from a state cancer registry using active clinician consent protocols [11]. Wang et al.’s (2023) review of 14 AYA recruitment studies found hospital‑based strategies consistently underperformed internet and social media approaches [12].

Denominator uncertainty compounds the difficulty. The 2,850 figure assumes all post‑treatment survivors pass the eligibility filters (age 18–39, within 5 years of treatment, recurrence‑free, English‑literate, internet‑equipped) [1]. At Northwestern, only 185 of an unspecified larger pool were deemed potentially eligible in the pilot [1], suggesting the stepwise filtering may substantially reduce the true denominator.

At the Wave 1 rate of 9.7%, expected enrollment from 2,850 would be approximately 276—a shortfall of roughly 76 participants (22% below target). If the true eligible denominator is smaller than assumed, the gap widens further. The community partner (Cancer Services) is identified as a backup route [1], but the proposal provides no estimate of accessible survivors through this channel, no historical yield data, and no activation trigger.

**Retention assumptions carry correlated risk.** The 72% retention assumption underlying the analytic sample of 256 rests on very small pilot estimates: 72% in Wave 1 (13 of 18), 66.7% in Wave 2 (23 enrolled, 11 still on study), and 79% in LILAC (n = 39) [1]. The Brunet et al. (2025) ACCESS trial in young adult cancer survivors achieved 71.4% retention at final follow‑up, providing an external benchmark consistent with the proposal’s assumption [13]. However, if recruitment yields a more representative, less selective sample than the pilots, retention could fall below 72%, and the combined effect of lower enrollment and lower retention would further reduce statistical power.

The recruitment plan’s use of multiple channels—letters, follow‑up calls, local organizations, flyers, electronic and community postings [1]—mirrors the multi‑method approach that Wang et al. (2023) found more effective than single‑strategy recruitment [12], which partially mitigates this concern. The Brunet et al. (2025) trial demonstrated that multi‑channel recruitment can sustain approximately four enrolled participants per month from a single site [13]—a pace that, if replicated across two sites, could approach the target. The 36‑month recruitment window is adequate if the 13% rate proves accurate, but leaves limited buffer for corrective action if early enrollment lags. A stepwise eligibility funnel with quantified partner contributions and an adaptive recruitment plan with predefined milestones would materially strengthen the feasibility case.

## Ethics and safety

**The study's risk profile is low and proportionate, with appropriate eligibility boundaries and standard data‑security practices. The main ethics limitation is the absence of an explicit safety‑monitoring plan for psychological distress detected during assessments—a gap that is manageable but should be addressed before recruitment begins.**

The web‑based, self‑administered format delivers supportive‑care psychoeducational content rather than clinical treatment, and the five skills—noticing positive events, mindfulness, reappraisal, strengths‑setting, and acts of kindness—are established techniques with no documented pattern of iatrogenic harm in prior trials [1]. Participants retain their usual clinical care throughout, and the intervention explicitly does not replace medical or psychological treatment [1]. The protocol's exclusion of individuals with significant psychiatric history and those in palliative or hospice care provides a safety boundary that reduces the likelihood of severe adverse reactions to self‑guided emotional exercises [1].

Consent and data governance follow standard institutional workflows. Potential participants complete a REDCap eligibility screener, provide informed consent electronically, and receive unique study IDs and PINs that control questionnaire access [1]. REDCap supports secure survey and database collection, and the Wake Forest Research Information Systems Unit hosts the intervention platform [1]. These arrangements are adequate for the sensitivity of the data collected—psychological well‑being measures, demographics, and clinical characteristics [1]—and are consistent with practices in comparable web‑based psychosocial trials.

**The principal gap is the absence of procedures for identifying and responding to psychological distress that may emerge during assessments.** Participants complete PROMIS depression and anxiety computer‑adaptive tests at baseline, weekly during the intervention, and at four follow‑up points through six months [1]. While these measures are research instruments rather than clinical screens, scores in elevated ranges may indicate treatable distress. The prior EMPOWER pilots noted that participants who left the study had poorer baseline well‑being on several measures [1], confirming that attrition and distress can co‑occur. A protocol that specifies how site staff will monitor for concerning scores or participant communications, what threshold would trigger outreach, and what referral resources are available would materially strengthen the ethics framework without altering the study's low‑risk character. Such plans are standard in behavioural intervention trials and would be straightforward to add.

The exclusion of individuals without internet access or a compatible device [1] raises a modest equity concern. The proposal's rationale—that the web‑based format enables the factorial manipulation and reaches a geographically dispersed young adult population [1]—is sound, but the exclusion may disproportionately affect socioeconomically disadvantaged survivors, a group the proposal's broader health‑equity motivation would otherwise prioritise. The planned confirmatory trial in community‑practice settings through NCORP [1] could later address this reach limitation, but the optimization phase itself will generate evidence from a digitally connected subset.

The compensation structure—$20 per assessment, up to $100 across five time points [1]—is modest and consistent with recognition of participant time rather than an inducement to accept risk. The overall risk‑benefit balance is favourable: the intervention teaches adaptive emotion‑regulation skills with potential direct benefit to participants, the research procedures add low incremental burden, and the knowledge gained serves the broader population of young adult cancer survivors with documented unmet psychosocial needs [1] [6].

## Management

**The team combines the necessary expertise, and the schedule and infrastructure are adequate for the proposed work; the main management risk is recruitment underperformance, which would compress analysis timelines and reduce the analytic sample, and the proposal’s backup plan lacks quantified triggers or milestones to guide timely corrective action.**

The investigator team is a clear strength. Salsman contributes young‑adult oncology, recruitment, and outcome‑measurement expertise, while Moskowitz brings the positive‑emotion intervention programme developed and tested across HIV, diabetes, dementia caregiving, and breast cancer [1]. Wagner adds direct MOST trial experience, and the broader collaborative group covers psychosocial oncology, medical oncology, quantitative methods, survivorship, and eHealth [1]. The relevant prior work—the LILAC pilot comparing online and in‑person delivery [1], the two single‑arm EMPOWER adaptation waves [1], and the co‑design literature now emerging from this group [6] [7]—demonstrates that the team can execute the stages of adaptation, piloting, and analysis that the current project requires. The study statistician will administer block randomization with block size 64 after baseline assessment [1], a straightforward logistical arrangement that fits the factorial structure.

**The four‑year timeline is realistically apportioned** across the project’s phases [1]. Study startup occupies the first two quarters, optimization and participant follow‑up extend from the second quarter of Year 1 through the second quarter of Year 4, data analysis runs from Year 3 into Year 4, and manuscript preparation and preparation of the follow‑up R01 for the confirmatory trial fill the final period [1]. The 36‑month recruitment window aligns with the assumption of approximately 2,850 potentially eligible survivors over three years [1]. If recruitment proceeds at the projected pace, the window allows completion of follow‑up through the six‑month assessment for the final enrolled participants within the grant period. The Wake Forest Research Information Systems Unit will host the intervention platform [1], and the two comprehensive cancer centers provide established clinical registries, EHR‑based screening infrastructure, and dedicated research staff [1]—operational resources that match the study’s needs.

**The principal management concern is the absence of quantified adaptive recruitment planning.** The proposal identifies Cancer Services as a community partner backup “if accrual lags” [1] but provides no estimate of the number of eligible survivors accessible through this channel, no historical yield data from this partner, and no trigger point at which the backup would be activated. As detailed in the Feasibility assessment, pilot yields of 9.7% [1] and external benchmarks of 7–10% for hospital‑based AYA recruitment [10] [11] [12] suggest the 13% rate may be optimistic. If early enrollment tracks closer to the pilot rate than the projected rate, the study would need to activate the community partner or intensify alternative channels to reach the 352 target. Without a predefined milestone—for example, a minimum enrollment threshold at 12 or 18 months that triggers intensified backup recruitment—the timeline could erode before corrective action is taken, compressing the analysis phase or reducing the analytic sample below the target of 256.

The recruitment plan’s use of multiple channels (letters, phone calls, local organizations, flyers, electronic and community postings [1]) provides operational flexibility that partially mitigates this risk, and the ACCESS trial demonstrated that multi‑channel recruitment can sustain approximately four enrolled participants per month from a single site [13]—a pace that, if replicated across two sites, could approach the target. Adding quantified milestones and a decision rule for partner activation would convert a plausible operational plan into a robust one.

The expected products—component effect estimates, exploratory mediator/moderator findings, and an assembled intervention suitable for a subsequent multisite trial [1]—are clearly specified and match the four‑year scope. The management arrangements are therefore adequate to deliver the project, conditional on active monitoring of recruitment with a more explicit adaptive framework than the proposal currently documents.

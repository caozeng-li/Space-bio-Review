# Project review

## Overall assessment

This R03 proposal investigates whether six circulating MHC class I-like proteins—MICA, MICB, ULBP1, ULBP2, ULBP3, and ULBP4—measured prospectively in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) cohort are associated with subsequent colorectal cancer (CRC) risk, and what demographic, lifestyle, and genetic factors determine their levels in cancer-free adults. The long-term objective is to understand immune surveillance and evasion sufficiently to inform CRC prevention and early detection [1]. The project will perform secondary analyses integrating repeated SOMAscan plasma proteomics from three ARIC visits, genotypes from GWAS and whole-genome sequencing, detailed lifestyle measurements, and incident cancer follow-up through 2015 [1].

The most valuable expected knowledge comes from the first prospective assessment of ULBP1–4 in relation to CRC and the first systematic determinants analysis of all six proteins in a large, biracial population—filling genuine gaps that existing cross-sectional or case–control studies have not addressed. A third, secondary aim proposes Mendelian randomization to explore whether genetically predicted protein levels are causally related to CRC [1].

The project draws substantial support from the rich, pre-existing ARIC infrastructure: repeated SOMAscan measurements at three time points, deep genotype data, decades of cancer follow-up, and an experienced multidisciplinary team combining molecular cancer epidemiology, computational genetics, and biostatistics [1]. These resources make the proposed secondary analyses highly feasible within the requested two-year timeframe. The principal investigator, Anna Prizment, has led related work on MICA variants and soluble MICA in pancreatic cancer [2] and co-authored the 2023 ARIC publication that already examined sMICA and sMICB prospectively in relation to CRC [3].

The main limitations are analytic. The principal factor-score exposure for the risk analysis collapses six biologically heterogeneous proteins, two of which the investigators’ own published work shows are uncorrelated (r=0.01) in ARIC, into a single variable whose biological meaning and generalizability are unclear. The Mendelian randomization analysis contains a fundamental instrument-selection flaw and is undermined by the extensive pleiotropy of the HLA region. The marginal structural model approach for analyzing protein change over time is methodologically ambitious but underspecified in critical details, including the absence of a well-defined hypothetical intervention, and its power calculations do not account for the variance inflation that IPTW weights introduce.

On balance, the project makes a worthwhile, appropriately scaled contribution for an R03 mechanism. The planned work will generate new prospective data on ULBP proteins and the first systematic characterization of determinants of these biomarkers in a cancer-free population—both of which represent genuine advances. The main scientific value rests on Aims 1 and 2. Conditional on reframing the primary exposure around individual proteins with appropriate multiplicity adjustment and on repositioning the Mendelian randomization analysis as purely hypothesis-generating rather than a test of causality, the proposal is supportable in scope.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

**Rating: B. Confidence: B.**

**The project addresses a scientifically interesting and clinically relevant question about immune-regulating proteins that may precede colorectal cancer development, with the attainable knowledge being a useful but incremental contribution to understanding the natural history of MHC class I-like proteins in relation to cancer risk.**

The biological premise is well-grounded. MHC class I-like proteins function as ligands for the activating NKG2D receptor on natural killer and T cells. Their expression on stressed or malignant cells can support immune recognition, while shedding into the circulation can reduce membrane-bound ligand availability and downregulate the receptor, potentially weakening immune surveillance [1]. The proposal’s central question—whether prediagnostic circulating levels and longitudinal changes associate with subsequent CRC risk—follows logically from this mechanistic understanding [1].

The study will generate two genuinely new resources. First, it will provide the first prospective data on soluble ULBP1–4 proteins in relation to subsequent CRC. These four proteins have been minimally studied in any cancer context and never examined prospectively for CRC. Second, it will deliver the first systematic characterization of demographic, lifestyle, and genetic determinants of all six MHC class I-like proteins in a cancer-free population. These are meaningful contributions because the existing literature on soluble NKG2D ligands is almost entirely cross-sectional or case–control, measuring protein levels after cancer diagnosis rather than before it [4]. No population-based study has systematically mapped what determines normal variation in these proteins, making the determinants analysis a genuine reference resource for future biomarker research.

The ARIC cohort provides an exceptional platform for addressing these questions: 12,589 cancer-free participants at baseline (Visit 2, 1990–1992) with 360 incident CRC cases during follow-up, repeated SOMAscan proteomics at three visits spanning two decades, genotypes from GWAS and whole-genome sequencing through TOPMed and the Centers for Common Disease Genomics, and detailed longitudinal lifestyle measurements [1]. The SOMAscan version 4 platform measures 4,931 proteins with a lifetime median CV of 6.2% across 220 runs and 660 replicate QC samples, and the investigators’ pilot work in 42 ARIC participants demonstrated strong technical performance for the MHC class I-like proteins specifically: median CV 5%, Spearman correlation 0.89, and intraclass correlation 0.96 in blinded split samples [1].

**The value of Aim 1 (prospective CRC associations) is bounded by what the investigators’ own published work has already established.** The Wang et al. 2023 ARIC paper, using the same SOMAscan platform, the same Visit 2 baseline, and overlapping participants, examined sMICA and sMICB prospectively in 10,834 cancer-free participants with 312 incident CRC cases and found no overall association with CRC risk, though a sex-stratified signal emerged for lower sMICA with lower risk in males only (HR=0.68, 95% CI 0.49–0.96) [3]. The current proposal therefore retreads ground for MICA and MICB while extending the analysis to the four ULBP proteins. Whether this extension is scientifically valuable depends on the incremental information the ULBP proteins carry beyond what MICA and MICB already showed.

Only one prior study has measured multiple NKG2D ligands together. Nagai et al. (2022) measured five of the six proteins by ELISA in 370 hemodialysis patients and found strong inter-correlations among MICA, MICB, ULBP2, and ULBP3 (r=0.49–0.94) but negligible correlations involving ULBP1 (r=0.05–0.14 with other ligands) [5]. If similar patterns hold in ARIC cancer-free participants, ULBP1 may be the most informative addition—biologically distinct as a GPI-anchored protein with a short cell-surface half-life that is rapidly internalized and degraded rather than shed, with only trace soluble levels detected [4] [6]—while ULBP2 and ULBP3 may largely recapitulate signals already captured by MICA and MICB. The proposal’s factor-score approach, discussed under Rigor, would obscure rather than illuminate this question of incremental value.

**Aim 2 (determinants analysis) addresses a clearly identifiable gap and is likely to produce the study’s most lasting contribution.** The Onyeaghala et al. (2019) pancreatic cancer study demonstrated the A5.1 microsatellite’s effect on soluble MICA levels in controls—one copy associated with 1.24-fold higher levels, two copies with 1.38-fold higher levels [2]—and Isernhagen et al. (2015) showed the MICA-129Met/Val dimorphism (rs1051792) affects both membrane expression and shedding [7]. However, no population-based study has systematically characterized how age, sex, race, BMI, smoking, physical activity, diabetes, and genetic variants associate with circulating levels of all six proteins in cancer-free adults. Knowing what drives normal inter-individual variation is a prerequisite for evaluating whether cancer-related elevations are clinically meaningful.

**The secondary Mendelian randomization aim contributes little independent scientific value in its current form.** As discussed under Rigor, the instrument-selection strategy and HLA-region pleiotropy render the causal design unreliable. The project’s expected knowledge gain therefore rests primarily on Aims 1 and 2, where the observational design is appropriate and the rich data resource provides a sound basis for descriptive and etiologic inference at a scale appropriate for an R03 mechanism [1].

## Innovation

**Rating: B. Confidence: B.**

**The proposal advances beyond prior work primarily by extending existing MICA/MICB findings to the under-studied ULBP protein family, adding repeated protein measurements over time, and conducting the first systematic determinants analysis of all six proteins—genuine but modest innovations that are appropriately scaled for an R03 mechanism.**

The innovation landscape is shaped decisively by the investigators’ own 2023 publication in *Cancer Epidemiology, Biomarkers & Prevention*, which reported prospective associations of sMICA and sMICB with CRC risk in ARIC participants using the same SOMAscan platform and the same Visit 2 baseline [3]. That paper already answered, for two of the six proteins, the central question posed by Aim 1a. The current proposal’s incremental contribution therefore rests on three extensions, each with a distinct degree of originality.

**The most novel element is the addition of ULBP1–4 measurements.** These four proteins have been minimally studied in any cancer context and never examined prospectively in relation to CRC. The ULBP family is biologically distinct from MICA and MICB: ULBP1–3 are GPI-anchored rather than transmembrane, ULBP4 is transmembrane, and their shedding mechanisms differ fundamentally—metalloprotease-mediated for ULBP2, exosomal for ULBP3, with no established primary release mode for ULBP1 [4] [8] [9]. The Nagai et al. (2022) study measured ULBP1–3 alongside MICA and MICB, but that was cross-sectional, conducted in hemodialysis patients with elevated baseline levels due to renal failure, and found no impact of cancer history on ligand levels [5]. No published study has measured ULBP4 together with the other five. The proposal therefore generates genuinely new prospective data on proteins that may carry independent or complementary information about immune surveillance status—a defensible contribution for an exploratory R03.

**The repeated-measures component represents a further advance.** No study has examined within-person changes in NKG2D ligand levels over time in relation to subsequent cancer. The proposal examines short-term change over approximately three years (Visit 2 to Visit 3) and exploratory long-term change over approximately two decades (Visit 2 to Visit 5) [1]. The biological rationale—that increasing shedding over time may reflect evolving immune evasion—is plausible, though the approximately three-year window may be brief relative to CRC’s prolonged preclinical phase.

**The determinants analysis (Aim 2) fills a clearly identifiable gap.** The inclusion of both Black and White participants also allows assessment in populations not well represented in prior MHC class I-like protein studies [1]. The genetic component, while partially documented for MICA—the preliminary work shows MICA SNP associations with CRC in ARIC and A5.1 microsatellite effects on soluble MICA in pancreatic study controls [1]—has not been examined for MICB or ULBP variants in relation to protein levels in any large population. The candidate variant list is extensive: at least 8 MICA SNPs, 6 MICB SNPs, and 8 MICA microsatellites, plus ULBP variants [1]. The determinants analysis would produce the first reference dataset describing what drives normal variation in these biomarkers—a necessary foundation for interpreting whether cancer-associated elevations are clinically meaningful.

**The Mendelian randomization component offers no meaningful methodological or substantive innovation.** The proposed analytic approach (ivprobit) and the instrument-selection strategy do not advance beyond standard confounded observational estimates, and the HLA-region context makes causal attribution unreliable irrespective of method, as discussed under Rigor.

The proposal’s framing somewhat overstates its novelty by describing itself as the first prospective study without adequately distinguishing its contribution from the published ARIC MICA/MICB analysis [1]. However, the ULBP data, repeated measures, and determinants analysis collectively represent a genuine, if contained, extension of prior work that is consistent with the R03 mechanism’s purpose—establishing feasibility and generating hypotheses for subsequent larger-scale validation [1].

## Rigor

**Rating: C. Confidence: B.**

**The observational design of Aims 1 and 2 is appropriate to the study’s stated goals, and the ARIC cohort provides high-quality measurement and follow-up infrastructure. However, three analytic choices—the factor-score as primary exposure, the Mendelian randomization design, and the specification of the Aim 1b change analysis—materially weaken the inferences the proposal claims to deliver.**

### Factor-score as principal exposure

The proposal designates a factor score of the six proteins as the principal exposure for Aim 1a and 1b, anticipating correlations of “approximately 0.4–0.55” and retaining the first factor with eigenvalue greater than one [1]. This choice lacks empirical support and faces contrary evidence from the investigators’ own published work. The Wang et al. 2023 ARIC analysis, using the identical SOMAscan platform and the same Visit 2 samples, reported that “sMICA and sMICB levels were not correlated (r=0.01, p=0.24)” [3].

If the two best-studied family members, both transmembrane proteins shed by metalloproteases, are uncorrelated, the assumption that all six proteins will share moderate-to-strong inter-correlations is tenuous. The biological heterogeneity among these proteins reinforces this concern. MICA and MICB are transmembrane proteins with three extracellular domains; ULBP1–3 are GPI-anchored with two domains; ULBP4 is transmembrane [4] [9]. Their shedding mechanisms differ fundamentally: metalloprotease-mediated for MICA, MICB, and ULBP2; exosomal for ULBP3; with no established primary release mode for ULBP1, where “only trace amounts of soluble ULBP1” have been detected [4] [8]. As Zingoni et al. (2018) concluded, “NKG2D ligands are not equal or redundant in terms of final outcome” and “the expression of a certain ligand or a particular allelic variant is essential to drive a proper immune response” [10].

Collapsing these biologically distinct entities into a single latent factor risks obscuring rather than clarifying their relationship with CRC. The factor loadings are estimated from the same ARIC sample used to test the CRC association. The proposal acknowledges that later validation would use “standard methods” for “individual protein measurements” in an independent cohort [1], but this would validate individual proteins, not the factor score. Any association found with the factor score cannot be transported to another setting without re-estimating loadings, limiting the scientific contribution to a single-cohort description. The individual-protein analyses characterized as “exploratory” [1] should form the primary analytic strategy, with the factor score retained as a supplementary data-reduction exercise. The handling of undetectable proteins—particularly ULBP1, for which only trace soluble levels are reported [4], and ULBP3, which was undetectable in 77% of hemodialysis patients [5]—is not specified for the factor analysis, and factor analysis with substantial left-censored or imputed values can produce distorted loading estimates.

### Mendelian randomization (Secondary Aim 3)

The MR analysis contains a fundamental instrument-selection flaw: instruments are chosen as variants “strongly associated with the relevant proteins and associated with CRC” [1]. Selecting instruments conditioned on the outcome induces selection bias. In a one-sample setting where both exposure–instrument and outcome–instrument associations are estimated from the same data, this practice overestimates both the numerator and denominator of the Wald ratio, biasing the causal estimate toward the confounded observational association. Haycock et al. (2016) describe this as the winner’s curse: “When the discovery and MR analysis samples are the same, both the numerator and denominator will be overestimated” [11]. Proper practice requires selecting instruments solely based on exposure associations, using independent datasets or sample-splitting approaches such as cross-fitting [12] [13]. The use of F\>10 as the sole criterion for instrument strength [1] does not remedy this, as the F-statistic addresses weak-instrument bias but not selection bias from conditioning on the outcome [13].

Additionally, the proposed instruments—SNPs and microsatellites within or near MICA, MICB, and ULBP genes in the MHC region on chromosome 6p21.3—cannot plausibly satisfy the exclusion restriction. The HLA region exhibits extensive long-range linkage disequilibrium: Rahmouni et al. (2023) identified a haploblock spanning 1.9 megabases comprising 379 SNP alleles impacting 72 genes, including MICA and MICB [14]. Smith et al. (2025) found a 17-fold average per-SNP enrichment of disease associations in the HLA region, with 7,649 HLA associations across 647 diseases [15]. A genetic variant in MICA will therefore be in LD with potentially hundreds of variants in classical HLA genes, complement genes, and other immune genes, each with independent associations with cancer risk through immune surveillance, antigen presentation, and inflammatory pathways.

Variants such as rs1051792 (Val129Met), featured in the proposal’s preliminary data [1], have well-documented pleiotropic effects operating through pathways independent of circulating soluble protein levels. The Met isoform binds NKG2D with high affinity, the Val isoform with low affinity [16], directly modulating NK and CD8+ T-cell activation through membrane-bound MICA. Isernhagen et al. (2016) systematically reviewed associations of this variant with infections, autoimmune diseases—including ankylosing spondylitis, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, SLE, type 1 diabetes, and psoriasis—and multiple malignancies [16]. The same variant can be risk-increasing for some diseases and protective for others depending on population and disease context [16]. Furthermore, GWAS have assigned disease risks for nasopharyngeal carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, cervical cancer, and asthma to the MICA gene region [16]. These pleiotropic pathways mean a MICA variant’s association with CRC could operate through NKG2D-mediated immune surveillance, through HLA class I antigen presentation via LD, through inflammatory pathways, or through circulating soluble MICA levels—and the MR design cannot distinguish among these.

The proposed ivprobit estimator with a binary outcome (approximately 360 CRC cases) and potentially weak instruments is additionally vulnerable. Burgess et al. (2015) note that weak instruments produce “systematic finite sample bias, typically in the direction of the observational (confounded) association” [13], and that standard confidence intervals from 2SLS-like methods “tend to underestimate the true uncertainty in the estimates, leading to inflated Type I error rates” [13]. The proposal provides no power calculation for the MR analysis. Comparing “standard probit with instrumental-variable probit” [1] will be uninformative when both estimates are biased in the same direction.

These issues mean the MR analysis, as designed, cannot distinguish a genuine causal effect of circulating MHC class I-like proteins on CRC from confounding by the extensive pleiotropic functions of the HLA region. As a secondary, exploratory aim spanning months 19–24 [1], it is appropriately positioned in the proposal, but its contribution to the study’s overall inferences is negligible in its current form.

### Aim 1b change analysis

The use of marginal structural models (MSMs) with inverse-probability-of-treatment weighting (IPTW) to analyze protein change represents a methodologically ambitious approach with three underspecified dimensions.

First, the proposal does not articulate a well-defined hypothetical intervention on protein change. The exposure is described simply as “the Visit 3 minus Visit 2 change” [1], but the IPTW machinery is designed to estimate effects of interventions. Young et al. (2018) showed that IPW methods can consistently estimate risk under “representative interventions” on time-varying continuous treatments, but this requires specifying the intervention of interest, such as maintaining a continuous variable within a range [17]. The target trial framework of Hernán and colleagues emphasizes that causal questions should be framed as comparative effectiveness questions about interventions [18], yet “does greater increase in MHC class I-like proteins predict higher CRC risk?” is fundamentally a descriptive prognostic question. The MSM may estimate a weighted association, but the causal interpretation that the IPTW machinery ostensibly supports is not justified by the design. A simpler Cox regression with standard covariate adjustment would honestly estimate the same conditional association without overclaiming.

Second, the sensitivity analyses for reverse causation are insufficient. The central inferential threat is that protein increases may reflect occult preclinical cancer rather than precede it. The proposal’s timing rules—excluding cancer through Visit 3 and beginning follow-up at Visit 3 [1]—are appropriate but address only cancers diagnosed before the protein change is fully observed. They cannot address cancers present but undiagnosed at Visit 3 that are diagnosed shortly thereafter. The sensitivity analyses excluding cases within two or five years of sampling [1] are a reasonable step, but CRC has a prolonged preclinical phase (adenoma-to-carcinoma progression estimated at 10–15 years), and such exclusions substantially reduce already-limited case numbers. With only 306 CRC cases among 11,340 participants for the short-term change analysis, excluding cases within five years could reduce the analytic case count by 30–50%. IPTW addresses confounding by measured covariates but cannot resolve temporality—which came first, the protein change or the cancer? Analyses stratified by time since Visit 3 (e.g., 0–2, 2–5, 5+ years) or restricted to CRC cases diagnosed after a longer minimum induction period would more directly test whether protein changes precede cancer.

Third, the power calculation does not account for IPTW variance inflation. The stated thresholds—HR 1.26 per SD of factor-score change and HR 1.65 for the quartile comparison, based on 11,340 participants and 306 CRC cases [1]—appear to assume unweighted Cox regression. IPTW weights introduce additional variability that inflates standard errors and reduces effective sample size. Spreafico (2025) demonstrated that even minor near-positivity violations “can substantially destabilize IPTW-based estimators, inflating variance and bias” [19], and that “even when \[the truncation indicator\] is small, performance may still be poor due to the presence of extreme weights” [19]. The proposal mentions that “separate weights will represent the relevant short-term covariate changes” [1] but does not describe whether these will be stabilized weights, the specification of the weight model, or what diagnostics will be employed. Best practice recommends inspecting the distribution of weights, investigating stabilized weights with a mean far from one as an indicator of model misspecification, and performing sensitivity analyses using a range of truncation thresholds [19]. The actual power is likely lower than stated.

## Feasibility

**Rating: A. Confidence: A.**

**The project is a secondary analytical study relying on data and biospecimens that have already been collected or are undergoing measurement, making it highly feasible within the two-year R03 timeframe.** The proposal’s feasibility rests on three firmly established pillars: the ARIC cohort infrastructure, the availability of core assay data, and the investigative team’s direct access and relevant expertise.

The ARIC cohort provides an exceptionally complete platform. Originally enrolling 15,792 Black and White adults aged 45–64 in 1987–1989 from four U.S. communities, the study includes repeated interviews, clinical examinations, anthropometry, and blood collection at multiple visits [1]. Cancer ascertainment through 2015 combines state registry linkage and active follow-up. Genotyping data from GWAS and deep whole-genome sequencing are available for approximately 12,000 participants through TOPMed and the Centers for Common Disease Genomics [1]. Critically, no new participant recruitment, contact, or biospecimen collection is required—the project is purely analytical, using frozen plasma aliquots and existing data extracts.

The SOMAscan proteomic measurements are either completed or underway. Visit 3 and Visit 5 assays have been finished, and Visit 2 assays are described as underway [1]. The platform’s quality metrics—lifetime median CV of 6.2% across 220 runs—and the investigators’ pilot results in 42 ARIC participants (median CV 5%, intraclass correlation 0.96) [1] support the assay’s suitability for detecting protein–CRC associations of the magnitude the power calculations target. Over a six-to-eight-week window, only one protein showed significant change in the pilot, indicating acceptable short-term within-person stability for longitudinal analyses [1].

The team’s access to ARIC data is documented through prior publications. The Wang et al. 2023 paper, examining sMICA and sMICB in 10,834 ARIC participants using the same SOMAscan platform, demonstrates that the investigators have navigated the ARIC data request, approval, and analysis pipeline successfully [3]. The two-year schedule is realistic: months 1–3 for IRB and data extraction, months 4–12 for Aim 1, months 13–18 for Aim 2, months 19–24 for the secondary aim with overlapping manuscript preparation [1]. The principal work involves statistical analysis of existing data rather than new data generation. SAS and Stata, the specified software tools, are adequate for the proposed descriptive, survival, mixed-model, and instrumental-variable analyses [1].

The principal feasibility risk is not logistical but analytic: whether the statistical methods proposed can deliver the intended inferences given the data structure, a concern addressed under Rigor. From a practical standpoint, the core resources are in place, the timeline is appropriate, and the team has demonstrated the capability to execute this type of analysis in this cohort.

## Ethics and safety

**Rating: A. Confidence: B.**

**The project involves secondary analysis of existing de-identified data and biospecimens with no participant contact, additional data collection, or experimental intervention, making the ethical and safety risks minimal and the proposed protections proportionate.**

The investigators receive coded research IDs, while direct identifiers and the identity linkage remain with the ARIC Coordinating Center [1]. This separation of the analytical dataset from personally identifying information is a standard and effective mechanism for protecting participant confidentiality. The proposal describes privacy protections covering transmission, handling, and distribution of data, stated in summary form appropriate for an R03 scientific narrative where the detailed data-use agreement resides with the parent cohort’s administrative infrastructure.

No new participant contact, recruitment, or consent process is required [1]. The original ARIC participants provided informed consent for the collection and future research use of their biospecimens and data, and the proposed analyses fall within the scope of that consent as determined by ARIC leadership and institutional review boards. The schedule allocates months 1–3 to IRB arrangements [1], indicating that the investigators will obtain necessary institutional approvals before commencing analysis, consistent with the practice demonstrated in their prior ARIC publications.

Descriptive and association results will be shared under NIH policy, while access to the study dataset follows ARIC leadership and publication-committee policies [1]. This tiered approach—summary results shared openly, individual-level data access governed by established cohort procedures—balances transparency and reproducibility with participant privacy protection and the data-generating investigators’ stewardship responsibilities.

The project involves no environmental risks, no animal subjects, and no experimental materials requiring special handling. The plasma specimens were collected and frozen decades ago; the SOMAscan assays have been performed or are underway as part of broader ARIC proteomic initiatives, with no additional laboratory work proposed. The biomarkers under study could in principle generate findings with clinical significance, but the coded-data structure precludes individual-level result reporting, the assays are research-grade (SOMAscan relative fluorescence units) rather than clinically validated tests, and the study’s objective is to generate knowledge that may eventually inform prevention and early detection strategies [1], not to provide clinical information to ARIC participants in the current project.

## Management

**Rating: B. Confidence: B.**

**The project is a focused analytical study within an established research infrastructure, supported by a multidisciplinary team whose expertise aligns closely with the proposed work, and a realistic timeline that reflects the secondary nature of the analyses.**

The team combines complementary strengths in molecular cancer epidemiology, computational genetics, and biostatistics [1]. The principal investigator has led related work on MICA variants and soluble MICA in pancreatic cancer [2] and co-authored the 2023 ARIC publication examining sMICA and sMICB in relation to CRC risk [3]. This track record demonstrates familiarity with both the biological questions and the ARIC data environment. The breadth of analytical techniques proposed—from descriptive factor analysis through Cox regression and IPTW-weighted MSMs to Mendelian randomization—requires statistical sophistication, and the team’s publication record suggests this is available. The ARIC data extracts are existing resources managed by the ARIC Coordinating Center, with which the investigators have an established working relationship demonstrated by prior data access and publications.

The schedule is logically sequenced [1]. Aim 1 (prospective CRC associations) is the central analysis and receives the largest allocation (months 4–12). Aim 2 (determinants) follows once protein measurements are fully characterized (months 13–18). The secondary MR analysis, methodologically the most tenuous component, is placed last with the smallest allocation (months 19–24). Concurrent manuscript preparation during the second year reflects efficient use of analysis downtime and is realistic for an experienced team.

Two considerations merit attention, though neither substantially weakens the rating for an R03 mechanism. First, the analytic choices—particularly the factor-score approach and the MSM specification for Aim 1b—may require iterative methodological refinement not explicitly budgeted in the schedule. The two-month allocation for determinants analysis is compact given the number of candidate predictors across six proteins with Bonferroni correction (α≈0.008) [1], though the ARIC data are already structured and the analytical pipelines are well-established in the team’s earlier work. Second, the proposal does not describe contingency planning should key assumptions fail—for instance, if the factor structure is unsupported or if IPTW weights prove unstable. In a larger grant mechanism, explicit fallback analyses and decision rules would be expected; for an R03, the absence of detailed contingency planning is acceptable given the team’s practical experience with the data characteristics that would inform analytical decisions. The principal uncertainties are scientific rather than managerial, concerning whether the chosen methods can support the intended inferences, as discussed under Rigor.

# Project review

## Overall assessment

This prospective observational study, jointly led by Heinemann and Cherney, aims to define individual recovery trajectories for 300 stroke survivors with aphasia followed from inpatient rehabilitation through 18 months post-onset. The project moves beyond single-domain impairment measures to integrate linguistic performance (WAB–R Aphasia Quotient), cognitive-communicative participation (Communicative Participation Item Bank), and quality of life (Stroke and Aphasia Quality of Life Scale-39) within a unified analytical framework using Individual Growth Curve models. The most valuable expected knowledge is a detailed, multi-domain description of the natural history of aphasia recovery, for the first time concurrently characterizing how language, participation, and well-being evolve and plateau, both typically and in relation to patient characteristics, clinical treatment, and potential biological markers [1].

The project is strongly supported by its scientifically important and clinically relevant question, an experienced investigative team with a proven track record of analogous work, and a methodologically rigorous core design that includes multi-site recruitment, well-validated assessment tools, and a sophisticated plan for statistically modeling change. Preliminary studies demonstrate immediate feasibility [1]. The main evidence that materially limits the judgment centers on three issues. The observational design precludes causal interpretation of therapy dose–outcome associations, as adjustment cannot fully account for confounding by indication where patients with better prognoses naturally receive or seek out more therapy [1]. The plan to create an interactive clinical prediction tool relies solely on internal validation methods such as bootstrap resampling and cross-validation, which cannot guarantee that the tool would be accurate for patients at a new, independent clinical site [1]. Finally, the project schedule is compressed, with the final primary analysis and model validation concentrated in the last five quarters, at the very time the last enrolled participant’s complete data is arriving, creating a plausible risk that analyses will be rushed or incomplete [1].

Weighing the evidence, the proposal’s significant descriptive and methodological strengths must be balanced against limitations in the rigor of causal attribution, the readiness of the clinical tool, and schedule feasibility. The core descriptive aims are ambitious and well-designed, promising fundamental knowledge about aphasia recovery that the field currently lacks. However, the plan to build a clinical prediction tool is premature without external validation, and the interpretation of therapy–outcome associations must be treated with appropriate caution given the absence of a design or analysis plan to control for major confounders. The compressed timeline raises a genuine risk that the final, integrated multi-modal analyses will not be completed as planned. These are correctable weaknesses that do not negate the fundamental value of generating the cohort and recovery trajectories. The project is therefore a worthwhile investment with a strong recommendation to fortify the plan for the final analytical phase and to explicitly frame the trajectory tool as a prototype requiring future external validation.

## Scientific value

**Scientific merit of the question and the attainable knowledge is high, though the practical value of one deliverable depends on future validation steps not included in the plan.** The project targets a clinically and scientifically important gap: the absence of prospective, longitudinal data that simultaneously characterize recovery across the three distinct but interrelated domains of linguistic impairment, everyday communicative participation, and quality of life after stroke-related aphasia. Most previous aphasia studies have emphasized linguistic outcomes, used small or retrospective samples, or measured only a few endpoints [1]. Improvement on an impairment test does not necessarily describe a person’s communication confidence or ability to participate [1]. The proposal therefore asks a well-motivated question: what are the typical trajectories of language, participation, and well-being from inpatient rehabilitation through 18 months post-onset, which factors predict individual variation, and do these domains follow the same or different recovery time-courses?

The primary knowledge contribution is a detailed, multi-domain description of the natural history of aphasia recovery in a prospectively assembled, adequately powered cohort of 300 participants retained at 18 months [1]. The team’s hypothesis that linguistic improvement slows around six months while communicative participation and QoL continue to improve toward 18 months [1] is both testable and clinically consequential: if confirmed, it would imply that services and outcome measurement should extend well beyond the period when impairment gains plateau. The concurrent measurement of real-world therapy exposure—capturing inpatient and outpatient services through clinician logs validated for modality-specific reliability, patient and caregiver checklists, and regular research assistant contact [1]—adds substantial descriptive value. It will produce the most granular picture yet assembled of what aphasia rehabilitation actually looks like across care settings and how service patterns relate to outcomes.

The secondary scientific contributions—genetic and neuroimaging biomarkers entered into the individual growth-curve models—extend the knowledge ambition beyond clinical description toward biological stratification of recovery. The preliminary imaging pilot linking baseline default-mode-to-auditory connectivity with Aphasia Quotient change and salience-to-visual connectivity with attention change [1] provides a principled, hypothesis-driven foundation. Even if the genetic epistasis analysis proves exploratory, the imaging component with n = 100 at Shirley Ryan AbilityLab [1] can reasonably detect moderate-to-strong associations between network measures and domain-specific outcomes.

**The predictive tool is a proof-of-concept contribution, not a clinically deployable instrument.** An interactive trajectory tool that generates expected recovery curves from entered patient, stroke, and therapy characteristics [1] represents a tangible practical contribution with potential as a prognostic aid for clinicians, patients, and families. However, its immediate value is qualified: the tool is developed and assessed only through internal validation methods—bootstrap stability assessment and Monte Carlo cross-validation on the same three-site cohort [1]—which cannot establish transportability to independent clinical settings. This limitation, while material to the tool’s clinical readiness, does not diminish the scientific value of the cohort and trajectory models that make such a tool possible in principle.

In sum, the project addresses a question of genuine importance to post-stroke rehabilitation research. Its most valuable expected contribution—integrating linguistic, participation, and QoL outcomes within a single longitudinal framework and characterizing their predictors—would fill a recognized evidence gap and provide an essential foundation for designing more holistic, longer-term interventions.

## Innovation

The proposal is **innovative primarily through integration rather than through any single novel component**, and its originality lies in concurrently addressing questions that prior work has studied in isolation. The scientific contribution is substantial and methodologically fresh, but it is the combination of elements—outcome domains, measurement methods, and analytical framework—that distinguishes this project from the existing literature.

**Simultaneous modeling of three recovery domains.** The most important advance is tracking linguistic impairment (WAB–R Aphasia Quotient), cognitive-communicative participation (Communicative Participation Item Bank), and health-related quality of life (Stroke and Aphasia Quality of Life Scale-39) from inpatient rehabilitation through 18 months post-onset in a single cohort [1]. Prior aphasia outcome research has typically examined these domains separately, often in small or retrospective samples [1]. The hypothesis that linguistic improvement slows around six months while communicative participation and QoL continue to improve toward 18 months [1] directly tests whether different aspects of recovery follow distinct time-courses—a question that cannot be answered without concurrent measurement of all three constructs. The study’s n = 300 target retained at 18 months [1] provides substantially more precision than most existing longitudinal aphasia research for describing these domain-specific trajectories.

**Prospective, granular measurement of real-world therapy exposure.** Prior studies have relied on billing records that capture time and service codes but do not describe what language modality or task was treated [1]. The current plan has clinicians log each session’s start and stop time and identify the targeted modality—auditory comprehension, oral expression, reading comprehension, or written expression—using tablet-based tools [1]. The survey of 56 speech-language pathologists supporting feasibility, the requirement that clinicians exceed 90% reliability before contributing data, and the ten-percent video-recording check [1] represent a methodologically rigorous approach to a measurement problem that has limited the interpretability of prior therapy-exposure research. The post-discharge tracking via weekly patient and caregiver checklists, supplemented by research assistant contact [1], extends this granularity across care settings.

**Integration of resting-state functional connectivity into individual growth-curve models.** The team’s preliminary imaging pilot linking baseline default-mode-to-auditory connectivity with Aphasia Quotient change and salience-to-visual connectivity with attention change [1] provides a principled, domain-specific framework. The finding that language and attention changes were not correlated in the pilot sample [1], together with the distinct connectivity correlates, motivates a differentiated rather than unitary model of recovery—an approach that aligns with emerging network perspectives on aphasia but has not previously been operationalized in a large, longitudinal observational design. The proposed graph-theoretic measures of segregation, integration, and modularity at multiple network densities [1] similarly go beyond simple lesion-volume correlates to examine how the organization of surviving networks relates to domain-specific recovery.

**Individual growth-curve modeling applied to aphasia recovery.** The team has successfully applied IGC methods in spinal cord injury and traumatic brain injury [1] [2], but the extension to aphasia—with its multiple outcome modalities and the distinct trajectory shapes hypothesized for impairment versus participation domains—represents a substantive application to an under-studied population. The comparison of linear, polynomial, and negative-exponential trajectory forms using observed data [1] allows the data to determine the shape of recovery rather than imposing a pre-specified functional form, which is appropriate given the limited prior knowledge about long-term aphasia recovery trajectories.

**The interactive trajectory tool as a novel translational deliverable.** As a proof-of-concept generated from this richly characterized cohort, the tool [1] would represent the first attempt to produce individualized recovery projections integrating the three outcome domains with patient, stroke, and treatment characteristics. Even as an internally validated demonstration, it is a novel contribution to the aphasia outcomes literature, though its characterization should reflect that it is a prototype requiring external validation.

## Rigor

**The study design is rigorous for its core descriptive aims but has two material limitations: the observational framework does not support causal interpretation of therapy–outcome associations, and the clinical prediction tool is assessed only by internal validation, which is insufficient for its stated purpose.**

**The core design is methodologically strong.** Well-validated instruments capture linguistic impairment (WAB–R Aphasia Quotient), cognitive-communicative participation (Communicative Participation Item Bank), and quality of life (Stroke and Aphasia Quality of Life Scale-39) at admission, discharge, and six, 12, and 18 months post-stroke [1]. Secondary measures address attention, communication confidence, caregiver-rated effectiveness, mood, fatigue, social participation, and global health using established tools including Neuro-QoL banks and PROMIS Global Health [1]. The assessment schedule is appropriately dense to capture the non-linear recovery trajectories hypothesized in the proposal, and the use of aphasia-supportive communication during testing [1] addresses a common barrier to valid measurement in this population.

Individual growth-curve modeling is a well-chosen analytical framework. It retains individual variation in starting point, rate, and plateau while estimating population tendencies, and the plan to compare linear, polynomial, and negative-exponential forms using information criteria [1] allows the data to determine trajectory shape. The treatment of intermittent missed visits under a missing-at-random assumption, with all available assessments contributing [1], is standard and appropriate, though the proposal acknowledges that dropout may challenge this assumption. The internal validation plan—500 bootstrap resamples to assess predictor-selection frequency and Monte Carlo cross-validation comparing prediction error against the minimally clinically important difference [1]—provides relevant quality checks on model stability and optimism-corrected performance within the development sample.

The therapy-exposure measurement protocol is unusually rigorous for an observational study. Clinicians log each session’s timing and targeted modality on tablets, with training and feedback until they exceed 90% reliability; ten percent of sessions are video-recorded to verify logs [1]. Post-discharge services are tracked through weekly patient and caregiver checklists, supplemented by research assistant contact [1]. This protocol addresses a recognized limitation of prior work, where billing records provided time and service codes but did not describe what language modality or task was treated [1].

**Therapy–outcome associations are observational and cannot support causal conclusions.** The proposal frames treatment exposure as a set of modifiable predictors whose coefficients are expected to inform practice [1], while the design precludes causal interpretation of these associations. Treatment is not assigned; patients with milder strokes, fewer comorbidities, and higher motivation both receive more therapy and recover better, creating a positive confounding structure that covariate adjustment cannot fully resolve. The protocol paper’s candid acknowledgment is commendable: “the observational design does not permit accounting for unmeasured confounders, provide strong evidence for causal relation between SLP intervention and outcomes, and does not control for biases due to confounding, selection, or other sources” [3]. However, equivalent clarity is absent from the aims and the interactive tool description, and the analysis plan does not include sensitivity analyses for unmeasured confounding (e.g., E-values) that would help readers assess how robust the findings are to alternative explanations. The bootstrap stability assessment under Aim 3 evaluates whether the same predictors are selected across resamples, but stable selection of a confounded predictor does not make it causal. Under the current design, therapy coefficients should be interpreted as adjusted associations whose estimated magnitude cannot be attributed to the causal effect of modifying therapy delivery.

**Internal validation is insufficient for a clinical prediction tool.** The bootstrap stability assessment and Monte Carlo cross-validation under Aim 3 are internal validation methods that correct for the optimism of fitting and selecting models on the same data. They do not assess whether the models would predict accurately for patients at a different facility, in a different region, or treated under different clinical practices, which is what a clinical tool deployed beyond the study sites would require. Under standard prediction-model frameworks such as TRIPOD, this corresponds to development with internal validation, not external validation. External evidence reinforces this concern: a recent independent evaluation of the Disconnection Symptom Discoverer model found that promising internal performance failed to generalize to an independent cohort for most cognitive domains and timepoints, illustrating “potential overfitting issues within cognitive outcome prediction models, highlighting the need for caution when aiming to predict long-term post-stroke cognitive outcomes and further external validation” [4]. A scoping review of 19 machine learning models in post-stroke aphasia found that most models relied on single-center, small-sample data with validation limited to internal cross-validation, and identified the lack of multicenter external validation as a primary barrier to clinical feasibility [5]. Only one of 19 models had undergone external validation in an independent cohort [5]. The trajectory tool is therefore best understood as a prototype or proof-of-concept demonstration, not a clinically deployable instrument.

**The genetic and imaging analyses have appropriately differentiated inferential strength.** The core Aim 1 trajectory estimation is well-supported by the n = 300 design and the clinical predictor analyses in Aim 2 are appropriately powered for detecting moderate-to-large associations. The genetic epistasis analysis—using Multifactor Dimensionality Reduction on 10 SNPs across six genes [1]—should be evaluated as exploratory hypothesis generation: external evidence indicates that sample sizes of 1,000 or more are typically required to detect gene-gene interactions of plausible magnitude even under moderate relative risk, and power with n = 300 for epistatic interactions is very low [6]. The imaging analyses (n = 100 at a single site) occupy a middle ground: moderate-to-strong associations between established measures such as lesion volume or global efficiency and domain-specific outcomes are detectable, but the full multivariate plan with dozens of graph metrics across multiple modalities and parcellation schemes exceeds what can be reliably estimated from 100 participants.

These limitations do not undermine the study’s core descriptive contribution: the cohort and trajectory models will provide fundamental knowledge about aphasia recovery across outcome domains. They do, however, constrain the inferential strength of the therapy-exposure findings and the clinical readiness of the prediction tool.

## Feasibility

**The project is feasible for its core descriptive aims, supported by demonstrated preliminary work, a credible recruitment plan, and appropriate institutional resources, though schedule compression introduces uncertainty about completing the final integrated analyses within the proposed timeframe.**

**Preliminary work confirms practical viability.** Extraction of billing records from 71 stroke admissions demonstrated that therapy time can be assembled and related to length of stay, with a median stay of 12 days (SD 9.2) [1]. A pilot of eight participants with left-hemisphere stroke and aphasia confirmed that structural and resting-state imaging, combined with behavioral testing, can be obtained before and after intensive therapy, yielding interpretable principal-component structures that separated language and attention factors [1]. The imaging pilot further demonstrated that baseline resting-state connectivity measures—specifically global efficiency, interhemispheric connectivity, and default-mode-to-auditory and salience-to-visual connectivity—were associated with domain-specific treatment gains [1]. These pilot data do not guarantee the larger study’s findings, but they confirm that the measurement protocols, imaging acquisition, and analytical pipelines function as intended in the target population.

**Recruitment is credibly specified with contingency provisions.** The study will consent 400 participants across three inpatient rehabilitation facilities (Shirley Ryan AbilityLab in Chicago, Alexian Brothers Rehabilitation Hospital, and Mary Free Bed Rehabilitation Hospital in Grand Rapids) over approximately 30 months, aiming to retain 300 at 18 months [1]. Attrition is projected at 15% by six months and an additional 10% by 18 months, yielding a final sample of 300 [1]. These projections are informed by the team’s experience in rehabilitation populations and appear realistic for a longitudinal observational study with regular research assistant contact. The eligibility criteria—first left-hemisphere infarct with aphasia, age at least 21, first IRF admission, no right-hemisphere lesions or other neurological conditions likely to affect cognition or speech—define a clinically coherent population [1]. The proposal explicitly anticipates that slow recruitment or excessive loss may be addressed by longer accrual, added recruitment resources, or the addition of a fourth site to preserve the 300-person follow-up target [1]. This contingency planning is appropriate and reflects practical experience with multi-site rehabilitation research.

**Institutional resources are strong.** The Shirley Ryan AbilityLab provides the specialized MRI capability for the 100-participant imaging subsample, with a protocol that includes two resting-state scans, T1 anatomical imaging, T2 FLAIR, and two-shell diffusion imaging [1]. The other sites contribute clinical imaging and the core behavioral data, ensuring the broader cohort remains informative even without research-grade neuroimaging. REDCap supports common case-report forms and central data checks [1], and Northwestern University serves as the single IRB [1], reducing administrative complexity across the three sites.

**Schedule compression introduces a manageable but real risk.** The five-year timeline concentrates the primary IGC modeling, bootstrap stability assessment, and cross-validation into approximately five quarters—Year 4 Q4 through Year 5 Q4—while the last 18-month outcome assessments continue through Year 5 Q1 [1]. The final enrolled participants’ complete longitudinal data, including all primary and secondary outcomes and the full post-discharge therapy tracking, arrives only three quarters before the grant ends. Formal data cleaning windows are calendared at Year 1 Q4, Years 2–3 Q2/Q4, and Year 4 Q2 [1], but the last of these precedes the final 18-month assessments by three quarters. Therapy extraction and coding is scheduled through Year 4 Q2, yet participants enrolled late in the accrual window continue receiving outpatient and community services tracked through their 18-month follow-up [1]. The proposal does not include an explicit, dedicated final data lock, cleaning, and integration milestone after the last assessment window closes.

In mitigation, the staggered enrollment pattern means that participants enrolled in the first 18 months of accrual have their complete data available by roughly Year 4 Q1, providing approximately eight quarters for analysis for about three-quarters of the sample. IGC models can be developed iteratively on early-completing participants and refined as data accumulate. The imaging data—acquired at admission only [1]—is available well before the analysis phase, so the schedule risk for neuroimaging pertains to processing throughput rather than data availability. The genetic assays, using commercial TaqMan allelic-discrimination assays on saliva collected near admission [1], can similarly be batched before the modeling phase.

The core enrollment, measurement, and data collection activities are feasible based on the team’s track record and institutional resources. The schedule compression represents a real risk that the final integrated analyses incorporating the complete cohort’s 18-month outcomes may require a no-cost extension or post-grant completion to reach their full potential. The iterative nature of the modeling approach and the staggered enrollment pattern mean the primary trajectory estimation (Aim 1) can proceed meaningfully with most of the sample well within the grant period.

## Ethics and safety

**The ethics and safety plan is excellent, proactively addressing the ethically complex aspects of studying individuals with aphasia through carefully designed consent procedures, proportionate risk mitigation, and robust oversight structures.**

**Consent procedures are appropriately adapted.** The project explicitly requires that participants have sufficient capacity to consent and participate in testing [1], and it uses supported conversation techniques during recruitment and consent to ensure that aphasia does not itself prevent understanding [1]. This approach recognizes that communication barriers do not equate to decisional incapacity and that appropriate accommodations can uphold participant autonomy—an ethically important commitment that is well-suited to this population.

**Risk identification and mitigation are thorough and proportionate.** The proposal identifies fatigue, frustration, MRI discomfort, and loss of confidentiality as material risks, and addresses each with practical strategies: frequent breaks, the option to split follow-up visits across consecutive days, home-based assessments where appropriate, and trained communication support [1]. The follow-up sessions last approximately three to four hours [1]; splitting them across days directly reduces the burden that could otherwise lead to excessive fatigue or dropout. The MRI protocol includes explicit screening for implants, other MRI contraindications, claustrophobia, and pregnancy considerations, with the requirement that only eligible participants undergo research scanning [1]. The imaging subsample is embedded within the broader study such that participants who cannot undergo MRI can still contribute the core observational data and available clinical imaging [1], ensuring that exclusion from the imaging component does not deprive participants of the study’s primary benefits or scientific contribution.

**Data management and sharing provisions are strong.** Research records are separate from medical records and use codes rather than names; electronic files are password protected, physical records secured, and access restricted [1]. The data-sharing plan structures access appropriately: the de-identified REDCap dataset will be available on request under an agreement that explicitly prohibits reidentification and unauthorized redistribution, defines reporting responsibilities, and requires destruction of data after analyses [1]. Shared data include behavioral and patient-reported outcomes, treatment exposure, relevant imaging measures, and candidate-marker information [1]. This structured rather than fully open sharing approach is appropriate given the identifiability concerns inherent in the combination of rare clinical phenotypes, detailed demographic and treatment data, and genetic information. Participation is voluntary, withdrawal will not affect clinical services, and treatment remains part of ordinary clinical care [1], meaning participants face no therapeutic disadvantage from declining or discontinuing participation.

**The oversight architecture is well-developed.** Northwestern University serves as the single IRB [1], reducing regulatory fragmentation across the three sites. A Data Monitoring and Oversight Committee, including clinical, imaging, genetic, epidemiologic, and patient and family perspectives, will review readiness initially and then safety, burden, progress, and data integrity annually [1]. The inclusion of patient and family perspectives on this committee is a notable strength, ensuring that the lived experience of aphasia informs ongoing oversight of study conduct. Quality monitoring is systematic: ten percent of assessment sessions will be videotaped and rescored, ten percent of case-report forms rechecked, and a separate ten-percent data-entry sample verified [1]. Adverse events are communicated through site leadership to the co-PIs and institutional oversight according to a stated reporting schedule [1]. These measures collectively provide assurance that ethical and safety concerns will be detected and addressed throughout the study. The ethical framework is well-developed, proportionate to the identified risks, and appropriately tailored to a population whose communication needs require deliberate accommodation rather than paternalistic exclusion.

## Management

**The coordinating team combines complementary expertise that directly maps onto the project’s multi-modal demands, and the operational infrastructure is well-specified at the level of site coordination, data management, and quality control—but the schedule does not adequately sequence the final data integration and analysis activities, introducing a material risk that the primary modeling phase will proceed on incomplete or partially cleaned data.**

**Team expertise is well-matched to the project.** The principal investigators, Allen W. Heinemann and Leora R. Cherney [1], bring established track records in the two domains most critical to the project’s success. Heinemann’s prior IGC applications in spinal cord injury demonstrated how demographic, injury, and rehabilitation characteristics can describe individual plateaus and rates [1] [2], while Cherney’s work on aphasia measurement anchors the linguistic and participation outcome domain. The additional team members contribute expertise in genomics, neuroimaging, and biostatistics [1], providing the necessary depth for the genetic and imaging components. This configuration is well-matched to the proposed work: the study does not require expertise the team lacks, and the division between clinical aphasia science and quantitative rehabilitation outcomes modeling is covered by the two co-PIs.

**Operational coordination is supported by concrete mechanisms.** Northwestern University serves as the single IRB [1], avoiding the regulatory delays and inconsistencies that can arise with multi-site IRB review. REDCap provides common case-report forms and central data checks [1], enabling standardized data entry and real-time quality monitoring across sites. Dedicated research assistants at each site screen admissions daily, coordinate assessments, and maintain post-discharge contact [1], while NIHSS certification, standardized training, and continuous speech-language pathology supervision support consistency [1]. The therapist training protocol—requiring clinicians to exceed 90% reliability in recording timing and modality, with ten percent of sessions video-recorded for verification [1]—is a well-defined quality assurance procedure. The Data Monitoring and Oversight Committee, with annual reviews of readiness, safety, burden, progress, and data integrity [1], and the systematic quality monitoring plan (ten percent of assessments videotaped and rescored, ten percent of case-report forms rechecked, ten percent of data entry verified) [1] provide ongoing quality surveillance. The data-sharing infrastructure, with de-identified data available on request under a structured agreement [1], satisfies dissemination commitments.

**Schedule sequencing is the critical management weakness.** The five-year timeline [1] concentrates the primary IGC modeling, bootstrap stability assessment, and cross-validation into approximately five quarters (Year 4 Q4 through Year 5 Q4), while the last 18-month outcome assessments extend through Year 5 Q1 [1]. This means the final enrolled participants’ complete longitudinal data—including WAB–R, CPIB, SAQoL-39, all secondary outcomes, and the full 18 months of post-discharge therapy tracking—arrives only three quarters before the grant ends. The formal data cleaning windows for Aims 1 and 2 are calendared at Year 1 Q4, Years 2–3 Q2/Q4, and Year 4 Q2 [1], but the last of these precedes the final 18-month assessments by three quarters. Therapy extraction and coding is scheduled through Year 4 Q2 [1], yet participants enrolled late in the accrual window continue receiving outpatient and community services through their 18-month follow-up [1]. No explicit final data lock, cleaning, and integration milestone appears in the schedule table or narrative after Year 5 Q1.

The staggered enrollment pattern partially mitigates this compression—participants enrolled in the first 18 months of accrual will have their complete data available by roughly Year 4 Q1, providing approximately eight quarters for analysis for about three-quarters of the sample. IGC models can be developed iteratively on early-completing participants and refined as data accumulate. The imaging processing pipeline, adapted from the Human Connectome Project and incorporating multiple software packages with lesion-aware quality control [1], benefits from admission-only acquisition, and genetic assays on saliva samples accumulate throughout enrollment and can be batched. However, the absence of a dedicated final data lock and integration phase after the last patient’s last visit means that either the final integrated models will be built on data that is not yet fully cleaned or locked, or the analysis will need to compress into approximately three quarters with dissemination running concurrently. Either scenario represents a management risk that the proposal acknowledges only indirectly, through the statement that the schedule “allows extension or an additional site if accrual or retention is insufficient” [1]—a contingency that addresses enrollment volume but not the analysis timeline. The Aim 2 integrated models and Aim 3 validation activities, which depend on the complete, cleaned dataset, are most exposed to this compression, while Aim 1 trajectory estimation is better protected by the iterative modeling approach and the earlier availability of data from the first three-quarters of enrollees.

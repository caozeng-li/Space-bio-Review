# Project review

## Overall assessment

The PRECISE project proposes to test whether a centralized telephone patient navigation program improves completion of follow-up colonoscopy among safety-net patients with an abnormal fecal immunochemical test (FIT) result. Conducted at Sea Mar Community Health Centers in Washington State, the study combines external validation of a prediction model for colonoscopy non-adherence with a pragmatic, patient-randomized trial comparing telephone navigation to usual care for patients identified as having moderate or low probability of completing follow-up on their own. The trial's most valuable expected contribution is robust, real-world evidence on the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of a scalable navigation intervention in a Federally Qualified Health Center, where follow-up rates are critically low.

The proposal is strongly grounded in the research team's extensive preliminary work, including the STOP CRC trial, which documented the problem of low colonoscopy adherence and developed the initial prediction model. The collaboration with the Sea Mar health system, clear methodological plans for external validation, randomization, outcome ascertainment, and economic evaluation from a health-plan perspective all support the study's feasibility and rigor. The primary limitations center on the central innovation of risk-stratified targeting: the prediction model's ability to discriminate between patients who will and will not complete colonoscopy without help is modest (bootstrap-corrected c-statistic of approximately 0.65), and the trial is underpowered to detect plausible treatment-effect heterogeneity across risk strata. Evidence from the completed trial confirms these limitations, showing a clear overall navigation benefit but no significant differential effect by predicted adherence.

On balance, the proposal outlines a high-quality, pragmatic effectiveness trial addressing a clinically important question for underserved populations. The main effect of navigation is clearly established by the study, making a valuable contribution. The proposed innovation of using a prediction model to target those most in need is a sound concept, but the model's performance and the eventual null finding on heterogeneity materially limit the additional knowledge gained from this element of the design. Nonetheless, the core trial is well-conceived and its successful completion validates the overall scientific approach. The work delivers clear, actionable evidence on telephone navigation in a real-world FQHC setting, making it worthy of support.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**The question is important and the trial's primary answer constitutes a clear gain in practical knowledge; the contribution from risk-stratified targeting is much more limited than the proposal envisions.**

Colonoscopy follow-up after a positive FIT is the indispensable second step that makes stool-based colorectal cancer screening effective, yet it remains conspicuously suboptimal in the safety-net settings that serve populations with the highest disease burden. The proposal documents that only 54% of FIT-positive patients in the STOP CRC trial completed colonoscopy within one year, despite 87% receiving a referral [1]. Comparable low rates—18% to 57% across eight Southern California Federally Qualified Health Centers [2] and 36% at six months in the CARES trial [3]—underscore that this is a pervasive implementation failure, not an isolated observation. Establishing whether a scalable, telephone-based navigation program that does not depend on parallel research infrastructure can meaningfully close this gap is a question with direct consequences for health systems that serve low-income, underinsured, and minority populations.

The trial's core finding—a 13 percentage-point increase in one-year colonoscopy completion (55.1% vs. 42.1%, P \< .001) [4]—is a substantive contribution in precisely the terms the proposal set out: a pragmatic, intention-to-treat estimate from a patient-randomized design conducted within the ordinary clinical workflows of a large FQHC. The adverse baseline environment in which this effect was obtained strengthens rather than weakens its significance. The trial spanned the COVID-19 pandemic, endured nine navigators cycling through a single full-time position, and still achieved an absolute improvement consistent with or exceeding that reported by earlier, smaller randomized studies in integrated systems with stronger usual-care infrastructure [4]. An NNT of approximately 8 to produce one additional completed colonoscopy provides health-system decision-makers with a concrete parameter for resource planning.

Where the proposal overstates its attainable knowledge is in the question of targeting. The stated aim is not simply to test whether navigation works on average, but whether navigation effectiveness differs by patients' predicted probability of colonoscopy adherence—the comparison that would justify directing scarce navigation resources preferentially to those least likely to complete follow-up without help [1]. The prediction model that underwrites this question has a bootstrap-corrected c-statistic of approximately 0.65 [1], a level of discrimination that the Hayward et al. framework shows can enhance power for subgroup analyses in principle but which the authors themselves characterize as insufficient to achieve even moderate statistical power when the AUROC falls below 0.6 [5].

The separation of usual-care adherence rates across probability strata in the completed trial—43.4% in the lowest tertile versus 48.8% in the highest [4]—is a narrow 5.4 percentage points, a fraction of the roughly 68-point spread observed across quintiles in the STOP CRC development cohort [1]. The consequence is that the trial, despite being the largest randomized evaluation of navigation for follow-up colonoscopy to date, could not detect significant treatment-effect heterogeneity (P = 0.16) [4], and the counterintuitive pattern—the smallest absolute benefit appearing in the lowest-probability stratum [4]—means the data provide no empirical support for the targeting hypothesis. The knowledge gained from this element of the design is therefore confirmatory only of the null.

The economic and secondary contributions partially rescue the value proposition. The cost and cost-effectiveness analyses, done from a health-plan perspective [1], address the implementation question that matters for sustainability: what does it cost to achieve an additional colonoscopy, and how does that vary across probability strata? The one-year horizon limits the outcomes to process measures and intermediate endpoints rather than life-years gained [1], but the budget-impact scenarios and number-needed-to-navigate estimates by stratum [1] produce information that a health-system manager can use even if the cost-effectiveness ratio is not itself decisive. The qualitative work—approximately 60 patient interviews and 25–30 staff interviews [1]—adds explanatory depth about barriers and program experience that the quantitative stratum comparisons could not resolve.

In sum, PRECISE makes a genuine, well-supported contribution to the evidence base for patient navigation in FQHCs. Its headline effectiveness result is robust and actionable. The central proposition that a prediction model can efficiently target navigation to those most in need remains, contrary to the proposal's framing, substantially unproven by the work. The study's value lies in the demonstration that telephone navigation works in real-world safety-net conditions, and in the economic and qualitative evidence that helps health systems decide whether and how to implement it—not in resolving the stratification question that motivated the design.

## Innovation

**The proposal takes a reasonable but ultimately unconfirmed step beyond prior work; its most original element—risk-stratified targeting of navigation—is constrained by the discrimination of the prediction model and did not yield confirmatory evidence in the completed trial.**

The innovation claim rests on four features: targeting colonoscopy follow-up rather than initial screening, testing navigation effects within predicted-adherence strata, measuring value for money across those strata, and using trained clinic staff embedded in ordinary workflows rather than a parallel research-only service [1]. Each is a genuine extension of the existing literature, but they differ materially in how much they actually add.

Targeting follow-up rather than screening does represent a shift in emphasis. Most prior navigation trials and programs—including the New Hampshire Colorectal Cancer Screening Program from which PRECISE adapts its materials [1], the Raich et al. trial in Denver's safety-net system [6], and Coronado's own STOP CRC and SMARTER CRC work [7]—focused on increasing initial screening uptake. The proposal correctly identifies that colonoscopy completion after a positive FIT is a distinct clinical moment with different barriers (bowel preparation, transport, arranging an escort, cost, fear [1]) and a smaller, higher-risk patient population. This reorientation from the screening step to the diagnostic-resolution step is a meaningful change in the question, not merely an extension of the population.

The use of clinic-based staff rather than research-dedicated personnel is another distinguishing feature. The Green et al. trial used registered nurses already practicing within Group Health [8]; the New Hampshire program used a nurse with free colonoscopy services funded by the CDC [4]; Raich et al. employed lay navigators [6]. PRECISE proposed nurse practitioners or staff with a similar clinical role and medical-record access, delivering navigation by telephone as part of their clinic responsibilities [1]. In practice, the navigators were medical assistants or care coordinators [4], which if anything strengthens the argument that the model is reproducible in resource-constrained FQHCs. This is a genuine design choice with practical implications; it tests whether navigation can be absorbed into existing clinic operations rather than remaining dependent on grant-funded personnel.

However, the central innovation—the risk-stratified targeting that gives the project its name (PRECISE) and its scientific identity—is where the originality claims exceed what the design could deliver. The idea of using a multivariable prediction model to identify patients unlikely to complete colonoscopy without help is sensible in principle and follows logically from the Hayward et al. framework [5]. But the proposal presents testing effects within predicted-adherence strata [1] and measuring variation in effect according to predicted adherence [1] as a confirmatory aim, when the prediction model's bootstrap-corrected c-statistic of approximately 0.65—and the external validation showing a drop to 0.55 at Sea Mar before redevelopment [9]—means the separation between strata is simply too modest to support the intended inference.

The development model showed a 25% to 93% spread across quintiles [1]; the completed trial showed 43.4% to 48.8% across tertiles in the usual-care arm [4]. The practical consequence is that the high-adherence group that was supposed to be observed without navigation still had nearly half of its members not completing colonoscopy on their own—hardly a group for whom navigation can be confidently withheld. The prior Coronado et al. trial at Kaiser Permanente Northwest, using the same prediction model with 415 patients, similarly found no significant heterogeneity across probability bands and acknowledged it was underpowered for that analysis [10]. The proposal's detectable interaction odds ratios of 2.17–2.98 [1] are so large that even a genuinely differential effect of plausible magnitude would escape detection. Framing this as an innovation that the trial could validate, rather than as an exploratory question for which only descriptive patterns could be obtained, overstates what was achievable.

The economic stratification—estimating cost-effectiveness and numbers needed to navigate by probability stratum [1]—is a useful descriptive addition that does not depend on statistically significant interactions. Showing that the cost per additional colonoscopy varies across strata, even without formal heterogeneity testing, provides health-system managers with information they can apply under different assumptions about targeting. This is original in the FQHC context and the methods are sound, but it is an incremental refinement rather than a conceptual advance.

In weighing the whole, PRECISE advances the literature primarily by demonstrating that telephone navigation works in a real-world FQHC under pragmatic conditions—an important complement to trials conducted in integrated systems with stronger usual-care infrastructure. Its most distinctive stated contribution, the validation of a prediction-model-based targeting strategy, was a well-motivated attempt that the evidence from the completed trial did not support. The innovation is genuine in its intent and sensible in its logic, but its scientific yield was substantially below what the proposal's framing implied.

## Rigor

**The study design supports the primary effectiveness inference well; the central limitation is that the trial cannot answer its own stratification question, and the within-system randomization introduces contamination risks that are identified but not fully resolved.**

The two-phase structure—external validation of the existing prediction model in a distinct Sea Mar cohort, followed by a patient-randomized trial—is a logical sequence that addresses the known risk of model transportability before committing patients to stratification [1]. The validation plan specifies discrimination assessment via c-statistic with a decline of ≥0.05 triggering model updating, calibration plots comparing predicted and observed probabilities in quintiles, and recalibration or redevelopment if needed [1]. This was prescient: the published recalibration study confirmed the original model's c-statistic dropped from 0.66 to 0.61 at Sea Mar, with the bootstrap-corrected value falling to 0.55, exceeding the proposal's own inferior-separation threshold [9]. The model was redeveloped, yielding a bootstrap-corrected c-statistic of 0.61 [9]. The Phase I plan was therefore well-conceived and necessary, even though the redeveloped model still provided only modest discrimination.

### Strengths of the trial design

The randomized trial itself has clear strengths. The primary outcome—chart-confirmed colonoscopy within one year—is an objective endpoint ascertained from EHR codes supplemented by blinded chart audits for patients without coded completion [1]. The proposal's prior work established that codes alone retrieved approximately 80% of colonoscopy reports, and the auditor is explicitly blinded to assignment [1]. This mitigates detection bias from the unblinded design: the meta-epidemiological literature shows that lack of blinding inflates effect estimates primarily when outcomes involve subjective judgment [11], while objective endpoints with blinded ascertainment are substantially less vulnerable [11]. The primary analysis uses multilevel logistic regression with study arm as a fixed effect and clinic as a random effect, following intention to treat [1], which appropriately handles the clustered data structure.

The power calculation is well-specified and realistic for the main effect. Starting from 1,186 annual positive FIT results, allowing 3.5% not needing colonoscopy, and selecting approximately 80% with lower predicted adherence yields 924 randomized patients across 28 clinics [1]. At an assumed 44% usual-care completion rate, two-sided alpha 0.05, and 80% power, the detectable improvement is 12.9 percentage points with an ICC of 0.03 (OR 1.68) or 9.2 points without the cluster design effect (OR 1.45) [1]. The published trial achieved 967 patients in the modified intention-to-treat analysis with an observed risk difference of 13.0 percentage points and an adjusted OR of 1.69 [4], confirming that the power assumptions were adequate and the detected effect was close to the design target.

### The stratification power limitation

The design's principal rigor limitation concerns the stratification analysis. The same power calculation reveals that the detectable interaction odds ratios for testing treatment-effect heterogeneity by predicted-adherence stratum are 2.98 (with the cluster design effect) or 2.17 (without it) [1]. These represent differential effects of implausible magnitude for a behavioral support intervention—a consequence of the fundamental statistical reality that interaction tests require substantially more information than main-effect tests, particularly in cluster-randomized designs [12]. The Hayward et al. framework, which the proposal cites, showed that risk indices with AUROC below 0.6 do not achieve moderate statistical power, and their worked example used 8,800 patients to demonstrate the method [5]. PRECISE's 924 patients in 28 clusters, combined with a prediction model at the margin of useful discrimination, could not provide confirmatory evidence on the stratification hypothesis. The proposal would be stronger if it acknowledged this explicitly and framed the stratum-specific analyses as descriptive rather than confirmatory.

### Contamination and unblinding

The within-system, individually-randomized, unblinded design creates a genuine contamination concern. Randomizing patients within 28 clinics of a single health system means the same clinicians and referral coordinators manage both navigation and usual-care patients [1]. Navigators, being clinic-based staff with EHR access [1], may influence the overall follow-up workflow in ways that benefit control patients. The proposal's monitoring—a 10% sample of usual-care encounters assessed for changes and possible contamination [1]—is a useful fidelity check but provides only qualitative evidence and cannot yield a quantitative bias correction.

The direction of net bias is uncertain: contamination from shared clinical processes would attenuate the treatment effect toward the null, while unblinding of patients could inflate it (navigation patients feeling more accountable, control patients potentially demoralized). These forces pull in opposite directions. The Green et al. precedent is instructive: an individually-randomized navigation trial at Group Health found a non-significant 10.1 percentage-point difference (91.0% vs. 80.8%, P = 0.10) in a system where usual care already included an EHR-based positive-FOBT registry with persistent physician reminders [8]. The authors attributed the null result partly to strong usual-care infrastructure leaving limited room for navigation to show an incremental effect [8]. PRECISE's lower baseline completion rate (44% assumed, 42.1% observed [4]) provides more room to detect an effect, but also means that any contamination-driven improvement in the control arm would directly consume the detectable margin.

### Secondary outcomes and fidelity

The outcome-ascertainment plan includes appropriate detail beyond the primary endpoint. Secondary outcomes—time to colonoscopy, missed and cancelled appointments, colonoscopy quality, adenoma and cancer detection—are specified with operational definitions and data sources [1]. The proposal acknowledges that time-to-treatment analyses may be underpowered given the anticipated approximately 59 cancers [1]. Fidelity monitoring is thorough: 25% of telephone contacts recorded during the first three months and new navigators' first three months, decreasing to 5% thereafter, with review of timing and content against the six-topic contact sequence [1]. This is a genuine strength of the implementation-science orientation.

The qualitative component is well-integrated. Approximately 60 patient interviews will purposively sample both probability groups and both completers and non-completers, while 25–30 staff interviews will examine adaptation and sustainability [1]. The coding plan using content analysis, the Chronic Care Model framework, and emergent themes, with consensus resolution of disagreements and advisory-board interpretation of de-identified findings [1], follows established practice for implementation research. The qualitative work provides explanatory depth that the underpowered quantitative interaction tests cannot supply, though it cannot substitute for them in testing the targeting hypothesis.

Overall, the design is rigorous for its primary aim of estimating the average effectiveness of telephone navigation. The outcome is objective, the ascertainment is blinded, the analysis plan is appropriate for the clustered data, and the power calculation is realistic. The limitations—underpowered interaction tests, contamination risk from within-system randomization, and dependence on a prediction model with modest discrimination—are inherent to the pragmatic approach and the specific scientific question. The proposal identifies most of these constraints but does not fully acknowledge their implications for the stratification analysis that it presents as a central contribution.

## Feasibility

**The project is a pragmatic trial conducted within an existing clinical system by a team with demonstrated capacity to execute this exact type of work; the logistics, access, and resources are well-established, and the trial was successfully completed.**

The feasibility of PRECISE rests on three interlocking elements that are each substantiated in the proposal: access to a sufficiently large patient population with the outcome of interest, an operational infrastructure that can support the research procedures, and a research team with direct prior experience conducting similar work in similar settings.

The enrollment foundation is Sea Mar Community Health Centers, which operates 28 nonspecialty clinics serving approximately 29,000 screening-eligible patients, of whom roughly 37% are Latino [1]. Planning data estimated 1,186 positive FIT results annually at a 9% positivity rate, with approximately 949 falling in the moderate or low adherence strata before final eligibility exclusions [1]. These figures are derived from the health system's own EHR data for the period July 2016 to June 2017 [1], not from external extrapolations, lending them credibility. The proposal specifies 924 randomized patients (462 per arm) enrolled over 18 months [1], which translates to enrolling approximately 51 patients per month across 28 clinics—a feasible volume given the annual positive-FIT pool. The published trial ultimately enrolled 985 participants [4], confirming that the enrollment projections were realistic.

The operational infrastructure depends on Sea Mar's integrated EHR, which supports the proposed workflow of identifying FIT-positive patients, applying the prediction model using eight routinely available variables, generating study IDs, and supplying de-identified data through secure quarterly transfers [1]. The EHR-based identification and data-capture approach was successfully used in STOP CRC across 26 clinics in eight health centers [1], representing a directly transferable methodology. The navigation program adapts materials from the established New Hampshire Colorectal Cancer Screening Program, modified for Sea Mar's local GI referral arrangements, insurance landscape, and community resources [1]. The six-topic contact sequence—covering barrier assessment, bowel preparation, transport, appointment confirmation, post-procedure support, and results understanding [1]—is a structured protocol that can be taught and fidelity-monitored. Training includes two didactic days followed by eight weeks of coaching, with competency and fidelity checklists, weekly meetings for the first three months transitioning to biweekly thereafter, and quarterly specialist consultation [1]. This represents a realistic training investment for clinic-embedded staff.

The research team's track record is the strongest feasibility argument. Coronado led STOP CRC, which involved approximately 50,000 patients across 26 clinics and successfully implemented mailed FIT outreach with EHR-based tracking [1]. The same team developed the prediction model from 1,122 FIT-positive patients with one-year follow-up [1], conducted the Kaiser Permanente Northwest targeted-navigation pilot with 415 patients [10], and designed the cluster-randomized SMARTER CRC trial in rural Medicaid populations [7]. The Sea Mar partnership is institutionalized through co-investigator Ricardo Jimenez, Sea Mar's medical director [1], with commitments for staff, training, tracking, and a clinical champion [1]. The advisory board structure including clinic and patient representatives, gastroenterology providers, researchers, payers, and policymakers [1] provides both implementation guidance and dissemination pathways.

The five-year schedule [1] is appropriately sequenced: regulatory approval (months 1–3), model validation (months 4–7), materials adaptation (months 5–9), randomization (months 12–14), intervention delivery (months 15–34), qualitative interviews (months 34–40), follow-up data collection and chart abstraction (months 36–53), and analysis through dissemination (months 47–60). This allows adequate time for the validation phase to inform the trial and for the one-year follow-up window to elapse before analysis.

Critical dependencies identified in the proposal—changing real-world clinic processes, supportive local leadership, and the prediction model's performance [1]—were all borne out in practice. The published trial experienced COVID-19 disruptions, nine navigators cycling through one position, and the prediction model requiring redevelopment rather than recalibration [4] [9], yet still achieved its primary endpoint. This outcome validates the fundamental feasibility judgement: the infrastructure, population, and team were sufficient to execute the study as designed, even under adverse conditions the proposal could not have anticipated.

## Ethics and safety

**The proposal describes ethically appropriate arrangements that manage the limited risks proportionately; the research procedures augment existing clinical care rather than substituting for it, and the data-protection and consent provisions are fit for purpose.**

PRECISE does not introduce an experimental clinical procedure or withhold indicated care. Navigation helps patients access colonoscopy that is already clinically recommended following a positive FIT result [1]; patients in both arms remain under their usual providers' care and can complete colonoscopy through existing referral pathways. The intervention addresses practical and informational barriers—bowel preparation, transport, scheduling, insurance, fear [1]—rather than altering the diagnostic or therapeutic pathway itself. This framing is important because it means the ethical calculus is not about equipoise between two clinical strategies but about whether the research procedures for evaluating a support service are proportionate and fair.

The proposal specifies that IRB approval from the Kaiser Permanente Northwest Institutional Review Board and the Sea Mar Research Committee will be obtained during months 1–3 of the five-year schedule [1]. The published trial confirms this was secured (protocol number 00000779) and that the need for informed consent was waived for the trial itself due to minimal risks [4]. Verbal consent was obtained specifically for recording navigation telephone calls, which were sampled at 25% during the first three months and a new navigator's first three months, decreasing to 5% thereafter [1]. This graduated sampling approach respects privacy by reducing recording intensity once fidelity is established, while the verbal consent mechanism is appropriate for a telephone-based intervention where written consent would be impractical and potentially alienating.

The data-protection arrangements are well-specified. A secure interactive database populates de-identified EHR data, assigns study IDs, and supplies the eight predictor variables needed for risk classification [1]. Secure quarterly transfers support the study data flow, and controlled clinical-record access is limited to staff with a legitimate care or research role [1]. The chart auditor reviewing colonoscopy completion is blinded to assignment [1], which serves both scientific and ethical functions: it protects against ascertainment bias while ensuring that the audit process does not differentially expose patients in one arm to additional scrutiny. The advisory board reviews de-identified interview data [1], maintaining the separation between research analysis and identifiable clinical information.

The qualitative component receives appropriate ethical attention. Patient interviews (approximately 60, sampling both probability groups and both completers and non-completers) are conducted in English and Spanish [1], matching the linguistic diversity of the Sea Mar population where approximately 37% of screening-eligible patients are Latino [1]. Staff interviews (25–30 among navigators, clinic staff, and GI providers) examine adaptation and sustainability [1]. The proposal states that transcripts will be coded and that disagreements will be resolved by consensus, with the advisory board helping interpret de-identified findings [1]. While the proposal does not detail a formal distress protocol for interviews in which patients might disclose fear, financial hardship, or negative healthcare experiences, the navigation program itself is designed to address exactly these barriers [1], creating a natural pathway for referral to support if interview discussions reveal unmet needs.

The community advisory board—including clinic and patient representatives, gastroenterology providers, researchers, payers, and policymakers [1]—provides a structural mechanism for community oversight that extends beyond regulatory compliance. Quarterly meetings throughout the five-year project period allow ongoing input on study conduct and interpretation, which is particularly relevant for a trial conducted in a safety-net population where historical research exploitation warrants deliberate community engagement.

The post-trial access provision is ethically sound: "If navigation is effective, it will subsequently be offered to usual-care and surveillance patients who have not completed colonoscopy" [1]. This addresses the concern that control-arm patients might be denied a beneficial service after contributing data to demonstrate its value. Because the primary outcome is colonoscopy within one year and the post-trial offer occurs after that window, the provision does not interfere with endpoint ascertainment.

The published trial experienced two developments relevant to this assessment that the proposal could not have anticipated: COVID-19-related care disruptions and high navigator turnover (nine individuals in one position) [4]. The research team adapted training to pre-recorded videos and interactive webinars [4], a reasonable response to pandemic constraints. These developments did not represent ethical failures in the original plan; they were operational challenges managed within the existing ethical framework.

## Management

**The management plan is appropriate for the proposed work: the PI has deep, directly relevant experience, the partnership with Sea Mar is institutionalized, and the team covers the necessary disciplinary range; the high navigator turnover experienced in the completed trial reveals a dependency that the proposal's training and support provisions could mitigate but not eliminate.**

The principal investigator, Gloria Coronado, leads a team that has conducted the most consequential body of work on mailed FIT outreach and follow-up navigation in community health centers in the United States. The STOP CRC trial involved approximately 50,000 patients across 26 clinics in eight health centers and generated both the problem documentation (54% one-year colonoscopy completion despite 87% referral) and the prediction model that PRECISE builds upon [1]. The same group subsequently conducted the Kaiser Permanente Northwest targeted-navigation pilot [10] and designed the cluster-randomized SMARTER CRC trial in rural Medicaid populations [7]. This is not a team learning the setting or the intervention for the first time; it is a team refining a research program through successive iterations, each informing the next.

The Sea Mar partnership is anchored by co-investigator Ricardo Jimenez, Sea Mar's medical director [1], providing the institutional authority needed to integrate research procedures into clinical workflows. Sea Mar commits staff, training, tracking, and a clinical champion [1]—commitments that are specific enough to be meaningful without being so detailed as to imply the partnership is fragile. The advisory board structure, which includes clinic and patient representatives, gastroenterology providers, researchers, payers, and policymakers meeting quarterly throughout the five-year period [1], creates a governance mechanism that can identify and resolve implementation problems before they threaten the trial.

The disciplinary coverage is comprehensive for the stated aims. The team includes navigation and implementation expertise, gastroenterologists, prediction-modeling and economic-analysis specialists, qualitative researchers, and health-information support [1]. Each of the three specific aims—model validation and implementation, effectiveness and economic evaluation, and secondary outcomes with moderation analyses [1]—has identifiable expertise allocated to it. The economic evaluation, which requires distinct skills in trial-based costing, net-benefit regression, and budget-impact modeling [1], is supported by a plan that separates research costs from replication costs and uses staff logs, interviews, budgets, and national wage data [1], consistent with best practices for microcosting in implementation science [13].

The navigator training and support structure is detailed and appears adequate on paper: two didactic days followed by eight weeks of coaching, competency and fidelity checklists, weekly meetings for the first three months transitioning to biweekly, and quarterly specialist consultation [1]. The planning estimate of 126 minutes per navigated patient, approximately 970 hours for 462 patients [1], corresponds to roughly half a full-time equivalent over the 20-month intervention period [1], a realistic staffing level for one centralized navigator position.

The principal vulnerability that the completed trial exposed was navigator turnover: nine different staff members served in the single full-time navigator role during the study [4]. This turnover rate—essentially a new navigator every few months—far exceeds what the training plan could reasonably accommodate with full fidelity, since each new navigator required condensed and remote training during the pandemic [4]. The proposal's training provisions (two didactic days plus eight weeks of coaching) assume reasonable continuity; they cannot substitute for the institutional stability that keeps navigators in post. This is a dependency on the practice setting rather than a deficiency in the management plan per se, but it is a dependency with consequences.

The published trial nevertheless achieved its primary endpoint [4], suggesting that the overall management structure—including the EHR-based identification system, the structured contact protocol, and the institutional partnership—was resilient enough to absorb this disruption, even if the fidelity and reach of navigation delivery were compromised. The 94.2% colonoscopy completion rate among the 242 patients who actually received the allocated navigation [4], compared to 14.4% among the 247 who did not, underscores that management success in this context depends as much on reaching patients as on the quality of navigation once delivered.

The five-year schedule [1] is appropriately sequenced and allows adequate time for the Phase I validation to inform the Phase II trial, for the one-year follow-up window to elapse, and for the substantial chart-abstraction and analysis tasks. The long-term dissemination plans—embedding the prediction model in EHR workflows and producing a model specification guide and decision-support tools [1]—are sensible extensions of the research rather than speculative additions.

In summary, the management plan as submitted demonstrates a capable team with directly relevant experience, an institutionalized partnership with the study site, appropriate disciplinary coverage, and a realistic schedule. The navigator turnover that occurred in practice represents the most significant management challenge that the proposal's provisions could address only partially, but the trial's successful completion under those conditions validates the core management judgement.

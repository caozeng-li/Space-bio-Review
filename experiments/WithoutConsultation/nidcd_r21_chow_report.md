# Project review

## Overall assessment

This R21 project proposes an fMRI study of continuous storytelling speech in children aged 5–11, comparing those with persistent stuttering (pCWS), those who have recovered (rCWS), and fluent controls (CWNS). The central question is whether persistence is associated with anomalous left-hemisphere speech-motor activity and whether recovery involves normalization of left perisylvian activity or compensatory right-hemisphere recruitment. The study will use spatial independent component analysis (sICA) to remove speech-related motion artifacts from fMRI data, a method the team has validated against PET. The most valuable expected knowledge is the first functional characterization of brain activity during naturalistic connected speech in children who have recovered from stuttering—a genuinely new contribution that no published study has provided.

The proposal draws on substantial strengths: a well-characterized longitudinal cohort, the team's published expertise in pediatric stuttering neuroimaging and sICA denoising, and a coherent theoretical framework grounded in both adult functional and childhood structural preliminary data. Chief scientific limitations include: the cross-sectional design cannot establish developmental mechanisms; the SSI-4=10 threshold creates heterogeneous persistent and recovered groups, and the team's own follow-up data show approximately 39% reclassification over development; treatment history is recorded but not controlled, so recovery-related neural differences cannot be attributed specifically to spontaneous compensation; and power calculations, derived from adult effect sizes of uncertain transferability, are adequate primarily for large effects. The event-related analysis separating stuttering instances from fluent speech faces feasibility constraints given the team's prior childhood study could not achieve sufficient in-scanner events despite better acquisition parameters.

On balance, the proposal's genuine scientific contribution and the team's strong research base outweigh the recognized design limitations, given the exploratory R21 mechanism. The most secure and valuable outcome—the first three-group continuous-speech fMRI comparison—remains achievable even if the event-separation aim proves infeasible at the planned sample size. The study would not definitively answer the mechanism questions it poses, but it would substantially advance the descriptive foundation on which future longitudinal mechanistic work can be built. **The proposal warrants support, provided the interpretive limitations regarding recovery mechanisms, treatment confounds, and group heterogeneity are transparently reported.**

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

**The study addresses a genuinely important and timely question, and its most secure contribution—the first functional neuroimaging characterization of brain activity during naturalistic, connected speech in children who have recovered from stuttering compared to those who persist and fluent controls—fills a clear gap in the childhood stuttering literature.** The question of what neural activity patterns during continuous speech distinguish children whose stuttering persists from those who recover is central to understanding the neurobiology of stuttering, and it carries longer-term implications for identifying neural markers that could guide intervention timing and targets. The proposal's immediate purposes—distinguishing neural activity accompanying fluent speech from activity associated with individual stuttering instances, and characterizing recovery [1]—are appropriately ambitious for an exploratory R21 mechanism.

**The study's most certain knowledge gain is the between-group comparison of fluent, continuous speech across all three groups.** No published fMRI or fNIRS study has included a recovered group during connected speech in childhood; prior task-based childhood neuroimaging has compared children who stutter with fluent controls [2] [3] [4], including the team's own work showing reduced left ventral premotor activation during spontaneous speech in children with persistent stuttering [2]. The proposed three-group design with continuous storytelling can reveal whether recovered children's functional brain activity during natural speech resembles that of controls (suggesting normalization), resembles that of persistent children (suggesting residual neural anomaly despite behavioral recovery), or exhibits a distinct pattern (suggesting compensatory re-organization). This descriptive finding would materially advance the theoretical framework distinguishing left-hemisphere normalization from right-hemisphere compensation as recovery pathways [1].

**A secondary contribution is the attempt to separate transient stuttering-event activity from sustained fluent-speech activity within the same task** [1]. The team's adult preliminary data demonstrated increased putamen, SMA, and ventral premotor activity specifically during stuttering instances [1], and extending this dissociation to children would provide a finer-grained picture of the motor control disturbances underlying stuttering moments. However, the team's own prior childhood study (Chow et al., 2023) states that "as the majority of the participants produced relatively few stuttering moments (\<10) in the scanner, the statistical power for analyzing stuttering-related activity may not be sufficient" and that stuttering events were consequently treated as an effect of no interest [2]. This feasibility constraint narrows the confidence with which the event-related Aim 1b can be expected to succeed, but the between-group fluent-speech comparisons (Aims 1a and 2) do not depend on adequate stuttering instances and remain attainable.

**The proposal's value is supported by convergent structural neuroimaging evidence from the same longitudinal cohort.** Chow et al. (2023, *Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience*) found that recovered children showed higher white matter volume growth rates in tracts—including the left corona radiata, corpus callosum, and superior longitudinal fasciculus—where persistent children exhibited reduced volumes, "strongly suggesting that the WMV increases underlie a possible normalization or compensatory process associated with natural recovery" [5]. The proposed functional study would provide the complementary task-evoked activity data that the structural findings motivate but cannot supply. However, a recent resting-state fMRI study from the same cohort (Höbler et al., 2026) found no significant difference between persistent and recovered groups in network density of the speech planning loop, with only a trend-level effect in the motor loop [6], suggesting that recovery-related functional differences may be subtle and task-dependent—a finding that underscores rather than diminishes the value of examining task-evoked activity during continuous speech.

**The value is moderated by the cross-sectional design**, which "characterizes current group differences" rather than developmental mechanisms [1], and by the group definitions that place the SSI-4 threshold at the floor of clinical severity [5]. These limitations mean the study can generate but not test mechanistic hypotheses about recovery. Nevertheless, as an exploratory investigation that would produce the first functional dataset linking continuous-speech brain activity to clinically confirmed recovery status in childhood, the proposed work represents a genuine and worthwhile scientific contribution that can substantially inform future longitudinal studies.

**Rating: B (confidence: B)**

## Innovation

**The proposal addresses a clear gap in the childhood stuttering neuroimaging literature, and its most original feature—including a recovered group in a continuous-speech fMRI paradigm—distinguishes it from all published task-based studies in this age range.** Prior childhood neuroimaging of stuttering has compared children who stutter with fluent controls using resting-state fMRI [7], structural MRI and DTI [8] [5] [9], fNIRS during picture description [3], and task-based fMRI during spontaneous versus automatic speech [2]. None of these studies included a recovered group during connected speech production. The present proposal's three-group design (pCWS, rCWS, CWNS) during naturalistic storytelling therefore constitutes a genuinely novel investigation.

**A second novel element is the attempt to separate transient stuttering-event activity from sustained fluent-speech activity within the same continuous-speech task** [1]. The theoretical framework distinguishes a left-hemisphere speech-motor control system from right-hemisphere compensatory pathways [1], and the proposed mixed block/event GLM aims to dissociate these components empirically by modeling stuttering instances as event regressors while estimating sustained speech activity from block regressors [1]. The team's adult preliminary data already demonstrated this dissociation: fluent storytelling showed reduced left perisylvian activity in adults who stutter, while stuttering instances were associated with increased putamen, SMA, and ventral premotor activity [1]. Extending this within-subject dissociation to children would provide a finer-grained account of the motor control disturbances that characterize individual stuttering moments versus the neural organization supporting fluent intervals. The combination of the recovered-group comparison and the event-separation aim in a single pediatric study is, to our knowledge, unprecedented.

**The innovation is incremental rather than transformational in several respects.** The sICA denoising method is an established, validated technique from the team's prior work [10] [11]; the storytelling paradigm using *Frog, Where Are You?* has been used successfully in the team's cohort [1]; and the core finding of left premotor hypoactivation during spontaneous speech in children who stutter has already been reported by the same team [2]. The proposal extends these elements to a three-group design with event separation, which advances knowledge meaningfully but does not introduce a fundamentally new method or theoretical framework.

**The proposal's fallback position—that childhood activity could differ substantially from adult predictions and that disconfirmatory findings can revise the hypotheses** [1]—strengthens the innovation case rather than weakening it. An exploratory study that produces unexpected patterns in the recovered group or reveals that childhood stuttering-event activity differs from the adult pattern would generate data-driven hypotheses that are genuinely new, even if they depart from the proposal's stated predictions. The R21 mechanism is designed for exactly this kind of high-risk, hypothesis-generating work, and the study's capacity to produce informative results whether or not the specific hypotheses are confirmed is a strength of its innovative design.

**Rating: B (confidence: B)**

## Rigor

**The research design is appropriately constructed for the study's exploratory aims but contains material limitations that reduce the strength of the intended inferences, particularly for the recovery-specific contrasts and the event-related analysis.** The mixed block/event GLM, the sICA denoising pipeline, the speech coding procedures, and the use of permutation-based multiple-comparison correction are well-specified and grounded in the team's published methods. The proposal also honestly acknowledges key vulnerabilities and outlines fallback strategies. However, several design features—the power analysis, the group classification criteria, the absence of treatment control, and the linguistic comparability of groups during storytelling—collectively constrain what can be concluded, especially regarding the mechanisms of recovery.

### Power and sample size

The power analysis illustrates both the strengths and the limits of the inferential framework. Using effect sizes from the team's adult study (d=0.7 between-group, d=0.58 within-stuttering) [1], the proposal estimates 15 per group as sufficient at power 0.8 and plans 20 to accommodate exclusion and potentially smaller recovery effects. This is a defensible starting point for an R21. However, the transfer of adult effect sizes to children aged 5–11 is uncertain because children exhibit greater inter-subject variability in fMRI measures, and the team's own childhood study (Chow et al., 2023) acknowledged that its sample size of 22 CWS versus 18 controls "could be relatively small for detecting subtler effects" [2]. Empirical work by Grady et al. (2020) has shown that effect size estimates from small samples (n \~20) are substantially inflated and stabilize only with approximately 80 or more participants [12], and Kong et al. (2020) found that for effects in the range d=0.4–0.6, reproducibility rates reach only 80% when total sample size exceeds 100, with effect sizes from single datasets typically inflated [13].

At n=20 per group, the minimum detectable effect size at power 0.8 is approximately d=0.91 for between-group comparisons. The recovery-group contrasts in Aim 2—which are central to the proposal's scientific contribution—are not separately powered and would be sensitive primarily to large effects. Moderate effects in the range d=0.4–0.7, which may be more realistic for children, would be detectable only with substantially reduced power. Furthermore, the proposal does not quantitatively account for attrition from movement censoring (the Chow 2023 study excluded approximately 15% of scanned children for excessive movement [2]), task non-compliance (12 enrolled children did not complete MRI scanning in the Chow 2023 study [2]), and insufficient in-scanner stuttering instances, all of which will reduce effective sample sizes toward or below the powered minimum. The result is a study that can confidently detect only large neural differences, leaving null or marginal findings—particularly in the rCWS comparisons—ambiguous between genuine similarity and insufficient power. Null findings should not be interpreted as evidence of absence of recovery-related neural differences.

### Group classification

The group classification criteria introduce additional inferential constraint. The SSI-4 threshold of 10 corresponds to "very mild" in the SSI-4 severity classification [5] and sits at the lowest clinical severity boundary. This produces heterogeneous persistent and recovered groups, where a two-point shift on a multi-component instrument can change group assignment. Singer et al. (2021), using data that included children from this same longitudinal cohort, identified SSI ≥ 17 as the optimal predictive cutoff for persistence risk, yielding 93% sensitivity and 65% specificity [14]—nearly twice the threshold used here.

The classification instability is not hypothetical: the team's own follow-up data show that when 23 participants from the longitudinal cohort were reassessed as adolescents and young adults, recovery status changed for 9 (39.13%), including 5 late recoveries and 4 children whose clinician-observed very mild stuttering was not self-identified as stuttering [15]. This means the neural signatures identified at the time of scanning may not correspond to stable developmental outcomes.

### Treatment confound

Treatment history is recorded but not controlled through the design [1]. The meta-analytic evidence indicates that persistent children are disproportionately treated compared to recovered children: across 11 longitudinal studies, treatment rates in recovered groups ranged from 0% to 91%, while persistent groups showed systematically higher rates within each study [16]. This creates a systematic confound where any neural difference between pCWS and rCWS could reflect a neurobiological signature of persistence, a neural effect of speech therapy, or an interaction between the two. Including treatment type as a covariate in the GLM [1] cannot adequately resolve this when treatment is confounded with group membership, is heterogeneous in type and intensity, and the degrees of freedom for covariate adjustment are severely limited at n=20 per group. The Chow et al. (2023) structural MRI paper from this team, analyzing the same cohort, did not discuss treatment as a potential confound among its limitations [2], raising concern that treatment effects may be insufficiently addressed in the proposed fMRI study. This does not invalidate the pCWS-versus-CWNS comparison (Aim 1a) or the within-subject stuttering event contrast (Aim 1b), but it means Aim 2 cannot distinguish spontaneous neural compensation from treatment-induced plasticity.

### Task and baseline comparability

The storytelling task and its baseline introduce further interpretive challenges. Groups are unlikely to produce storytelling output matched on speech rate, syntactic complexity, and lexical diversity. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that children who stutter, as a group, score below fluent peers on overall language (Hedges's g = −0.48) and mean length of utterance (g = −0.23) [17], though the relationship between stuttering and linguistic complexity is not uniformly one of deficit—Sasisekaran and Basu (2023) found that school-age CWS actually produced higher IPsyn scores and more elaborated noun phrases than CWNS in conversational samples [18]. When groups differ in linguistic output, the BOLD contrast between them will conflate neural differences attributable to stuttering persistence with differences attributable to differing linguistic processing load. The proposal's plan to extract principal components of linguistic measures as covariates [1] is a defensible statistical strategy, but covariate adjustment cannot disentangle correlated predictors when group membership and linguistic productivity are systematically associated.

The overlearned-speech baseline (alphabet recitation) introduces a subtraction that conflates linguistic formulation, narrative construction, visual scene interpretation, and discourse planning—all of which may recruit perisylvian and prefrontal regions hypothesized to differ between groups. The adult study by AbdulSabur et al. (2014), on which the team's adult work is based, contrasted narrative production with narrative comprehension, isolating production-specific processes within a matched linguistic domain [19]. The proposed subtraction here is substantially less specific. Additionally, if overlearned sequential speech engages compensatory mechanisms in pCWS that are not engaged in CWNS, then the "storytelling minus alphabet" contrast will underestimate pCWS anomalies. A rest or fixation baseline, while less specific to speech motor control, would provide an orthogonal check on whether group differences reflect differential storytelling activity or differential baseline engagement.

### GLM design and sICA denoising

The mixed block/event GLM at TR=2s with sICA denoising is otherwise well-conceived. The natural variability of stuttering event timing within storytelling blocks provides effective temporal jitter for decorrelating sustained and transient regressors, a principle established by Petersen and Dubis (2012) [20]. The sICA method identifies noise components by their spatial patterns—six noise templates and three component-intensity measures—rather than temporal characteristics, preserving transient neuronal signals [1]. The pipeline has been validated against PET [10] [11] and demonstrated in the team's adult continuous-speech study (Garnett et al., 2022) with near-identical acquisition parameters: TR=2s, 3.4×3.4×4mm voxels [11]. The explicit separation of "transient stuttering effects from the fluent speech activity on which they occur" [1] is a stated strength.

However, the event-related Aim 1b faces the feasibility constraint already encountered by the team: their 2023 childhood study could not analyze stuttering events because participants produced fewer than 10 in-scanner instances [2], using better parameters (TR=1.2s, 32-channel coil, 2.5×2.5×3mm voxels [2]) than those proposed here (TR=2s, 8-channel coil, 3.45×3.45×4mm voxels [1]). The proposal's fallback—excluding children below the 10-event threshold from Aim 1b only [1]—is prudent but means one of the study's two most distinctive contributions may not be realized with adequate precision.

**The proposal's scientific attitude toward uncertain outcomes strengthens the design.** The acknowledgment that childhood activity could differ from adult predictions and that "the functional data can revise the hypotheses rather than be treated as confirmation" [1] is an appropriate posture for an exploratory R21 and mitigates the risk that disconfirmation is interpreted as failure.

**Rating: C (confidence: B)**

## Feasibility

**The study is substantially feasible given the team's demonstrated expertise, well-characterized longitudinal cohort, and validated sICA denoising method, though one central element—the event-related analysis separating stuttering instances from fluent speech (Aim 1b)—faces a material feasibility risk that the proposal acknowledges but cannot fully resolve.**

### Cohort and recruitment

The proposal draws on an existing longitudinal cohort with annual assessments, from which 60 children (20 per group) aged 5–11 will be drawn [1]. Recovery and persistence classifications are determined from serial SSI-4 assessments, clinician and parent reports, and will be confirmed with new assessments at the time of scanning [1]. This cohort is a substantial asset: the team has already published structural and resting-state fMRI findings from the same participant pool, demonstrating the feasibility of recruiting, classifying, and scanning these children [5] [7]. The inclusion criterion that pCWS must show greater than 4.4% stuttering during quiet speech assessment, combined with the expectation of approximately 300 syllables from the storytelling task and a conservative 25% scanner-related reduction in stuttering frequency, is designed to yield more than ten in-scanner stuttering instances for Aim 1b [1]. A child falling below this threshold is excluded from the event analysis only, preserving the between-group fluent-speech comparisons [1].

### Imaging infrastructure and task

The scanning and speech-recording infrastructure is established. The proposed GE MR750 3T scanner, optical noise-cancellation microphone, MR-compatible camera, and E-Prime synchronization [1] mirror or match the equipment the team used in their published adult continuous-speech study (Garnett et al., 2022), which employed near-identical acquisition parameters—TR=2s, 3.4×3.4×4mm voxels—and successfully detected condition-related activity [11]. The storytelling paradigm using the textless picture book *Frog, Where Are You?* has been used successfully in the team's childhood cohort, and an 8-year-old child has already completed the paradigm showing perisylvian activation for storytelling relative to overlearned speech [1]. Children will be familiarized with the procedure before scanning, and non-compliant children can be rescheduled with extra training [1]. The existing participants' prior experience with scans and storytelling further supports compliance [1].

### The event-related analysis: a critical feasibility risk

The principal feasibility concern is Aim 1b's event-related analysis. The team's own published childhood study (Chow et al., 2023) explicitly states that "as the majority of the participants produced relatively few stuttering moments (\<10) in the scanner, the statistical power for analyzing stuttering-related activity may not be sufficient" and that stuttering events were consequently "treated as an effect of no interest and are not reported" [2]. This was achieved using better acquisition parameters—TR=1.2s, 32-channel head coil, 2.5×2.5×3mm voxels, multiband factor 3 [2]—than those proposed here (TR=2s, 8-channel coil, 3.45×3.45×4mm voxels, acceleration factor 2 [1]).

The proposal's estimate of more than ten in-scanner stuttering instances sits at the threshold: 300 syllables at 4.4% stuttering yields approximately 13 stutters outside the scanner, and a 25% scanner-related reduction brings this to roughly 10 [1]. Children just above the screening threshold who experience somewhat greater scanner-related reduction, or who produce fewer syllables than anticipated, would fall below the threshold and be excluded. Moreover, the wide age range (5–11) compounds this: younger children produce fewer and shorter utterances per picture than older children, and this age-related variation in speech output may not be evenly distributed across groups. The proposal's contingency of excluding such children from Aim 1b only is sensible, but it means the effective sample for the event-related analysis could be materially smaller than the 20 planned pCWS. The between-group fluent-speech comparisons—the study's most secure contribution—do not depend on this aim succeeding.

### Movement-related attrition

Movement-related attrition is a further practical consideration. The Chow et al. (2023) study excluded 3 CWS and 4 controls (approximately 15% of scanned children) for excessive movement [2], and an additional 12 children who did not complete MRI scanning or withdrew [2]. The proposal mitigates this risk through sICA denoising, which the team has shown keeps DVARS below approximately 1% for movements up to 9 mm [1], and by scrubbing volumes with movements beyond the denoising method's working range [1]. These measures are appropriate and grounded in the team's published validation work [10] [11], but they cannot eliminate attrition entirely. The storytelling-versus-overlearned-speech paradigm and the mixed block/event GLM are otherwise technically sound within the stated parameters and well within the team's demonstrated capabilities.

**Rating: B (confidence: B)**

## Ethics and safety

**The ethics and safety plan is comprehensive, proportionate, and well-grounded in the team's established practices for pediatric neuroimaging research. No material ethical deficiencies or unmanaged risks are identified.**

The proposal specifies a thorough consent and assent procedure: parents or guardians will provide informed consent, and children will provide verbal or written assent [1]. This two-tier approach follows standard practice for research involving minors and appropriately respects the developing autonomy of children aged 5–11 while ensuring legal authorization from guardians.

The management of participant discomfort and distress during scanning is addressed through multiple, mutually reinforcing measures. MRI safety screening will be conducted, hearing protection (headphones or earplugs) will be provided, and the scanning session includes breaks and familiarization with the procedure before scanning [1]. Children who cannot complete the task can be rescheduled with extra training [1], and participants retain the option to stop without penalty at any point [1]. Parents are permitted to remain nearby or with a distressed child [1]. These arrangements are consistent with the team's published pediatric protocols: Chow et al. (2023), which scanned children aged 5–12 using a similar speech-production paradigm, reported that a mock MRI familiarization visit was included and that children were excluded if they did not want to participate or could not complete the scan [2], indicating that voluntary withdrawal was operationalized in practice.

The storytelling task itself is child-appropriate: a textless picture book (*Frog, Where Are You?*) previously used successfully in the team's childhood cohort [1], with each picture displayed for 15 seconds followed by rest, keeping the fMRI task under 15 minutes [1]. The overlearned control condition (alphabet recitation) is undemanding and unlikely to cause frustration.

The risks to participants are those inherent in pediatric MRI research without contrast agents or sedation: mild discomfort from scanner noise and confinement, possible anxiety, and fatigue. The mitigation measures described—familiarization, breaks, hearing protection, parental presence, and the right to withdraw—are standard and sufficient. The scanner noise itself is a specific consideration for this protocol because it may reduce stuttering frequency [1], but this affects data yield rather than participant safety. No invasive procedures, pharmacological interventions, or significant physical risks are involved.

Data protection arrangements are appropriately specified. Audio/video recordings and imaging data will use coded identifiers, with data stored in locked or password-protected systems and collaborator access controlled [1]. De-identified datasets will be shared after analysis and publication under formal data-use agreements, with release anticipated within one year after study closeout [1]. This plan addresses confidentiality and data security at a level suitable for a single-site neuroimaging study collecting speech recordings alongside brain images. The use of coded identifiers rather than fully anonymized data during analysis is a practical necessity for linking behavioral transcripts with imaging data, and the controlled-access model for subsequent sharing is consistent with the sensitive nature of video and audio recordings that could, in principle, be re-identified.

One consideration worth noting is that the proposal does not describe a formal data monitoring committee or an explicit adverse-event reporting pathway. Given the minimal-risk nature of the procedures—a non-invasive fMRI scan with a speech task in healthy children—this absence does not constitute a deficiency. The institutional review board approval that the proposal implies would include standard mechanisms for reporting and addressing any unanticipated problems.

There are no risks to communities, animals, or the environment in this study design. The research does not involve vulnerable populations beyond the childhood age range already addressed by the consent and assent procedures, and the inclusion criterion restricting participation to children without diagnosed developmental or psychiatric disorders other than stuttering [1] is scientifically motivated rather than ethically problematic.

**Rating: A (confidence: B)**

## Management

**The management arrangements are well-conceived and draw on a team with complementary expertise, established infrastructure, and a longitudinal cohort that substantially de-risks recruitment; the main weakness is the absence of detailed coordination and contingency governance, though the team's track record supports confidence that the work is professionally manageable.**

### Team expertise

The study integrates three distinct areas of expertise essential to the project: Chow's fMRI acquisition and analysis methods, including the sICA denoising approach; Chang's longitudinal childhood stuttering cohort and neuroimaging research program; and Ratner's speech-language pathology and transcription expertise [1]. This combination directly addresses the critical dependencies of the proposal—fMRI data quality during overt speech, access to a well-characterized participant pool with confirmed persistence and recovery status, and reliable speech coding for both stuttering-event identification and linguistic transcript measures. Speech coding will involve two independent speech-language pathologists for reliable diagnosis and event identification, with transcript measures including speech rate, utterance length, semantic diversity (TTR and VocD), and syntactic complexity (DSS and IPSYN) [1]. The PI and team have published together on childhood stuttering neuroimaging using the same cohort and comparable methods [2] [5], demonstrating that the collaborative infrastructure is operational rather than aspirational.

### Schedule and equipment

The 36-month schedule is appropriately scaled to the R21 scope and the known throughput of the longitudinal cohort. Recruitment and imaging occupy months 1–18, with preparation of analyses proceeding in parallel, followed by speech and fMRI analysis in months 12–24 and manuscript preparation and dissemination in months 24–36 [1]. This sequencing allows overlapping activity while providing a realistic buffer for the known attrition pathways that the team has documented in prior work [2]. The schedule does not, however, specify milestones, decision points, or criteria for determining whether the event-related Aim 1b has sufficient data to proceed as planned versus defaulting to the fluent-speech-only analyses. Given that the team's own prior childhood study could not analyze stuttering events due to insufficient in-scanner instances [2], a pre-specified rule for evaluating Aim 1b feasibility mid-study (e.g., after the first 10 pCWS scans) would strengthen governance without altering the schedule.

The required equipment—a GE MR750 3T scanner with an eight-channel head coil, an optical noise-cancellation microphone, an MR-compatible camera, and E-Prime stimulus presentation software [1]—is stated as available. The team has used this scanner model in their published adult continuous-speech study with near-identical acquisition parameters [11], and the Chow et al. (2023) childhood study used the same scanner with a 32-channel coil [2]. The proposal does not explain the downgrade from the 32-channel coil and multiband acquisition used in the 2023 pediatric study to the 8-channel coil and standard EPI proposed here, which affects temporal and spatial resolution; a brief justification would clarify whether this reflects equipment scheduling constraints, a deliberate signal-to-noise trade-off, or other practical considerations. This does not undermine the feasibility of data collection—the 8-channel configuration is adequate for the stated aims—but it represents a coordination detail that the management documentation leaves unresolved.

### Data management and future directions

Data management and analysis workflows are described in functional terms: speech transcription in CLAN/CHAT with independent SLP coding [1], sICA denoising and GLM analysis in SPM12 with blinded analysts [1]. The proposal does not specify how data will be organized, versioned, or shared among the three collaborating groups during active analysis. The data sharing plan addresses post-study release under formal data-use agreements [1], but the day-to-day coordination of imaging data, speech transcripts, and behavioral covariates across sites is not detailed. Given the team's established publication record together, this omission likely reflects existing informal workflows rather than a genuine coordination gap, but explicit attention to data management would strengthen the plan.

The future directions—functional connectivity during continuous speech, time-resolved analyses of transitions into stuttering, and longitudinal tracking from onset through persistence or recovery [1]—are appropriately identified as beyond the current scope, which helps define the project's boundaries and prevents scope expansion during execution.

**Rating: B (confidence: B)**

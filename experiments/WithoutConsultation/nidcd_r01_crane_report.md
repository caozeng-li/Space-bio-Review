# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project aims to determine how people combine visual and inertial information to perceive heading direction, whether the two signals are judged to arise from a common cause, and how heading perception adapts when visual and inertial cues remain systematically inconsistent. The work spans healthy participants and individuals with unilateral vestibular loss, using a motion platform and synchronised visual displays to map the discrepancy range over which explicit unity judgments and implicit cross-modal influence operate. The most valuable expected knowledge is the parametric characterisation of the dissociation between these two processes, the first systematic evidence on whether visual-inertial adaptation can durably correct heading errors after vestibular damage, and a longitudinal description of how heading, rotation and sway biases co-evolve following acute unilateral loss.

The plan is grounded in a decade of laboratory experience with the required motion-platform psychophysics; healthy-participant studies are well-designed to test the central prediction. Published preliminary data already demonstrate the core dissociation, and the proposed parametric manipulations will consolidate and extend that finding. Patient recruitment represents the decisive limitation: the laboratory has published almost exclusively on healthy samples, and the projected accrual of acutely vertiginous patients for an intensive longitudinal protocol, and of chronic patients with sufficiently large baseline heading deviations, exceeds what the available evidence suggests is realistic at a single centre. Across-visit measurement reliability for heading bias (minimum detectable change approximately 9°) further constrains the individual-level inferences that Aim III depends on, while the adaptation experiments would be strengthened by explicit de-adaptation and control conditions to distinguish perceptual recalibration from short-term aftereffects.

The project pursues worthwhile questions with well-established psychophysical methods in a laboratory that demonstrably can execute the mechanistic experiments in healthy participants. The incremental scientific contribution is offset by genuine feasibility risks in the clinical aims, which the proposal's recruitment plan does not adequately address, and by measurement-reliability limits that constrain the mechanistic resolution of the longitudinal and adaptation comparisons. The adaptation studies could further benefit from design features—de-adaptation assessments, control conditions—that would sharpen the inference between perceptual recalibration and alternative accounts. With explicit contingency planning for patient accrual and appropriately bounded interpretive claims, a useful subset of the programme—the parametric causal-inference work and an initial adaptation characterisation—is supportable now, whereas the clinical cohort ambitions require a firmer recruitment basis.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

Understanding how humans combine visual and inertial information to perceive heading direction addresses a genuine gap between the reflexive measures that dominate clinical vestibular assessment—the vestibulo-ocular reflex and vestibular-evoked myopotentials—and the perceptual symptoms of dizziness, vertigo, and spatial disorientation that patients actually report [1]. The project will deliver three concrete contributions, each at a different stage of maturity.

First, it will provide normative psychophysical characterisation of the dissociation between explicit common-cause judgments and implicit cross-modal influence across the full horizontal plane, extending the single-condition finding already published by the laboratory [2] through systematic manipulation of temporal offset, motion duration, acceleration, and visual-field size [1]. This parametric work responds directly to Acerbi et al.'s (2018) recognition that the binding tendency in causal-inference models "lacks an independent empirical correlate" and that understanding "which experimental manipulations it is sensitive to is venue for future work" [3]. The inclusion of temporal-offset conditions is supported by the laboratory's own subsequent demonstration that visual influence on inertial heading remains robust within ±250 ms but diminishes sharply at ±500 ms [4], and the proposed 50-ms-step mapping will characterise the time window of cross-modal influence with finer resolution.

Second, the project will produce the first systematic evidence on whether two candidate perceptual-adaptation methods—systematically offset visual-inertial translations and rotating virtual environments—can durably correct heading errors in individuals with chronic unilateral vestibular hypofunction [1]. The relationship between causal inference and recalibration is an active research frontier, synthesised in a recent review proposing that the two processes form a coupled adaptive system operating across distinct timescales [5]. The proposal's within-subject design measuring both heading adaptation and changes in common-cause judgments offers a direct test of this coupling.

Third, it will generate a longitudinal description of how heading, yaw-rotation and sway-translation biases co-evolve during the first year following acute unilateral vestibular loss, a dataset that does not currently exist at this temporal resolution [1]. The underlying mechanistic question—whether persistent heading errors reflect otolith asymmetry or compensation for prolonged rotatory vertigo—has empirical precedent: Borel et al. (2021) demonstrated that after complete unilateral vestibular neurotomy, the rotation component of subjective straight ahead resolved to near zero by day 60 while a rightward translation bias persisted [6]. The proposal's weekly-then-monthly measurement schedule captures the critical time window when this dissociation should emerge.

The project is explicitly positioned as mechanistic groundwork for subsequent rehabilitation trials and does not claim immediate therapeutic benefit [1]. This scoping is appropriate: the adaptation experiments in Aim IIIB address durability and tolerability of heading correction, while the proposal acknowledges that functional benefit would require a randomised clinical trial [1]. Similarly, the longitudinal study aims to distinguish otolith-based from rotation-driven heading errors, but the inference will be observational and correlational rather than definitively causal [1]. The value of these clinical aims lies in generating the methods, normative data, and effect-size estimates needed to design definitive studies.

**Constraints on attainable knowledge.** The attainable knowledge is nonetheless limited by measurement and design considerations that affect the granularity of the mechanistic insights. Across-visit test-retest reliability for inertial heading point of subjective equality is modest (intraclass correlation coefficient 0.59), with a minimum detectable change of approximately 9–10°, as documented in a recent psychometric evaluation co-authored by the principal investigator [7]. This means that individual trajectory analyses in the longitudinal study will be able to resolve only relatively large shifts in bias over time, and that the dissociation between otolith and rotational contributions—which the proposal seeks to infer from correlations among heading, rotation, and sway measures—will remain inferential rather than conclusive for individual patients. An independent study further demonstrates that rotational signals exert a disproportionately strong influence on heading perception, more than double what would be expected from simple linear combination [8], meaning that heading errors cannot be simply parsed into independent otolith and canal components.

The adaptation experiments will demonstrate whether heading perception can be shifted and whether the shift generalises, but without de-adaptation measurements or no-adaptation control conditions, the distinction between genuine perceptual recalibration, short-term aftereffects, and non-perceptual biases will remain partially ambiguous. The Crane laboratory has itself demonstrated that translation and visual motion both produce aftereffects with limited cross-modal interaction [9], so the need for temporal decay data to separate recalibration from aftereffects is both well-established and directly relevant.

The value of the resulting evidence is therefore strongest for the parametric causal-inference work in healthy participants, where the laboratory's established paradigm supports well-powered hypothesis testing, and more exploratory for the clinical aims, where the observations will be pattern-generating rather than confirmatory. The work would provide methods and normative data valuable for subsequent studies, including the rehabilitation trials the proposal appropriately defers to a future randomised clinical trial.

## Innovation

The proposal's central distinction—that explicit common-cause judgments and implicit cross-modal influence operate over different discrepancy ranges during vestibular-visual heading perception—was already demonstrated by the laboratory's own published work. Rodriguez and Crane (2020) used the identical 12×12 full-circle factorial design in eight healthy participants and found that common-cause reports fell below 15% at offsets of 90° or more, while inertial heading estimates were biased 11–20° toward the visual heading across offsets of 30–120°, including at separations where participants explicitly denied common causation [2]. They concluded that "subjects tend to bias perception of inertial heading toward the visual stimulus direction even when common causation is not perceived" [2]. Experiment IA is therefore largely a replication of that published finding with an increased sample size (n ≈ 20 rather than n = 8 [1]), justified by the need for reproducible measurements and adequate power for individual-differences analysis, but not conceptually novel in itself.

**Parametric extension beyond the single-condition finding.** The innovation resides in three extensions that together constitute a meaningful but incremental advance. Experiment IB will systematically manipulate temporal offset (50–200 ms steps), motion duration, acceleration, and visual-field size (98°, 45°, 20°) [1], going beyond the single-condition finding of Rodriguez and Crane (2020) to determine whether the dissociation is a fixed property of the perceptual system or varies parametrically with stimulus conditions that affect the evidence for common causation. The three levels of visual-field size alone will test whether a broader field of view (which provides stronger evidence for self-motion rather than object motion) narrows or widens the discrepancy range over which cross-modal influence persists relative to explicit unity judgments. The inclusion of temporal-offset conditions is supported by the laboratory's own subsequent demonstration that visual influence on inertial heading remains robust within ±250 ms but is much diminished at ±500 ms [4]; the proposed 50-ms steps will map the time window with finer resolution than the 250-ms bins used in that prior work.

**Clinical translation and connection to adaptation.** Experiment IC will apply the same causal-inference paradigms to patients with chronic unilateral vestibular lesions, where reduced vestibular reliability is predicted to increase visual influence and broaden common-cause judgments [1]. No prior study has examined how the dissociation between unity judgments and cross-modal influence changes with unilateral vestibular hypofunction. The comparison will provide descriptive evidence on whether and how the dissociation is modulated by pathology, informing both mechanistic models of causal inference and the design of perceptual interventions. Aim II links causal inference to perceptual adaptation by determining whether exposure to systematically offset visual-inertial headings or rotating virtual environments alters common-cause judgments alongside heading estimates [1]. The relationship between causal inference and recalibration is an active research frontier [5], and the within-subject design measuring both heading adaptation and changes in common-cause judgments offers a direct, if descriptive, test of this coupling.

**Absence of computational modelling.** These contributions are tempered by the absence of a formal computational model. Acerbi et al. (2018) demonstrated that Bayesian model comparison of causal-inference strategies can extract substantially more mechanistic information from tasks of this kind than descriptive statistics alone: implicit-task data alone could not distinguish causal inference from forced fusion (fixed-criterion model posterior probability 0.79 versus forced fusion below 0.10 only when both tasks were modelled jointly) [3]. The proposal mentions integrating results into "a unified model" in Year 5 [1] but does not specify the computational architecture, leaving the distinction between explicit and implicit processes at a descriptive rather than mechanistic level. This omission limits the depth of the prospective advance, though the rich parametric data the design will produce would in principle support such modelling.

The proposal's contribution—establishing the explicit-implicit distinction across the full horizontal plane with independent modality estimates and parametric stimulus variation—represents a meaningful but incremental advance beyond prior work. The dissociation itself was demonstrated in Rodriguez and Crane (2020). The advance lies in its systematic parametric characterisation, extension to clinical populations, and connection to perceptual adaptation. These are worthwhile scientific goals that would consolidate and extend an interesting finding into a more comprehensive picture of how causal inference and cross-modal influence relate in vestibular-visual heading perception.

## Rigor

The project's inferential strength is concentrated in the causal-inference experiments in healthy participants, where the design is well-matched to the central prediction; the clinical aims and adaptation studies face genuine limitations from measurement reliability, the absence of control conditions, and order confounds that constrain the mechanistic conclusions they can support.

**Core psychophysical design.** The core psychophysical design is sound. Experiments IA–IC use the laboratory's established 12×12 full-circle factorial paradigm, pairing 12 visual and 12 inertial headings (30° increments) to yield 144 combinations per block [1]. Obtaining unity judgments and heading estimates in separate trial blocks was empirically motivated—preliminary work showed that "reporting both in the same block proved confusing" [1]—and this arrangement directly tests the central prediction that cross-modal influence spans a larger discrepancy range than explicit common-cause perception [1]. Because forced-choice heading estimates are fitted with cumulative Gaussian functions, bias (point of subjective equality) and precision (threshold) are estimated independently [1], avoiding the criterion confound that can affect binary judgments alone. These methods have been validated in multiple publications from the laboratory [2] and in the broader field [10].

Three features of the design address specific alternative explanations. First, the joint collection of unity judgments and heading estimates implements the key methodological lesson from Acerbi et al. (2018): implicit-task data alone cannot distinguish forced fusion from causal inference, but joint modelling of both tasks can [3]. Second, response-bias accounts are constrained by the pilot data showing unity judgments below 4% at offsets of at least 90° [1]—if participants were simply reluctant to report "different," unity judgments would remain high at all discrepancies. Third, the parametric manipulations in Experiment IB—timing, duration, visual-field size [1]—provide converging tests: if cross-modal influence reflects genuine multisensory integration processes, it should vary systematically with stimulus parameters that alter cue reliability; if it reflects a fixed response strategy, such variation should be minimal.

Experiment IA's sample of approximately 20 participants is adequately powered for the primary group-level comparison of unity-judgment and bias functions under the stated assumptions (10° bias, 15° SD, 80% power, α = 0.05) [1]. For individual-differences analysis, 20 participants permits characterising the distribution of strategies but is marginal for correlating individual parameters with external measures such as age or vestibular function. The sample sizes for Experiments IB (n ≈ 10) and IC (n ≈ 10) are consistent with precedents in the published literature—Acerbi et al. (2018) used n = 11 [3], de Winkel et al. (2017) used n = 8 [11]—but are the lower bound of what these prior studies found adequate for detecting condition effects, and Experiment IB in particular varies multiple parameters (timing offsets at several steps, three visual-field sizes, duration, acceleration) that risk being underpowered for interaction effects with only ten participants [1].

**Adaptation experiments: the recalibration-aftereffect distinction.** For the adaptation experiments (IIA, IIB), the baseline/post-adaptation design mirrors the established paradigm of Zaidel, Turner, and Angelaki (2011), in which single-cue psychometric functions were measured before and after exposure to systematically offset combined-cue stimuli, with post-adaptation blocks interleaving single-cue test trials with combined-cue maintenance trials [10]. Adaptation was quantified as the shift in the point of subjective equality of the cumulative Gaussian fit [10]. The proposal adopts essentially the same logic: forced-choice testing near straight ahead before and after the 80-presentation adaptation sequence [1].

Three features weaken the inference. First, the proposal does not include a no-adaptation control condition that would rule out fatigue, practice, or criterion drift over the 20–25 minute adaptation period. The shared methods note that "fatigue and session effects are minimal" for vestibular precision testing [12], but this observation comes from threshold measurement contexts rather than extended adaptation paradigms with sustained attentional demand. Second, the proposal does not specify de-adaptation or retention measurements in healthy participants that would distinguish durable perceptual recalibration from rapidly decaying aftereffects. The Crane laboratory has itself demonstrated that translation and visual motion both produce aftereffects—perceptual biases in the opposite direction following adaptation—with limited cross-modal interaction [9]. Without temporal decay data, an observed PSE shift cannot be confidently attributed to the kind of sustained recalibration that would be clinically relevant. Third, while the inclusion of continuous-estimate methods (mechanical dial) alongside forced-choice testing provides a valuable convergent measure—continuous estimates are less susceptible to criterion shifts—the proposal commits to using them only "in selected experiments" for generalisation mapping [1] rather than as a systematic cross-validation of the forced-choice adaptation measure in every participant.

**Longitudinal study and measurement constraints.** The longitudinal study (IIIA) is constrained by measurement properties documented by Geno et al. (2024), co-authored by the principal investigator. The minimum detectable change for inertial heading PSE across visits is 9.26–9.62°, with an across-visit intraclass correlation coefficient of only 0.59 [7]. The proposal's expected effect size of 7.5° [1] therefore falls below the threshold at which individual change can be confidently distinguished from measurement noise. Within-visit reliability is better (ICC = 0.80, MDC ≈ 6.2°) [7], but the longitudinal design's critical comparisons are necessarily across visits separated by weeks to months, where the poorer across-visit reliability applies. The addition of yaw-rotation thresholds and sway-translation measures alongside heading estimates is a strength because it provides complementary indices of canal and otolith function [1], but the inference that heading errors of otolith origin correlate with sway asymmetry while rotation-driven errors follow canal compensation [1] will be fundamentally correlational and limited by the precision of each component measure. The integrated nature of canal-otolith processing—rotational signals exert more than double the expected influence on heading perception [8]—further means that heading errors cannot be simply parsed into independent otolith and canal components.

**Chronic-lesion adaptation comparison.** For the chronic-lesion adaptation comparison (IIIB), enrolment-wave counterbalancing—"early enrollees receive the rotation method first and later enrollees the heading-offset method first" [1]—creates a confound between method order and recruitment epoch. If referral patterns or patient characteristics shift over the five-year study, these become inseparable from genuine method differences. Individual randomisation with an explicit washout period would be a stronger design, though the within-subject comparison at least ensures that each participant serves as their own control for baseline bias. The sample-size calculation (n = 8 required for an 8° change from 10° to 2°, SD = 8°) is appropriate for detecting a large within-subject effect [1], but the MDC of approximately 9° [7] means only changes exceeding that magnitude can be resolved for individual participants.

Experiment IC (n ≈ 10) and the etiology-specific analyses in IIIA and IIIB are explicitly exploratory—the proposal acknowledges clinical variability and slower recruitment [1]—and the small subgroup sizes will yield descriptive patterns rather than hypothesis tests. This is appropriate for an initial clinical comparison but should not be represented as a test of etiology-specific predictions.

## Feasibility

The mechanistic experiments in healthy participants and the adaptation methods are well-supported by established laboratory infrastructure and demonstrated expertise, but the clinical patient recruitment plan represents a genuine feasibility risk that the proposal's evidence and contingency provisions do not adequately address.

**Demonstrated capacity for mechanistic work.** The laboratory's capacity to execute the core psychophysical work is not in doubt. The principal investigator has over a decade of published experience with the required motion-platform methods—forced-choice and continuous-estimate heading psychophysics, visual-inertial stimulus synchronisation, and the 12×12 full-circle factorial paradigm—across journals including *Journal of Neurophysiology*, *Experimental Brain Research*, and *PLoS ONE* [1] [2]. The six-degree-of-freedom hexapod platform with 98-degree visual display, supplemented by a custom multi-axis sled/rotator offering unlimited yaw range and a 110-degree virtual-reality headset with binocular disparity, provides the apparatus flexibility needed for the parametric manipulations in Experiments IA and IB [1]. A full-time technician supports recruitment and testing, and the investigator maintains clinical access through an academic otolaryngology department [1]. The five-year schedule sequences method development before patient experiments and distributes the mechanistic and clinical work across years, with explicit contingencies such as denser sampling near each inertial heading if 30° increments miss narrow tuning [1], and adjustable adaptation session parameters if participants experience discomfort [1].

**The recruitment evidence gap.** The decisive feasibility concern is patient recruitment. The proposal anticipates approximately 30 participants with vestibulopathy over five years, divided between acute longitudinal follow-up (IIIA, target n ≈ 16 completers) and chronic adaptation studies (IIIB, target ≥ 20 with baseline heading deviations above 10°) [1]. The most significant gap in the evidence supporting these projections is that the laboratory's extensive published literature on heading perception reports almost exclusively on healthy participants, with typical sample sizes of 7–20 [13] [14] [4] [15] [16] [2] [17]. The proposal's preliminary data from five chronic unilateral vestibular hypofunction patients represent the entirety of the laboratory's reported experience with vestibular patients in heading-perception paradigms [1]. Every clinical research programme must begin somewhere, but the recruitment projections are extrapolations rather than demonstrated throughput.

**Acute lesion recruitment.** The acute longitudinal study (IIIA) requires enrolling and retaining acutely vertiginous patients through weekly visits in the first month, monthly for six months, then at nine months and one year [1]. The proposal estimates approximately 50% attrition, meaning 16 completers would require approximately 32 enrollees [1]. A recent multi-centre randomised trial of vestibular rehabilitation screened 390 individuals and enrolled 184 with acute vestibular syndrome across nine hospitals over 28 months—approximately 0.73 patients per hospital per month for a study that required only questionnaire completion and home exercises [18]. The proposal's protocol demands substantially more from patients during their most symptomatic period, when severe vertigo, nausea, and postural instability are common. Surgical cases (labyrinthectomy, tumour resection) provide the most predictable enrolment but occur at modest volumes—a high-volume referral centre reported 107 translabyrinthine schwannoma resections over five years [19], and only a subset would meet eligibility criteria and consent to participation. The proposal's own institutional data show that intratympanic gentamicin use for otologic indications represents a small fraction of total gentamicin administrations, with the large majority being neonates and obstetrics patients [20]. Across all three acute etiologies at a single centre, 6–7 eligible enrolments per year is an optimistic upper bound with little margin for additional exclusion or non-participation.

**Chronic cohort screening burden.** The chronic cohort (IIIB) faces a different recruitment challenge. The proposal seeks participants with baseline heading deviations above 10° [1], but the preliminary data found a mean of 7.5 ± 3.4° in five chronic patients [1], and an independent study of 26 unilateral vestibular hypofunction patients reported mean shifts of only 3.33–4.90° [8]. If the population distribution has a mean of approximately 5–8°, screening to identify 20 participants exceeding 10° would realistically require evaluating 40–60 chronic patients, depending on the actual distribution and the proportion willing to commit to multi-session adaptation protocols with weekly retention visits.

The proposal lacks explicit contingency provisions for what happens if recruitment falls materially short: whether the longitudinal analysis in IIIA could distinguish otolith-based from rotational-compensation accounts with 10 rather than 16 completers, or whether the chronic adaptation comparison remains informative with 12 rather than 20 participants. Without such planning, the clinical aims (IC, IIIA, and IIIB) carry a genuine risk of incomplete data that would limit their contribution beyond the already-published preliminary observations.

## Ethics and safety

The research is noninvasive and the principal foreseeable risks—motion sickness, temporary worsening of dizziness, and loss of privacy—are identified and proportionately managed; the acute-lesion protocol imposes substantial demands on symptomatic patients, but the planned symptom monitoring and stopping rules provide appropriate safeguards.

**Risk identification and mitigation.** The proposal explicitly acknowledges the primary risks. Motion sickness is a well-characterised consequence of passive translation on motion platforms and of exposure to rotating visual fields, and the proposal notes that "the principal foreseeable risks are motion sickness, worsening dizziness, and loss of privacy" [1]. Testing is noninvasive—participants sit in darkness or view visual displays while the hexapod delivers controlled translations and rotations—and no pharmacological, surgical, or electrical interventions are applied. This places the study in a risk category comparable to established vestibular psychophysics protocols that have been conducted safely in this laboratory and many others over decades [1] [2].

Several design features provide proportionate mitigation. Participants give informed consent and "may discontinue at any time"; adolescents, if enrolled (the protocol permits ages 13–89), require parental consent and an age-appropriate assent process [1]. Before testing begins, participants receive standardised explanations and large, unambiguous practice stimuli to establish comfort and comprehension [1]. The Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI) is administered during adaptation visits, providing a standardised self-report measure that can detect worsening symptoms [1]. Adverse events are documented with severity and relationship to participation and reviewed through institutional oversight [1]. The proposal states that "research participation will not prevent other clinical care" [1], an important safeguard for the patient cohorts in Aims IC and III, where participants have ongoing clinical needs unrelated to the research.

**Acute-lesion protocol burden.** The longitudinal acute-lesion protocol (IIIA) deserves particular ethical attention. Patients with recent vestibular neuritis, labyrinthectomy, or gentamicin treatment are likely to be symptomatic—vertigo, nausea, and postural instability are hallmarks of acute unilateral vestibular loss—and the protocol asks them to attend weekly visits in the first month, involving passive motion-platform testing that may itself provoke symptoms [1]. This is a material burden, not merely a feasibility concern: requiring acutely ill patients to travel and undergo provocative testing must be justified by the value of the knowledge gained and balanced by safeguards against harm. The proposal's approach—DHI monitoring, explicitly adjustable session parameters, and the unconditional right to discontinue—is appropriate. The longitudinal design's value lies in generating data on the natural history of perceptual recovery that cannot be obtained from cross-sectional comparisons, and the monitoring provisions ensure that worsening symptoms would be detected and responded to. However, if patients who are too ill to participate are systematically excluded, the resulting sample may represent a less severely affected subset, which would bias the natural-history description toward milder trajectories. This is a scientific limitation that arises from the ethically necessary exclusion of participants who cannot tolerate testing, and the proposal appropriately does not compel participation. Reporting the characteristics of excluded patients would strengthen the interpretation of the longitudinal data.

**Adaptation experiments and data management.** The adaptation experiments (IIA, IIB, IIIB) introduce additional exposure to sustained cross-modal discrepancy and rotating visual fields, both of which can provoke motion sickness. The preliminary data indicate that a rotating star field at 40° per second "became subjectively unobtrusive after one or two minutes" [1], and the proposal includes practical responses to discomfort—"reducing exposure duration or rotational speed is a proposed response" [1]—as well as minimum movement requirements that can be adjusted to standardise exposure. The DHI monitoring during adaptation sessions serves the dual purpose of tracking clinical status and detecting adverse responses [1].

Data management follows standard institutional practice: identifying information is maintained separately from research measurements, with access limited to designated study personnel [1]. Dissemination through scientific publications and presentations is planned, with trial registration before data collection [1]. These arrangements are appropriate for a single-centre psychophysical study of this nature. No environmental or community risks are identified, and the apparatus does not present unusual hazards beyond those of standard laboratory equipment.

## Management

The proposal's management arrangements are appropriately scaled for a single-centre psychophysics programme: the investigator–technician team, dedicated apparatus, clinical access, and five-year schedule are well-matched to the mechanistic experiments in healthy participants. The main management uncertainty is patient recruitment, which is a feasibility rather than a coordination or resource issue, and the schedule sensibly sequences method development before clinical work.

**Staffing and equipment.** The principal investigator will oversee all experiments, with a full-time technician supporting participant recruitment and testing [1]. This is a lean but adequate staffing model for the proposed volume of healthy-participant data collection. The laboratory's decade-long track record of publishing heading-perception studies with similar methods and comparable samples [1] [2] demonstrates that the investigator–technician arrangement can deliver the planned experiments. Clinical access through an academic otolaryngology department provides the referral pathways for the patient cohorts, and the investigator's dual role as clinician and researcher is appropriate for the clinical aims.

The equipment resources are a strength. A six-degree-of-freedom hexapod motion platform with synchronised 98-degree visual display is available for the core experiments, supplemented by a second platform that facilitates clinical access and parallel configurations [1]. A custom multi-axis sled/rotator provides greater translation range and unlimited yaw rotation for conditions that exceed the hexapod's limits, and a 110-degree virtual-reality headset with head tracking and binocular disparity extends the visual-field options. Motion tracking, video eye tracking, accelerometers, and rate gyros characterise both stimulus delivery and participant movement. No critical equipment gap is identified, and the proposal's contingency plan—testing wider visual fields up to 200° "contingent on equipment availability and results" [1]—appropriately makes that extension conditional.

**Schedule and dependencies.** The five-year schedule distributes the work in a logical sequence [1]:

| Year | Planned work |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Causal-inference method development (IA); begin offset adaptation (IIA), acute follow-up (IIIA), and chronic adaptation (IIIB) |
| 2 | Extend causal inference to timing (IB) and lesions (IC); begin rotational adaptation (IIB); analyse and publish initial findings |
| 3 | Stimulus-parameter effects on causal inference and offset adaptation; continue lesion studies |
| 4 | Duration and visual effects on causal inference; rotational-adaptation parameters; complete and analyse chronic-lesion work |
| 5 | Integrate into a unified model; complete analysis and publication; follow emergent findings |

This sequencing is sensible. Year 1 establishes the basic paradigm in healthy participants before clinical recruitment begins in earnest, and the parametric extensions in Years 2–4 (temporal offset, field size, duration, acceleration) build on the reference condition established in IA. Running the acute longitudinal follow-up and chronic adaptation studies from Year 1 maximises the recruitment window—an important consideration given the enrolment challenges discussed under Feasibility. The explicit provision in Year 5 to "follow findings that motivate further experiments" [1] allows the programme to adapt to unexpected results, which is appropriate for a project spanning both basic and clinical aims. The proposal does not identify critical-path dependencies that would stall other aims if one component is delayed; the healthy-participant experiments and patient recruitment can proceed in parallel once the methods are established.

The proposal does not provide a detailed budget, itemised personnel effort, or individual CVs, but these are standard components of a full administrative application rather than the scientific narrative under review. The absence of these materials—and of formal data-management or dissemination milestones beyond scientific publication [1]—does not in itself indicate a management deficiency at the level of detail available. Given the investigator's established track record and institutional setting, the coordination plan as described is credible for the mechanistic core of the programme, with patient accrual representing the principal management variable that will determine whether the clinical aims can be completed as planned.

# Project review

## Overall assessment

This R21 project from Howard Petrie’s laboratory addresses the underexplored lymphoid‑to‑stromal direction of thymic crosstalk—the signals by which developing T lymphocytes instruct the growth, differentiation, and survival of the thymic stromal microenvironment. The planned work combines an inducible mouse model of thymic growth (IL7R‑deficient recipients reconstituted with wild‑type progenitors without myeloablative conditioning) with a previously published computational method that estimates region‑specific stromal gene expression by subtracting matched lymphoid profiles from microdissected cortical and medullary tissue. The pipeline will generate a five‑stage time course of stromal expression changes during the full growth trajectory, systematically identify stromal receptor–lymphoid ligand pairs using the Human Plasma Membrane Receptome and literature‑based co‑citation mining, prioritize candidates through a multi‑field evidence framework incorporating expression dynamics, interaction evidence, and reagent availability, and provide initial biological validation of one or more high‑priority candidates in existing mouse models. The most valuable expected knowledge is a dynamic, searchable stromal expression resource combined with a prioritized set of testable candidate interactions—a systematic foundation for subsequent mechanistic dissection of the lymphoid‑to‑stromal signaling axis.

The proposal is supported by a published and validated stromal expression mapping method that previously recovered known signals including Notch ligands, IL7, kit ligand, and Cxcl12; an established non‑myeloablative transplantation model that avoids stromal damage from irradiation conditioning; and a principled multi‑field prioritization framework that preserves individual evidence streams rather than collapsing into a single opaque score. The principal limitations are: (i) the IL7R‑deficient thymus at baseline is not merely hypocellular but structurally and compositionally altered, with an inverted cTEC‑to‑mTEC ratio and disorganized medullary architecture, which qualifies the generalizability of signals identified from this starting state; (ii) the subtraction method produces a composite stromal signal that cannot resolve contributions from distinct stromal subtypes (cTEC, mTEC, fibroblasts, endothelium); (iii) the receptor‑ligand identification strategy relies on the Human Plasma Membrane Receptome, which captures only a fraction of currently known interactions and systematically misses non‑peptide, ECM, and channel‑mediated signals; (iv) the schedule allocates 6–8 months to sequential kinetic studies on which the main expression experiment depends, with little contingency within the two‑year R21 ceiling; and (v) the sole postdoctoral fellow must span mouse transplantation, microdissection, flow cytometry, microarrays, and a substantial computational pipeline including literature mining and database construction. The proposal honestly acknowledges several of these limitations, including the dependence of Aim 4 on Aims 1–3 and the inability of thymic size and cellularity readouts to distinguish proliferation, survival, and differentiation.

The project’s central trade‑off is between scientific novelty and implementation risk. The discovery strategy is well‑conceived, and its products—a dynamic stromal expression atlas, a prioritized candidate interactome, and initial biological validation—would constitute a genuinely useful resource for the thymus biology community. These strengths are weighed against a schedule that appears to exceed the R21’s two‑year limit under a serial reading, a personnel plan that concentrates extraordinary breadth of skill on a single postdoctoral fellow, and a validation aim whose experimental content cannot be specified until the discovery phase concludes. The IL7R‑deficient model’s altered stromal baseline and the subtraction method’s inability to resolve stromal subtypes are acknowledged limitations that constrain the resource’s biological resolution but do not defeat its discovery purpose. The current plan is sufficient to generate the candidate resource and achieve proof‑of‑concept validation if the kinetic timeline can be compressed and the postdoctoral fellow possesses or rapidly acquires the requisite dual expertise. Without explicit contingency planning for the kinetic‑dependency bottleneck, however, there is a material risk that Aim 4—the biological validation that distinguishes this proposal from a purely computational resource—will be incomplete. **Overall recommendation: Conditional support.**

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

**Scientific value is rated good (B), with high confidence.** The project addresses an understudied and biologically significant question: the reciprocal signals by which developing lymphoid cells instruct the growth, differentiation, and organization of the thymic stroma. The stromal‑to‑lymphoid direction of thymic crosstalk—how the microenvironment supports T‑cell development—has been extensively characterized, but the reverse axis remains far less understood [1]. The proposal makes a well‑judged case that systematic identification of lymphoid‑to‑stromal signals would fill a recognized gap, with implications for understanding thymic involution, immune reconstitution after transplantation, and thymic regeneration [1]. The question is framed at an appropriate resolution for an exploratory R21, distinguishing post‑natal growth and maintenance from embryonic specification of the thymic primordium [1].

The attainable knowledge contribution is a set of three linked resources: a dynamic, time‑resolved stromal expression atlas across five stages of induced thymic growth; a searchable collection of candidate receptor‑ligand interactions between lymphoid cells and stromal cells, prioritized by multiple evidence fields; and initial biological validation of one or more high‑priority candidates [1]. These deliverables are prospectively valuable because the field currently lacks systematic, region‑matched expression resources that capture stromal dynamics during a physiological growth response. The computational subtraction method underpinning the atlas has been published and validated by Griffith et al. (2009), where it successfully recovered known stromal signals including Notch ligands, IL7, kit ligand, Cxcl12, and MHC‑associated genes [1]. This precedent demonstrates that the approach can identify biologically meaningful stromal expression, lending credibility to the resource’s likely utility.

The value proposition is appropriately calibrated for an R21 mechanism. The project aims to generate a hypothesis‑generating discovery resource and proof‑of‑concept validation, not a complete mechanistic account. The proposal explicitly acknowledges that detailed dissection of how individual signals control stromal proliferation, differentiation, or survival is a subsequent research direction [1], and that Aim 4 establishes that selected interactions matter for thymic growth in vivo without distinguishing among these mechanisms [1]. This is a legitimate and well‑defined contribution: a systematic nomination engine for lymphoid‑to‑stromal signals, backed by initial in vivo evidence that the discovery pipeline produces candidates with biological relevance.

The resource’s practical utility is enhanced by several design features. The planned database will preserve individual evidence scores—co‑citation frequency, thymus‑specific co‑citation, time‑course ANOVA q‑values, signal‑intensity decile, STRING evidence, and reagent availability—rather than collapsing them into a single ranking [1]. This allows subsequent users to apply different weighting schemes for different biological questions. The inclusion of reagent availability as a pragmatic criterion, combined with links to existing mouse mutants and embryonic stem‑cell resources [1], directly facilitates the transition from candidate nomination to experimental testing in other laboratories. A genuinely useful resource for the thymus biology community is therefore within the project’s reach.

## Innovation

**Innovation is rated good (B), with high confidence.** The project’s innovation lies not in a single new method or a previously unstudied molecule, but in the systematic, time‑resolved discovery strategy it brings to a long‑recognised yet still underexplored problem: the lymphoid‑to‑stromal direction of thymic crosstalk. The stromal‑to‑lymphoid axis has been extensively characterised, but the reverse signals remain far less systematically catalogued [1]. The proposal asks how developing thymocytes instruct stromal growth and organisation during a physiological growth response—a framing that shifts the emphasis from steady‑state description to dynamic, time‑resolved discovery [1]. This orientation is genuinely novel for the field, where most prior work has either examined individual candidate pathways in isolation or described stromal expression at a single time point.

The integrated pipeline itself constitutes the primary innovation. The proposal does not simply apply an existing method to a new biological context; it assembles several components into a discovery engine whose combined output exceeds what any single component could produce. The individual elements—the non‑myeloablative IL7R‑deficient reconstitution model, the computational stromal expression subtraction method, and the Human Plasma Membrane Receptome—are each published [1]. Their integration, however, creates a novel capability: the ability to generate a time‑resolved, region‑matched map of stromal receptor expression changes during induced growth and to systematically pair those receptors with lymphoid ligands expressed at the corresponding stage and anatomical compartment. The cortical‑versus‑medullary separation combined with the time‑course design adds a spatial and temporal resolution that most cell–cell communication resources lack. Ramilowski et al. (2015), in constructing their draft human ligand–receptor network, noted that they abstracted to the simple requirement that receptor and ligand are both expressed, without cell‑type or temporal specificity beyond the primary cell type panel available to them [2]. The present proposal improves on this by requiring co‑expression at matched anatomical locations and matched time points, a biologically grounded filter that strengthens the specificity of candidate pairing [1].

A further creative element is the thymus‑specific co‑citation metric in the prioritisation framework. The proposal plans to compute the ratio of receptor–ligand co‑citation frequency to the co‑citation of either member with thymus, thymic, or thymocyte terms [1]. This is designed to identify known interactions with potentially unrecognised thymic roles—interactions that are well‑documented in other tissues but have escaped attention in thymus biology. This metric explicitly operationalises the project’s aim of discovering new biology rather than simply recovering interactions already studied in the thymus. It is a conceptually elegant addition that distinguishes the prioritisation scheme from generic co‑citation‑based rankings.

The contribution is incremental rather than paradigmatic, but it is well‑justified and proportionate to an R21 mechanism. The proposal does not claim to discover a new signalling pathway class or to re‑conceptualise thymic stromal biology. It claims to provide a systematic nomination engine and a prioritised, searchable candidate resource that will accelerate subsequent mechanistic studies—a contribution whose value is measured by the experiments it enables rather than by a single novel finding. The exploratory premise that combining a physiological growth response with region‑matched expression measurements can reveal candidate communication axes difficult to nominate from established pathways [1] is reasonable and, importantly, testable within the proposed scope. The final proof‑of‑concept validation in Aim 4 will demonstrate that the pipeline can identify signals with genuine in‑vivo relevance, establishing non‑zero yield for the discovery strategy.

## Rigor

**Rigor is rated good (B), with high confidence.** The research design is appropriately matched to the exploratory aims of an R21 discovery project, and the proposal honestly acknowledges the principal inferential limitations. The computational stromal expression subtraction method has been published and validated by recovery of known stromal signals, providing a reasonable foundation. The multi‑criteria prioritisation framework is transparent and well‑dimensional. The key limitations—inability to resolve stromal subtypes, a baseline state that is structurally altered rather than merely hypocellular, and biological validation readouts that cannot distinguish proliferation, survival, and differentiation—are real but do not defeat the stated proof‑of‑concept goals.

### Stromal expression estimation: validated method with recognised limits

The computational subtraction approach compares microdissected cortical or medullary tissue with region‑matched purified lymphoid populations from the contralateral thymic lobe, attributing the residual signal to stromal cells [1]. Griffith et al. (2009) used this method to recover known stromal signals including Notch ligands, IL7, kit ligand, Cxcl12, and MHC‑associated genes [1], demonstrating that the approach can identify biologically meaningful stromal expression. The present proposal extends this steady‑state method to a five‑stage time course, using within‑timepoint matching—each tissue sample is paired with its own time‑matched lymphoid reference—to manage the compositional drift as wild‑type donor progenitors progressively replace residual endogenous lymphoid cells [1]. This is a logical strategy that avoids the hazards of comparing stromal estimates across time points with different lymphoid compositions.

The method carries two important limitations that the proposal acknowledges, and that constrain the inferences the resource can support. First, the subtraction produces a composite stromal signal that cannot distinguish contributions from cortical thymic epithelial cells (cTEC), medullary thymic epithelial cells (mTEC), fibroblasts, endothelium, or other stromal populations. St‑Pierre et al. (2013), using purified cTEC and mTEC populations for RNA‑seq, directly noted that Griffith’s method “did not allow to segregate genes expressed by TECs vs. other stromal cells” [3], and demonstrated that 25% of genes expressed in TECs are differentially expressed by at least five‑fold between cTEC and mTEC [3]. A receptor that goes up in cTEC and down in mTEC by equal amounts would show no net change in the mixed signal. The proposal’s plan to use histological methods for subsequent cell‑type identification of individual candidates [1] is a reasonable two‑stage strategy for an exploratory project, but it means the primary expression resource reports changes in a mixed population. Second, the n=3 pooled‑sample design (three independently prepared microarrays, each pooling three mice, per condition) [1] limits statistical power for the time‑course ANOVA proposed in Aim 3, particularly after multiple‑testing correction across approximately 45,000 probe sets. The proposal’s use of multiple prioritisation criteria rather than a single statistical threshold [1] is a pragmatic response to this limitation, consistent with the resource‑generation aim.

### Biological model: a valid discovery platform with a qualified baseline

The IL7R‑deficient reconstitution model is strategically sound: it avoids irradiation conditioning that damages stromal tissue [4], the thymus is already formed with recognisable cortical and medullary regions, and growth is inducible by normal marrow‑derived progenitors [1]. However, Moore et al. (2017) demonstrated that the IL7R‑deficient thymus exhibits an approximately fivefold enrichment of cTEC frequency relative to mTEC, a near‑complete loss of organised medullary structures, and co‑expression of K8 and K5 in most remaining epithelial cells suggestive of developmental immaturity [5]. This means the baseline stromal state differs qualitatively from a normal thymus—it is structurally and compositionally altered by chronic lymphoid deficiency, not merely hypocellular. The proposal is correct that the model concerns growth after nascent organ formation rather than embryonic specification [1], and the Moore et al. data show that the stromal compartment retains the capacity to respond to wild‑type lymphoid signals in mixed bone marrow chimeras [5], supporting the inference that signals identified during induced growth reflect lymphoid‑to‑stromal communication. The practical consequence is that signals already maximally engaged by residual lymphoid cells or innate lymphoid cells may not appear dynamic, and the candidate list will be filtered through the lens of this particular starting state—a qualification that constrains generalisability to normal thymic homeostasis but does not undermine the discovery purpose.

### Receptor‑ligand identification and prioritisation: transparent but underspecified at the integration step

The combined strategy—curated Human Plasma Membrane Receptome (HPMR) with mouse microarray identifiers, Gene Ontology‑based supplementary searches, and literature co‑citation mining [1]—is a reasonable design for an exploratory R21. The HPMR provides an anchor of high‑confidence interactions, but Ramilowski et al. (2015) found many genuine pairs missing from this and similar resources, which “originated many years ago, and are not extensively updated” [2]. The connectomeDB2025 release now contains 3,579 experimentally supported ligand‑receptor pairs [6], suggesting the Receptome captures only a minority of known interactions and systematically misses non‑peptide, ECM, and channel‑mediated signals. The literature co‑citation approach broadens coverage but at the cost of noise; Wright et al. (2024) noted that co‑citation networks can exhibit circularity with literature‑derived gene sets [7]. The expression filter—requiring both a stromal receptor and its lymphoid ligand to be expressed at a relevant stage and region [1]—is the critical specificity mechanism, but the proposal does not estimate how many candidates will survive this filter, and the final candidate set size remains uncertain.

The multi‑field prioritisation framework in Aim 3 is well‑dimensional and transparent: individual evidence scores are preserved rather than collapsed into a single ranking, allowing subsequent users to apply different weighting schemes [1]. The thymus‑specific co‑citation metric—receptor‑ligand co‑citation divided by thymus co‑citation—is a creative element designed to identify known interactions with unrecognised thymic roles. STRING evidence, time‑course ANOVA, signal intensity, and reagent availability provide orthogonal support channels. However, the transition from quantitative scores to final candidate selection relies on “manual review of the strongest quantitative candidates” [1] without an explicit decision rule, introducing a degree of investigator‑dependent selection. This is a pragmatic choice—comparable multi‑criteria frameworks in the literature similarly preserve separate evidence streams without automated integration [8] [9]—and the preservation of individual scores ensures the basis for any selection decision remains auditable. The subsequent biological validation in Aim 4 provides an independent test that can retrospectively validate or challenge the prioritisation logic.

### Biological validation: appropriately scoped proof of concept

Aim 4 is explicitly framed as supplying “proof of concept rather than a complete mechanistic account of every candidate” [1]. The main readouts are thymic size and cellular composition; candidates “responsible for growth support are expected to show a hypocellular thymus when disrupted” [1]. The proposal honestly acknowledges that detailed dissection of proliferation, differentiation, and survival mechanisms is outside this exploratory project’s scope [1]. This is a legitimate initial validation: finding that a candidate mutant mouse has a small thymus establishes that the disrupted interaction affects thymic growth in vivo, which is a meaningful biological finding at the R21 stage.

The marrow transfer test—transplanting mutant donor marrow into IL7R‑deficient recipients to assess whether the mutant lymphoid compartment fails to induce normal growth [1]—is a well‑precedented genetic complementation strategy that cleanly localises the requirement to the lymphoid ligand without confounding stromal effects. Van Ewijk et al. (2000), using a conceptually similar marrow transfer approach in RAGnull→tgε26 chimeras, demonstrated that lymphoid signals induce qualitative architectural reorganisation of cortical epithelium, not merely quantitative growth [10]. The Danzl et al. (2014) study used nine‑parameter flow cytometry to distinguish TEC subset expansion from differentiation, revealing proliferative transitions invisible to thymic size alone [11].

What Aim 4 cannot establish, by design, is whether the signal supports stromal proliferation, survival, differentiation, or some combination. The proposal does not specify which stromal subpopulations will be quantified in Aim 4, and the candidate identities—and therefore the detailed validation experiments—depend on Aims 1–3 [1]. This candidate‑dependence is inherent to a discovery pipeline and is acknowledged rather than hidden. The consequence is that Aim 4 will establish biological relevance at the level of “this interaction matters for thymic growth,” a defensible proof‑of‑concept outcome for an R21, with mechanistic dissection reserved for subsequent studies.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility is rated moderate (C), with high confidence.** The proposed work is achievable in principle—each individual method is published and within the laboratory’s demonstrated capability—but the project faces a substantial timeline risk arising from the serial dependency of the main expression experiment on two sequential kinetic studies, each of which must be completed and analysed before the subsequent phase can begin.

The scientific methods themselves are established. The laboratory has published both the computational stromal expression subtraction approach and the non‑myeloablative IL7R‑deficient transplantation model [1]. Figure 1 demonstrates successful laser microdissection and RNA recovery from an atrophic thymus only slightly larger than the four‑week‑old IL7R‑deficient thymus to be used in the main experiment, yielding approximately 10 ng RNA from a single 20 μm section [1]. This directly supports the feasibility of obtaining analysable RNA from the small samples involved, which is a critical practical hurdle. The Human Plasma Membrane Receptome group has supplied its receptor‑ligand database with mouse microarray identifiers for batch matching [1], removing a substantial manual curation burden from the computational pipeline. Existing computational scripts and the team’s prior experience with the expression analysis workflow provide the basis for the prioritisation step [1].

The core feasibility concern is the timeline, which the proposal describes in overlapping ranges without explicit decision gates. The schedule allocates 6–8 months to establishing growth kinetics and obtaining matched tissue and cell samples, the next 6–8 months primarily to analysis and prioritisation, and the remaining approximately 8–12 months to refining results for publication and biological testing [1]. Even at the midpoints of these ranges—7 months, 7 months, and 10 months—the total reaches 24 months against the stated two‑year project period. The R21 mechanism caps duration at two years, and no‑cost extensions are not typically granted beyond the second year [12]. A serial reading in which the kinetic series precede the main experiment, which in turn precedes analysis, produces a timeline that exceeds this ceiling with no contingency for the dependent Aim 4 validation.

The critical dependency is this: the first kinetic series (nine time points to day 32, three recipients per time point) must be completed and analysed before the second, higher‑resolution series can be designed to bracket growth initiation and the peak [1]. Only after both series—which together require 216 mice [1] and approximately 5–6 months of calendar time for transplantation, monitoring, and analysis—are complete can the five target stages for the main expression experiment be selected. The 6–8 month allocation for the combined kinetics and sampling phase therefore leaves little room for the main experiment’s 45 recipient mice, microdissection of cortex and medulla from each, lymphoid purification, RNA preparation, and 60 microarray hybridisations within that same window. A credible contingency would specify which kinetic endpoints are go/no‑go gates, what the fallback sampling stages would be if kinetics prove ambiguous, and how the main experiment can begin before the full analysis of the second kinetic series is complete. None of this is provided. Figure 2 is explicitly a theoretical curve, not observed data [1], so the sampling stages are entirely dependent on these kinetic outcomes.

The pooling strategy—three mice per array, three independently prepared arrays per condition [1]—is appropriately scaled for an exploratory resource and consistent with the R21 mechanism. The statistical power limitation resulting from this design is a recognised feature of exploratory discovery projects, not a feasibility failure.

A further practical risk is the unspecified number of candidates that will survive the receptor‑ligand identification and expression‑filtering steps. With approximately 900 receptors and 600 ligands yielding a potential pairwise search space of over half a million combinations before expression filtering, the final candidate set size is uncertain and depends on how many stromal receptors and lymphoid ligands are expressed above threshold at each stage. If the expression filter yields hundreds of candidates, the manual review step—described as making “focused qualitative evaluation possible after reducing the large candidate set” [1]—may itself become a bottleneck. This risk is inherent to a discovery pipeline and does not independently threaten feasibility, but it compounds the timeline pressure.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety is rated good (B), with high confidence.** The proposed animal work is ethically appropriate, justified by a clear scientific purpose that requires an intact organ system, and supported by oversight and welfare provisions that are proportionate to the procedures described.

The project asks how developing lymphoid cells instruct the growth and organization of thymic stromal tissue—a question that cannot be addressed in cell culture or organoid systems alone because it depends on the three‑dimensional architecture, vascular supply, and dynamic cellular trafficking of an intact thymus. The proposal explicitly justifies the animal model on this basis [1], and the reasoning is sound: the cortical and medullary regions, their distinct stromal populations, and the progressive colonization by donor‑derived progenitors during induced growth require the organ context that the IL7R‑deficient reconstitution model provides.

The animal numbers are itemized and justified. Aims 1–3 require 420 mice: 90 IL7R‑deficient mice to yield the 45 male recipients for the main expression experiment, 90 sex‑matched wild‑type donors, 108 mice for each of two preliminary kinetic series (216 total), and 24 animals for breeding [1]. The proposal notes that where feasible, marrow from a single donor can serve more than one recipient, reducing donor use [1]. Aim 4 is expected to require fewer than 200 additional animals, with an amendment to specify the number after candidate selection [1]. This staged commitment—bounding the anticipated need while deferring the precise request until the candidates are known—is appropriate for a discovery pipeline where the validation targets depend on the outcome of Aims 1–3. The total anticipated use, approximately 620 mice across the full project, is proportionate to the scope of a two‑year exploratory study.

The welfare provisions described are consistent with standard institutional and regulatory expectations for rodent research. The proposal states that procedures causing more than momentary distress will be performed under anesthesia, animals will be monitored through recovery and at least daily for the subsequent week, and humane intervention or euthanasia will be applied for severe discomfort [1]. Veterinary oversight is specified. The non‑myeloablative transplantation approach—intravenous administration of bone marrow progenitors without chemical or radiation conditioning [1]—is a meaningful refinement relative to conventional bone marrow transplant protocols. Zhang et al. (2014) demonstrated that irradiation conditioning profoundly impairs thymic homing of progenitors through chemokine disruption [4]; avoiding conditioning therefore serves both the scientific objective of preserving stromal integrity and the welfare objective of reducing procedural burden. The use of IL7R‑deficient mice as recipients rather than generating new conditional mutants for the discovery phase also leverages an existing model, avoiding unnecessary generation of additional lines.

The data‑sharing plan commits to communication through publications and presentations, with release upon acceptance of the main findings subject to a possible 60‑day institutional intellectual‑property review delay [1]. The specific presentation format and any access restrictions are stated to be determined when the data are generated. While the absence of a pre‑specified repository or accession commitment limits advance assessment of data accessibility, the commitment to release upon acceptance is a standard and acceptable practice for an R21. The proposal further notes that unique mouse resources, if produced, will be shared under applicable NIH resource‑sharing requirements [1]. These arrangements do not raise ethical concerns and are consistent with the exploratory, resource‑generating character of the project.

## Management

**Management is rated moderate (C), with high confidence.** The plan concentrates almost the entire experimental and computational workload onto a single postdoctoral fellow whose required skill breadth is unusually demanding, and the schedule lacks explicit decision gates for a serial dependency that can consume half the project period before the main expression experiment begins. These arrangements present a material risk to delivery of Aim 4 within the two‑year R21 ceiling, even though the laboratory’s published track record and the PI’s supervision provide a credible foundation.

### Personnel

The personnel plan assigns a full‑time postdoctoral fellow to perform most experimental and computational work, with PI Petrie supervising all biological and informatic activities, training the fellow, overseeing protocols and resources, and reviewing raw data before publication [1]. Mohammad Fallahi, an informatic scientist with prior experience in the laboratory, provides training and specialist assistance at 10% effort. The proposal acknowledges that Fallahi does not have capacity to absorb the computational workload alone and emphasises that the postdoctoral fellow must be trained to perform it [1]. This is an honest acknowledgement of a real constraint, but it does not resolve it.

The breadth of skills required from this single individual is considerable: intravenous transplantation in mice, timed sacrifice and thymus dissection, laser microdissection of cortical and medullary regions from sub‑millimetre tissue sections, RNA extraction from nanogram‑scale samples, lymphoid cell purification by flow cytometry, Affymetrix microarray processing, and the computational pipeline—microarray normalisation, the published stromal subtraction procedure, receptor‑ligand database matching, PubMed/PubMed Central literature mining with custom exclusion logic, Gene Ontology‑based receptor and ligand identification, collapsing multiple probesets to EntrezGene identifiers, time‑course ANOVA with multiple‑testing correction, STRING integration, hierarchical clustering, and construction of a searchable database with individual and combined scores [1]. The computational component is not a minor adjunct to the wet‑lab work; it is a substantial analytical undertaking that the proposal itself anticipates will require intensive effort around months 7–12 [1].

The laboratory’s prior publication record—Griffith et al. (2009) in Immunity, with Fallahi as a co‑author—demonstrates that the team has successfully executed the stromal mapping method [1]. However, that study was a steady‑state description of wild‑type adult thymus, not a five‑stage time course requiring coordination of timed transplantations with region‑matched microdissection and lymphoid purification at each stage. The added experimental complexity multiplies the hands‑on effort relative to the published work. Success depends heavily on whether the recruited fellow brings prior experience in both thymus transplantation and computational biology, or can acquire competence in one domain rapidly enough to execute the other independently. Fallahi’s 10% training role is a mitigant but cannot substitute for the fellow’s own capability.

### Schedule

The schedule allocates 6–8 months to establishing growth kinetics and obtaining matched samples, the next 6–8 months primarily to analysis and prioritisation, and the remaining approximately 8–12 months to refining results for publication and biological testing [1]. Summed at the upper ends, these phases reach 28 months against the stated two‑year project period. The R21 mechanism caps duration at two years, and no‑cost extensions beyond the second year are not typically granted [12].

The critical risk is the serial dependency already described under Feasibility: the two kinetic series must be completed and analysed before the five target stages for the main experiment can be selected, consuming 5–6 months of calendar time before a single microarray can be processed. The schedule language describes overlapping ranges, which introduces ambiguity about the degree of parallelism. If the main experiment’s sampling begins during months 6–8 while analysis starts on earlier samples, the total could fit within 24 months. However, the proposal does not articulate this as a deliberate parallel strategy with decision gates. The more natural reading—kinetic series precede the main experiment, which precedes analysis—produces a serial timeline that exceeds the R21 duration.

### Resources and coordination

The laboratory provides tissue preparation, microscopy, and standard molecular equipment; shared facilities provide flow cytometry and sorting, laser microdissection, microarray processing, and computational infrastructure [1]. These are appropriate and well‑matched to the proposed work, and the laboratory has established access and experience with each. The PI’s direct supervision, training role, and commitment to reviewing raw data before publication [1] provide appropriate scientific oversight.

The linked outputs—the expression time course, an annotated interaction database with links to supporting literature and resources, initial mouse‑model findings, and publications [1]—are appropriately specified for the project’s scope. The commitment to preserving individual evidence scores in the database rather than collapsing them into a single ranking [1] is a design choice that enhances the resource’s transparency and reusability, and it is consistent with good data management practice.

The overall management judgement reflects that the project has a credible scientific leadership structure and adequate institutional resources, but that the concentration of broad expertise in a single postdoctoral position and the unresolved serial dependency in the schedule create a material risk that Aim 4—the biological validation that distinguishes this proposal from a purely computational resource—will be compressed or incomplete within the two‑year period.

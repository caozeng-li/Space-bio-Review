# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project develops and tests a mechanistic neural-fluctuation account of masked detection and spectral coding in the auditory system, with a focus on individual differences between listeners with normal hearing and sensorineural hearing loss. The work combines a computational model of the auditory periphery and midbrain, single-neuron recordings from the inferior colliculus of awake rabbits, and human psychophysical testing across three aims: characterizing frequency-dependent cue use, testing models of simultaneous and forward masking, and extending the account to spectral slopes in pinna cues and fricative consonants. Its most valuable contribution would be a principled, individualized framework linking peripheral pathophysiology to suprathreshold perceptual deficits, explaining why listeners with similar audiograms can differ markedly in their hearing-in-noise performance, and providing a basis for personalized intervention strategies beyond simple amplification.

The proposal builds on a strong track record of prior work from the Carney laboratory, including published models (Zilany et al. auditory-nerve model, Maxwell et al. 2020 IC model) and human data (Leong et al. 2020) showing that listeners shift from envelope to energy cues at frequencies with elevated thresholds. The neural-fluctuation account offers falsifiable predictions that distinguish it from existing energy-based and modulation-filterbank models. The within-listener, individualized approach is methodologically appropriate and precedented. However, three limitations materially affect the judgement: (i) the physiological recording target of approximately 200 neurons per stimulus condition across three aims is not supported by the laboratory’s published throughput of approximately 160 neurons for a single focused paradigm; (ii) the individualized peripheral model relies on a fixed outer-hair-cell to inner-hair-cell attribution rule that is not verified per listener, and distortion-product otoacoustic emission measurements lose constraining power when emissions are absent in ears with moderate or greater loss; (iii) the proposal lacks explicit go/no-go decision points and a quantitative session-by-session yield projection for the physiology arm. The computational modeling using published datasets and the human psychophysics are well-supported and can proceed largely independently of these concerns.

This is a scientifically ambitious proposal grounded in a productive research program. Its strongest assets—a well-motivated theoretical framework, falsifiable predictions, and an individualized modeling strategy—are partly offset by an unrealistic physiological recording scope and unresolved parameter-constraint assumptions. The computational and human psychophysics components can deliver valuable knowledge even with a scaled-back physiology arm. The core contribution of individualized, fluctuation-based models tested across multiple paradigms is worthwhile and achievable within the proposed period if the physiological targets are realistically prioritized.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

The question at the centre of this project—whether a unified, physiologically grounded neural-fluctuation mechanism can explain masked detection, forward masking and spectral slope coding in listeners with normal and impaired hearing—is of substantial scientific importance. The attainable knowledge extends beyond a descriptive catalogue of deficits. The project aims to produce an individualized, mechanistic framework that links specific forms of peripheral sensorineural damage to the suprathreshold cues a listener actually uses. This would explain why two individuals with similar audiograms can perform markedly differently in noise, and it could provide a principled basis for interventions tailored to an individual’s residual cue profile rather than to their audibility loss alone [1]. That translational horizon is both clinically relevant and scientifically generative.

The proposal concentrates on a set of results that existing energy-based power-spectrum models handle poorly. Lentz et al. (1999) showed that listener thresholds in notched noise increased only approximately 3 dB when the overall stimulus level was randomly varied over a 30-dB range, whereas an energy-detection model fitted to the fixed-level data predicted approximately 10 dB of threshold elevation [2]. The Maxwell et al. (2020) IC model—which passes the Zilany et al. (2014) auditory-nerve periphery through modulation-tuned midbrain neurons—reproduced that small roving-level effect without parameter fitting, with model thresholds averaging approximately 3 dB below human thresholds and tracking the same trends across conditions [2]. The same model also reproduced the characteristic U-shaped profile-analysis function in the roving-level paradigm, explaining both the rise at low component counts (beat frequencies fall outside the IC neurons’ best-modulation-frequency range) and the rise at high component counts (the population response dips become blurred) within a single mechanism [2]. These are well-chosen benchmark findings: they discriminate between competing accounts, and the model’s success across them without retuning the auditory-filter bandwidth is an existence proof that the fluctuation framework is not merely a redescription at a different level.

The proposal’s emphasis on listener-specific models and cross-task prediction adds considerable value. Leong et al. (2020) from the same laboratory already documented within-listener, frequency-dependent shifts from envelope to energy cues in hearing-impaired listeners, with rove-effect size at 4 kHz correlated with pure-tone thresholds after controlling for age [3]. The current project builds directly on that observation: rather than pooling listeners into crude hearing-loss categories, it proposes to characterize each individual’s cue-use pattern in Aim 1 and then test whether a peripheral model constrained by that individual’s audiogram and distortion-product otoacoustic emissions—but not refitted task by task—predicts performance on the diverse masking and spectral-coding tasks of Aims 2 and 3 [1]. Success would demonstrate that a common, individually parameterized peripheral representation supports performance across qualitatively different auditory challenges. Even partial success—identifying which tasks and which listeners are well predicted and where the model breaks down—would illuminate the boundary between peripheral and central contributions to suprathreshold deficits. That is precisely the kind of structured knowledge that an ambitious investigation of this kind should seek.

## Innovation

The project adds a genuine conceptual and methodological advance beyond prior work rather than representing an incremental extension. Its core innovation lies in identifying the physiological origin and neural transformation of fluctuation cues—from nonlinear peripheral processing through modulation-tuned midbrain neurons to population-rate codes—and then testing this mechanistic account systematically across simultaneous masking, forward masking, and spectral slope coding within the same listeners and the same model framework. This integrative scope, combined with the individualized modeling strategy, distinguishes the proposal from earlier work that treated these phenomena separately or relied on group-average explanations.

**Falsifiable predictions that differentiate the account from existing models.** The proposal tests specific hypotheses that could be wrong rather than simply demonstrating that fluctuations matter. The Lentz et al. (1999) roving-level notched-noise results were already interpreted in terms of envelope cues, and the Dau et al. (1997) modulation-filterbank model explained several notched-noise masking patterns using envelope-fluctuation processing [4]. The proposal’s novelty lies in its specific physiological hypotheses: that peripheral nonlinearities—inner-hair-cell saturation, synaptic adaptation, synchrony capture—produce fluctuation cues that differ qualitatively from those in the acoustic envelope [2], and that midbrain modulation-tuned populations convert these cues into rate-based codes. Critically, the proposal identifies experimental manipulations that could falsify this account: replacing a tone target with equal-energy narrowband noise should produce different thresholds because the noise target’s own envelope fluctuations interact differently with masker fluctuations [1]; manipulating masker modulation content should affect thresholds in ways energy models cannot predict [1]; and adding masker modulation in forward masking should raise thresholds predictably based on fluctuation relationships during the gap [1]. The 2025 Fritzinger and Carney finding that narrowband tone-in-noise responses depend on neural-fluctuation sensitivity whereas wideband tone-in-noise involves fundamentally different broad-inhibition mechanisms [5] illustrates the kind of mechanistic specificity the framework demands. These are not post-hoc accommodations but prospective tests with clear alternative outcomes.

**Individualized, cross-task prediction strategy.** Prior work from the Carney laboratory established that listeners transition from envelope to energy cues at frequencies with elevated thresholds [1], and the Zaar and Carney (2022) study showed that individualized auditory-nerve models predict speech-in-noise performance across diverse interferers [6]. The current proposal extends this approach in a new direction: rather than fitting a model independently to each task, it proposes to constrain each listener’s peripheral model using only Aim 1 characterization (audiogram, distortion-product otoacoustic emissions, roving-level detection) and then use that same model to predict performance on simultaneous notched-noise masking, forward masking, pinna-cue processing, and consonant identification [1]. This cross-task prediction without re-fitting is methodologically innovative in the masking literature. It transforms the modeling enterprise from curve-fitting to genuine hypothesis testing: systematic prediction failures identify where central processing or additional peripheral factors must be invoked, which is scientifically more informative than task-specific optimized fits.

| Prior approach | Innovation in this proposal |
| --- | --- |
| Group-average models fitted to each task | Individualized models constrained once, tested across multiple tasks |
| Separate explanations for simultaneous masking, forward masking, profile analysis | Single fluctuation framework tested across all paradigms |
| Audiogram as sole individual predictor | Audiogram plus otoacoustic emissions constraining a physiologically detailed periphery |
| Stimulus-envelope or modulation-filterbank accounts | Neural-level account with explicit peripheral nonlinearities and midbrain MTF populations |

**Integrative scope across three experimental domains.** The combination of computational modeling, awake-rabbit IC physiology, and human psychophysics with normal-hearing and hearing-impaired listeners is uncommon in a single project. The proposal does not simply develop a model and test it against published data, nor does it collect physiology and psychophysics in separate efforts. It plans coordinated experiments where model simulations identify informative stimulus conditions for physiology, physiological results constrain model development, and individualized model predictions are tested against individual listeners’ psychophysical data. The physiological preparation itself provides access to the modulation-tuned IC neurons that are central to the theoretical framework [1], and the awake preparation avoids the confounds of anesthesia on modulation sensitivity.

**Relationship to existing frameworks.** The relationship to the established Dau et al. modulation-filterbank model requires acknowledgement but does not diminish the proposal’s originality. Both frameworks analyze envelope fluctuations through modulation-tuned channels, but they operate at different levels: the Dau model is a functional model of effective signal processing using linear Gammatone filters and a formal modulation filterbank, whereas the Carney model is a mechanistic neural model with physiologically detailed auditory-nerve processing, same-frequency inhibition-excitation brainstem circuits with explicit time constants, and signal-detection-theory decision variables incorporating population covariance [2] [4] [7]. Whether these architectural differences produce qualitatively different behavioural predictions across the full range of proposed experiments is precisely what the project aims to determine—it is the research question, not a premise that must be proven in advance. The framework’s current limitations—it does not yet incorporate cross-channel profile comparisons, envelope phase relationships, or dorsal cochlear nucleus contributions [1] [2]—are explicitly identified as future directions. Acknowledging these boundaries strengthens rather than weakens the proposal: it shows awareness of what the current model can and cannot explain, and it identifies where further development may be needed.

## Rigor

The project’s within-listener, individualized design, the quality-control procedures for psychophysical threshold estimation, and the model-testing strategy of evaluating predicted trends rather than single-point fits together support the intended inferences well. This core is appropriately rigorous for the stated goals.

**Evidential strategy.** The proposal does not rest on a single hypothesis test or a group comparison. It builds listener-specific models from Aim 1 characterization and then tests whether those models predict each listener’s pattern of thresholds across multiple frequencies, notch widths, and tasks in Aims 2 and 3 [1]. The decision variable is a population-level signal-detection metric that incorporates response covariance across neural channels [1] [2], avoiding the unrealistic independence assumptions of simpler decoding schemes. The emphasis on trends across stimulus parameters—a model whose trends cannot be reconciled with observed thresholds can be rejected even if a single threshold is matched [1]—is a principled approach that acknowledges the simplifying assumptions any model must make while still permitting strong inference.

**Within-listener psychophysical design.** Each listener serves as their own control for the fixed-versus-roving-level comparison that operationalizes cue use [1], and the interleaved tracking procedure controls for session-to-session variability. The threshold-estimation protocol—discarding the first six reversals, estimating from the final ten, repeating tracks where the standard deviation exceeds 5 dB, and running four repetitions per condition—is standard and adequate [1]. Leong et al. (2020), from the same laboratory, used comparable procedures and obtained within-listener standard deviations that allowed detection of rove effects as small as 0.8–1.4 dB in normal-hearing listeners [3].

**Limitations of the individualized modeling pipeline.** Two features of the individualized modeling strategy materially limit the strength of the cross-task predictions that the proposal treats as a central deliverable. Neither is fatal—the approach remains substantially more principled than group-average models—but they bound the resolution of individualization that the project can realistically achieve.

The Zilany et al. (2014) auditory-nerve model provides two free parameters per characteristic frequency: C\_OHC, governing cochlear amplifier gain, and C\_IHC, governing inner-hair-cell sensitivity [7]. Reducing either parameter elevates model threshold, so a given audiometric loss in dB can be achieved through many combinations of OHC and IHC impairment. The proposal’s approach—attributing up to two-thirds of loss to OHC impairment and one-third to IHC impairment—resolves this degeneracy by adopting a fixed ratio drawn from standard assumptions in the literature [7], citing Zilany and Bruce (2007), Bruce et al. (2013), and Zaar and Carney (2022). This is a reasonable starting point, but it is not verified for each listener, and Guest et al. (2024) demonstrated that varying the OHC/IHC partition produces different model predictions [6].

Distortion-product otoacoustic emissions, which are generated by OHC nonlinearity, could in principle provide independent constraints on C\_OHC [1] [8], but the proposal does not specify a quantitative procedure for incorporating DPOAE data into parameter estimation. More critically, DPOAEs are typically absent or substantially reduced in ears with thresholds exceeding approximately 50 dB HL [8], and the proposal’s sensorineural-hearing-loss eligibility criteria permit thresholds up to 60 dB HL [1]. For listeners in the upper half of the eligible loss range, DPOAEs will provide a binary constraint—absent—rather than a graded one, offering no help in separating OHC from IHC contributions. The proposal does not address how parameters will be determined when DPOAEs are absent.

A further limitation concerns the central stage of the model. The IC model uses fixed parameters for the same-frequency inhibition-excitation circuits—excitatory and inhibitory weights, time constants, delays—that are not individualized [7]. Guest et al. (2024) explicitly note that no changes were made in the central or IC stage of the model in the hearing-impaired simulations; the simulations of hearing loss only accounted for peripheral effects and not for possible central changes associated with hearing loss such as reduced central inhibition [7]. This is a defensible simplifying assumption, but it means that task-specific prediction errors—for example, on forward masking, which engages temporal processing beyond the periphery, or on consonant identification, which draws on phonetic processing—may reflect central rather than peripheral individual differences. The proposal would be strengthened by specifying how such prediction failures will be diagnosed and interpreted.

**Participant sample and testing burden.** The proposal states both “15 age-matched control listeners and 30 listeners with SNHL” and “a total recruitment of 40 listeners” [1]. This internal inconsistency is a resolvable drafting issue; the difference between 40 and 45 is not scientifically decisive for a within-listener individualized modeling approach. The Aim 1 time estimate of approximately three hours [1] appears optimistic given that Leong et al. (2020) required roughly 10–11 hours for a comparable characterization [3]. The difference likely reflects testing at only two sensation levels and a more lenient stopping criterion; 5–7 hours for Aim 1 is more realistic. These are addressable refinements rather than fundamental design flaws.

## Feasibility

The computational modeling and human psychophysics components are well-supported by the laboratory’s track record and available resources. The physiological recording plan, however, has a significant feasibility gap that requires resolution. The target of approximately 200 neurons per stimulus condition across three aims is inconsistent with the laboratory’s own published throughput using the identical preparation, and the proposal lacks a quantitative yield projection that would allow this discrepancy to be assessed.

**Computational modeling.** This is the most independent and lowest-risk component. Published datasets—Glasberg and Moore (1990) for classical filter descriptions, Baker and Rosen (2002, 2006) for normal and impaired results across levels and frequencies, Lentz et al. (1999) for roving-level notched-noise paradigms, Irino et al. (2013) for asymmetric-notch manipulations, and the forward-masking literature from Oxenham, Plack, Jesteadt and colleagues [1]—provide an extensive test bed that can be exploited from the start of the project, independent of human or animal data collection [1]. The Zilany et al. (2014) auditory-nerve model is publicly available and well-documented, and the laboratory has published extensively using the same-frequency inhibition-excitation brainstem and IC model architecture (Maxwell et al., 2020; Guest et al., 2024; Fritzinger and Carney, 2025, 2026) [2] [7] [5] [9]. Access to university high-performance computing and local computational facilities is adequate [1]. No critical dependency threatens this workstream.

**Human psychophysics.** The laboratory has direct, published experience with the core paradigm: Leong et al. (2020) tested 12 normal-hearing and 12 sensorineural-hearing-loss listeners using essentially the same roving-level detection task at 0.5–4 kHz, with participants completing 6–12 sessions of 3–6 tracks each [3]. The proposed 12–15 hours of testing per listener over 6–8 weeks [1] is consistent with that precedent. Sound-attenuated booths and auditory equipment are available [1], and recruitment procedures using institutional review are described [1].

**Physiological recording.** The proposal plans four Dutch-belted rabbits, each used for 12–24 months of recording, expecting responses from approximately 200 neurons for each stimulus condition [1]. The most directly comparable published evidence from this laboratory is Fan, Henry, and Carney (2021, Hearing Research), which used the identical preparation—four female Dutch-belted rabbits, the same tetrode microdrive system, daily approximately two-hour sessions—over extended periods (two rabbits from 17–55 months, two from 13–23 months) [10]. That multi-year effort yielded 163 single units total (120 tested with both Gaussian-noise and low-noise-noise maskers, 43 with Gaussian noise only) for essentially one stimulus paradigm: tone-in-noise with the tone near the neuron’s characteristic frequency, at five noise levels, with two masker types [10].

The proposal’s stimulus space is vastly larger. Aim 2a alone involves simultaneous notched-noise masking with systematic notch-width, target-level, and frequency manipulations, fixed, roving and equalized level comparisons, narrowband-noise targets, flanking-band slope variations, and low-noise noise [1]. Aim 2b adds forward-masking paradigms with roving levels, off-frequency maskers, and notched-noise forward maskers [1]. Aim 3 adds pinna-cue stimuli (head-related-impulse-response-convolved wideband noise, monaural and dichotic, multiple levels) and fricative consonant stimuli [1]. Before any of these conditions are tested, each neuron must be characterized for frequency tuning, response-map type, modulation transfer function, best modulation frequency, and interaural time and level sensitivity [1]—a characterization protocol that already consumed substantial recording time in the Fan et al. study without the additional interaural sensitivity measurement.

The Fan et al. throughput of 163 neurons for a single paradigm represents the practical upper bound from four animals over several years. The proposal’s requirement of approximately 200 neurons per stimulus condition across three aims would require throughput substantially exceeding any published study from this laboratory.

| Evidence | Throughput | Stimulus scope |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fan et al. (2021): same preparation, four rabbits, multi-year | 163 single units total | One paradigm (tone-in-noise, five levels, two masker types) |
| Proposed: four rabbits, 12–24 months each | \~200 per stimulus condition | Three aims, dozens of conditions across simultaneous masking, forward masking, pinna cues, fricatives |

The proposal’s own preliminary IC population data (Figures 3, 7, 8) appear based on modest neuron counts, consistent with the Fan et al. dataset. These figures demonstrate that meaningful model comparisons can be made with fewer neurons than 200 per condition, but they also reveal the actual throughput of the preparation. The laboratory has recently published focused physiological studies on narrowband tone-in-noise (Fan et al., 2021, 163 neurons), wideband tone-in-noise (Fritzinger and Carney, 2025), timbre (Fritzinger and Carney, 2026), and formant-frequency encoding (Agarwalla and Carney, 2026) [10] [5] [9] [11]. Each of these focused studies produced interpretable results with neuron counts consistent with the laboratory’s demonstrated throughput. The current proposal essentially combines work at the scale of several individual studies into a single project, which is ambitious but not inherently unrealistic if the work is recognized as a coordinated program requiring within-project prioritization. The principal concern is that the proposal does not provide a quantitative session-by-session yield projection. Without such a breakdown, the approximately 200-neuron-per-condition target must be treated as aspirational rather than grounded.

This feasibility gap is resolvable. The computational modeling can proceed independently, the human psychophysics can proceed independently, and the physiological arm can be prioritized to focus on the subset of stimulus conditions that most powerfully distinguish the neural-fluctuation account from energy-based alternatives—for example, the tone-versus-noise target comparison [1] and the narrowband notched-noise paradigms where fluctuation sensitivity dominates [5]. The proposal’s “Alternatives and scope” section [1] identifies scientific alternatives but does not address programmatic scope management; a quantitative yield projection and explicit prioritization criteria would convert this from a feasibility weakness into a manageable plan.

## Ethics and safety

The ethical framework described in the proposal is proportionate to the risks, and the specific safeguards for human participants and experimental animals reflect established practice in the laboratory and the wider field. The protections documented are adequate for the work described.

**Human participant safeguards** follow standard institutional procedures. The proposal specifies written informed consent, review through institutional oversight mechanisms, and data reporting using arbitrary identifiers to protect confidentiality [1]. Sound-delivery safeguards are designed to limit loudness exposure, and participants receive frequent breaks during testing sessions [1]. The maximum presentation level is capped at 100 dB SPL [1], which is a reasonable upper bound for a study of masking in hearing-impaired listeners. The testing burden—approximately 12–15 hours over 6–8 weeks per participant [1]—is within the range that the laboratory has successfully managed in prior work: Leong et al. (2020) reported that participants completed 6–12 sessions of roughly one hour each without documented adverse events [3]. The inclusion of older adults up to age 80 [1] is appropriate given that sensorineural hearing loss is strongly age-associated; excluding this population would undermine the project’s translational relevance.

**Animal welfare provisions** are described in appropriate detail. Four Dutch-belted rabbits will undergo sterile surgical implantation of a headbar and tetrode microdrive under general anesthesia, with postoperative analgesia and veterinary supervision [1] [10]. Animals are gradually habituated to the recording chair prior to data collection, which minimizes the stress of restraint. Daily recording sessions last approximately two hours [10], and the experimental task is entirely passive—animals listen to acoustic stimuli but perform no trained behaviour and receive no reinforcement contingent on neural activity [1]. This passive-listening design avoids the food- or water-restriction protocols common in operant animal psychophysics and limits welfare burden to the implantation surgery and the mild restraint of head-fixed recording. The laboratory has maintained the same preparation over multiple published studies (Fan et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2020; Fritzinger and Carney, 2025, 2026; Agarwalla and Carney, 2026), demonstrating that the surgical, husbandry, and recording procedures are established and sustainable [10] [12] [5] [9] [11]. Electrode locations may be verified histologically at the end of the study [1]; this terminal procedure is standard for chronic electrophysiology and provides essential anatomical confirmation. The number of animals—four rabbits—is justified by the need to sample neurons with diverse characteristic frequencies, modulation transfer function types, and binaural sensitivities [1].

**Data and resource sharing** is addressed through a commitment to make new models and collected data available on the laboratory website, with software supplied as MATLAB or C components including compiler instructions, readme files, and executable examples [1]. Associated publications will describe parameter determination and validation procedures [1], which is important given the complexity of the individualized modeling pipeline.

## Management

The project’s dependency structure is well-conceived at the conceptual level: computational modeling using published datasets can begin on day one, physiological recordings in rabbits proceed independently of human recruitment, and individualized model predictions can be tested for each listener as they complete Aim 1 characterization [1]. The Carney laboratory has an established record of sustained productivity with the proposed methods, including multiple published studies combining the same awake-rabbit IC preparation with computational modeling and human psychophysics [5] [9] [11] [7]. The consultant team provides complementary expertise in audiology, hearing-loss psychophysics, phonetics, and theoretical psychoacoustics [1], and the available infrastructure—sound-attenuated booths, auditory equipment, local and high-performance computing—is adequate for the proposed work [1].

Despite this solid structural foundation, the management plan has two weaknesses that materially affect confidence in delivering the full stated scope.

**Absence of a grounded quantitative plan for the physiological recording arm.** This is the most resource-intensive workstream and the one with the greatest demonstrated throughput constraints. The laboratory’s own published evidence—Fan et al. (2021), which used four rabbits over several years with the identical tetrode preparation and yielded 163 single units for a single stimulus paradigm [10]—provides a realistic benchmark. The proposal’s target of approximately 200 neurons per stimulus condition across three aims [1] implies a yield substantially exceeding that precedent, yet no quantitative session-by-session projection, expected number of fully characterized neurons per session, or projected yield per animal per month is supplied. The pre-characterization burden per neuron—frequency tuning, response-map type, modulation transfer function, best modulation frequency, and interaural sensitivity [1]—is extensive and consumes a significant fraction of the approximately two-hour daily recording window [10]. Without a grounded throughput analysis, the laboratory cannot credibly plan which stimulus conditions will be prioritized, how recording time will be allocated across aims, or when to conclude that a given condition has sufficient data. The most direct corrective would be to treat the Fan et al. (2021) throughput as the planning baseline and to identify the subset of stimulus conditions that carry the strongest diagnostic weight for distinguishing the neural-fluctuation account from energy-based alternatives.

**Lack of explicit decision points for scope adjustment.** The “Alternatives and scope” section describes scientific alternatives such as incorporating dorsal-cochlear-nucleus contributions or exploring envelope phase relationships [1], but it does not address programmatic scope management. This matters because the three aims span computational modeling, rabbit physiology, and human psychophysics across six stimulation paradigms; without decision rules, the risk of scope creep or diffuse under-delivery is real. The human psychophysics has a similar, though smaller, gap: the proposal would benefit from specifying a minimum number of fully characterized listeners—say, 20 sensorineural-hearing-loss participants after attrition—that would still support meaningful evaluation of individualized models, given that each complete participant contributes independently to the within-listener analysis [1].

| Management element | Strength | Gap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Workstream independence | Modeling uses published data; physiology independent of human recruitment | No coordination milestones for workstream convergence |
| Human participant flow | Staggered recruitment possible; each listener’s progression depends on Aim 1 completion | No minimum completer threshold for evaluating individualized models |
| Physiological throughput | Established preparation with published yield precedents | No session-by-session yield projection; aspirational target unsupported |
| Scope adjustment | Alternatives identified for scientific extensions | No go/no-go criteria or prioritization rules for the core experimental plan |

These gaps do not reflect on the scientific quality of the proposed work but on the ability to execute the full stated scope within the planned resources and period. They are addressable: a quantitative yield projection grounded in the laboratory’s historical data, a prioritized subset of physiological conditions, and a minimum-listener threshold for the individualized modeling evaluation would convert these management weaknesses into a credible operational plan. The computational and human psychophysics arms are independently well-supported and could deliver substantial value even if the physiological component requires mid-project reprioritization.

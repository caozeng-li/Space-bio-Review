# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project investigates when, how, and with what support patients should receive different categories of genetic test results through an electronic health record patient portal. Recognizing that genetic results range from pharmacogenomic findings to inherited cancer-risk variants, and that patients differ in their technology access, health literacy, and communication preferences, the study combines qualitative interviews with user-centered design to develop and iteratively test patient-facing report prototypes. The primary empirical contribution is the direct, within-study comparison of patient-defined concern thresholds and delivery preferences across four result categories—pharmacogenomic, hemochromatosis, colorectal cancer risk, and negative findings—linked to an evolving Points to Consider ethical framework. The intended outputs are downloadable electronic prototypes, an ethical framework refined by patient data, and a body of evidence about the kinds of portal content and access arrangements that patients find usable and ethically appropriate. The most valuable knowledge is the systematically derived, actionable design guidance for integrating genomic medicine into widely deployed patient-portal infrastructure, with explicit attention to equity and the continuum from electronic-only to provider-facilitated release.

The project draws its strength from the integration of thoughtfully chosen methods: paired qualitative studies that compare genetic with non-genetic portal results (Aim 1) and systematically explore four distinct genetic result types (Aim 2), followed by three rounds of iterative cognitive usability testing with both experienced portal users and non-users recruited through a safety-net clinic (Aim 3). The proposal builds directly on the team's prior work showing that clinicians disagree about what constitutes a "sensitive" result, turning appropriately to patients to define their own thresholds. The advisory structure is well-developed, with clinical, laboratory, informatics, and external prototype experts providing quality checks at key points.

Material limitations are identified in the sections that follow: the schedule bundles intensive analysis, prototype creation, and the first usability round into a single six-month block with minimal buffer; the usability problem-discovery rate is overstated, with the planned 14 users per participant type per round expected to find roughly 85–95% rather than the claimed 98% of problems; and Aim 2's 10 participants per category support code-level but not meaning saturation, limiting the depth of within-category claims. These limitations are inherent in the scope of a formative two-year R21 and are partially mitigated by the iterative design and external reviews, but they do constrain the certainty and scope of the project's inferential ambitions. The proposal does not specify a contingency plan for delays, leaving the project vulnerable to cascading schedule pressure.

Weighing the contribution against the limitations, the project addresses a genuine and well-defined knowledge gap with a coherent, methodologically grounded plan. The main identified weaknesses—schedule compression, a moderately overstated usability discovery-rate claim, and limited within-category sample sizes for Aim 2—reduce the inferential scope and add schedule risk but do not undermine the central deliverables. The principal threats to delivery concern recruitment within the compressed timeline and the lean execution capacity during the prototype phase. These are characteristic of an ambitious two-year R21 rather than fundamental design flaws. The trade-off supports a judgement that the current plan provides a sufficient route to a worthwhile, achievable contribution, meriting support with attention to schedule monitoring and realistic contingency planning for the Q5–Q6 prototype-and-testing block.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**The project addresses a timely and practically important question: how should electronic health record patient portals present genetic test results, given that such results vary widely in their implications and that patients differ in their access, literacy, and communication needs?** The most valuable expected knowledge is the systematic, patient-derived evidence on concern thresholds, information needs, and acceptable delivery arrangements across four deliberately distinct genetic result categories—pharmacogenomic, hemochromatosis, colorectal cancer risk, and negative findings. By connecting this evidence to iteratively tested electronic report prototypes and an evolving Points to Consider ethical framework, the project can produce actionable design guidance and a basis for subsequent implementation research.

The heterogeneity of genetic results is central to the project's rationale. Pharmacogenomic findings may inform medication choices without indicating illness; a hemochromatosis result identifies a treatable condition with implications for relatives; an inherited colorectal-cancer-risk result carries substantial consequences for the patient and family; a negative result may appear reassuring, but its interpretation depends on test context and family history [1]. The proposal argues—correctly, given the external evidence—that treating every genetic result as equally sensitive, or every negative result as simple, risks missing clinically and psychologically important distinctions.

The existing literature supports this concern while confirming the knowledge gap. Bruno et al. (2022), surveying 8,030 patients, found that 68% accepted electronic return of genetic test results with a three-week wait, but most preferred in-person delivery for cancer (54%) and fetal miscarriage (53%) results [2]. Petrovskaya et al. (2023) identified mode and timing of result release as among the least-discussed topics in the portal results literature [3]. Bell et al. (2026) reported that only 18% of patients who initially chose immediate electronic release of hereditary cancer results would make that choice again [4]. The proposal's direct, within-study comparison of these categories is therefore well-justified.

The continuum of delivery arrangements the project will investigate—electronic return alone; electronic return followed by possible conversation with a genetics professional; electronic availability only after such a conversation; and personal return with portal posting as a supplement [1]—moves beyond the binary mode questions that characterize most preference studies. The inclusion of non-portal-users in Aim 3 (42 of 84 participants, recruited partly through a safety-net clinic) [1] adds further value by linking equity considerations to concrete usability findings, addressing a gap that most portal studies overlook. This concern was recently reinforced by Lowe et al. (2025), whose survey of genetic counselors found that 51% of non-ordering counselors strongly agreed that patients may misunderstand results without support [5].

The intended outputs are appropriately framed as foundations for later work: downloadable prototypes, a Points to Consider framework refined by patient data, and evidence about the content and access arrangements patients find usable [1]. The direct comparison of genetic with non-genetic portal result experiences (Aim 1) provides an additional layer of evidence that the expert analysis of the literature indicates is novel. The project cannot establish health outcomes or population-level effectiveness at this formative stage, nor does it claim to; its contribution is the systematically derived knowledge needed to design the clinical and implementation studies that can.

## Innovation

**The project makes a well-defined and genuinely original contribution by integrating qualitative bioethics with user-centered design (UCD) to compare patient-defined concern thresholds and preferences across four deliberately varied genetic result categories—pharmacogenomic, hemochromatosis, colorectal cancer risk, and negative findings—within a single investigation, and by connecting those findings directly to iteratively tested electronic report prototypes.** The expert analysis of the literature confirms that no existing study has performed this head-to-head, within-study comparison in the context of EHR-mediated result return, nor has any study linked such comparative patient data to the systematic development of concrete, testable design artifacts. The inclusion of non-portal-users to explore whether electronic return can serve as a substitute for personal delivery rather than merely a supplement is a further distinctive feature of the design. The overall contribution is appropriately characterized as an applied synthesis and extension of prior work that addresses a recognized gap, rather than a fundamentally new paradigm.

### What the project combines that prior work has not

The project's originality is best understood by examining the elements it brings together. Prior work has pursued the relevant strands separately. The GenomeCOMPASS study (Williams et al., 2017) applied UCD principles to develop an enhanced genomic report for parents of children undergoing whole-genome sequencing, but this work focused on a single clinical context and found that reports for uninformative results were not valued by parents [6]. The MyCancerGene portal (Trieu et al., 2025) used a five-step development process with patient and provider input, but was restricted to germline cancer genetic results and did not embed an ethical framework [7]. The YourPGx Oncology tool (Cho et al., 2025) provides patient-facing pharmacogenomic summaries, but was developed for a single result category without cross-category comparison [8]. The Genetics Adviser trial (Shickh et al., 2022) is evaluating a digital platform for the entire genetic testing journey, but it deploys predetermined content rather than deriving design requirements from exploratory patient interviews [9].

Patient preference surveys have examined "genetic testing" as a single category—Bruno et al. (2022) found 68% acceptance of electronic return for genetic results with a three-week wait [2]—or have studied specific conditions in isolation, such as Chan et al. (2021), who found that African-American participants accepted mailed pharmacogenomic results while rejecting that mode for "life-threatening" results [10]. Qualitatively, the existing evidence consistently shows that patients distinguish among result types, but no study has systematically compared those distinctions within a shared methodological framework.

What distinguishes the current proposal is the deliberate assembly of four elements that have not previously been combined: (a) simultaneous, head-to-head qualitative comparison across four meaningfully distinct result categories using shared methods; (b) explicit connection between patient-derived concern thresholds and ethical analysis within an evolving framework; (c) translation of qualitative findings into both Epic-compatible and blue-sky prototypes, rather than stopping at general recommendations; and (d) systematic inclusion of people who do not use the portal, recruited through a safety-net clinic, to test whether electronic return can substitute for personal delivery. The proposal's inquiry into a continuum of arrangements—from electronic-only to supplementary portal availability [1]—further refines the binary mode questions typical of existing preference research.

### The contribution of the Points to Consider framework

The Points to Consider ethical framework employs established bioethical principles—beneficence/nonmaleficence, respect for autonomy, justice/equity—that are familiar from the return-of-results literature, including the Jarvik et al. (2014) CSER/eMERGE consensus [11] and the Davis and Smith (2016) discussion specific to EHR-mediated pathology disclosure [12]. The framework's novelty therefore lies not in its foundational principles but in three operational aspects. First, its grounding in patient- rather than expert-derived data, refined iteratively across Aims 1–3 rather than asserted a priori [1]. Second, its translation of abstract principles into concrete, testable questions that directly inform prototype design, such as "Which mode, content, or presentation benefits or harms the patient?" [1]. Third, its coupling to UCD outputs so that ethical reasoning produces design decisions rather than remaining at the level of recommendation. This operational, design-linked application of ethical principles to EHR-mediated genetic result return, carried out comparatively across categories, represents a meaningful advance in the literature.

### Empirical grounding and connection to prior team work

Korngiebel's prior work—interviews with 20 clinicians about returning Lynch syndrome screening results through a portal—supports the proposal's logic directly. That work found that clinicians disagreed about which results should count as sensitive [1], and the present proposal turns correctly from that finding to patients to define their own thresholds. The proposal also draws on CSER work that developed categories of genetic conditions relevant to reproductive planning, finding that patient ratings of attributes such as predictability and severity largely validated expert taxonomies but showed unexpected patterns, including minimal perceived difference between "mild" and "serious" categories [1] [13]. The aim of testing and refining initial concern categories with patient data is therefore both empirically grounded and novel in the portal-return context.

## Rigor

**The qualitative research design is well-conceived for an exploratory R21, and the iterative user-centered design sequence is methodologically sound. A material limitation concerns the overstatement of the usability problem-discovery rate: the planned 14 users per participant type per round can be expected to find approximately 85–95% of problems rather than the claimed 98% minimum. Aim 2's within-category samples (n=10) support code-level saturation for identifying concern thresholds but not meaning saturation or demographic subgroup analysis within a single result type. These constraints reduce the inferential scope in specific respects but do not undermine the project's ability to meet its stated formative goals.**

### Overall design coherence

The overall design follows a coherent trajectory: generative qualitative research in Aims 1 and 2 informs prototype development, followed by three rounds of iterative cognitive usability testing with independent participants in Aim 3 [1]. The sampling plans and demographic stratification targets—age bands, gender balance, and minimum minority representation—are thoughtfully specified and provide a credible basis for capturing variation in aggregated analyses [1].

### Saturation-based stopping rule

The hybrid stopping rule—recruiting until diversity targets are met and the team judges that major themes have reached idea saturation, up to the planned maxima [1]—is a sound approach for this exploratory stage. The methodological literature supports this general strategy. Theme saturation is reported at approximately 9 interviews, and meaning saturation at approximately 24 interviews [14].

Applied to the proposal, Aim 1's 40 interviews should comfortably reach meaning saturation for the comparison of genetic versus non-genetic result experiences. Aim 2's 10 participants per category should reach code saturation—sufficient to identify the range of concern thresholds and delivery preferences across categories—but are unlikely to achieve meaning saturation within any single category. This is consistent with the proposal's own characterization of the categories as "starting points, not fixed classifications" [1], and the stated goal of identifying "the elements that determine concern thresholds" rather than producing exhaustive phenomenological accounts [1].

A limitation worth noting is that the saturation assessment is not stratified: the proposal states saturation will be judged on major themes overall, not separately for, for example, younger versus older participants or portal users versus non-users. The demographic strata serve primarily to ensure diversity in the sample rather than to guarantee saturation within each stratum. For an exploratory R21 this is defensible, but it means that claims about age- or ethnicity-specific patterns should remain appropriately tentative. Similarly, the demographic dimension in Aim 2 is best viewed as contributing to overall sample diversity rather than enabling within-category subgroup analysis.

### Qualitative analysis methods

The analysis plan—directed content and thematic analysis led by Korngiebel and Robins, with dual coding of all transcripts, consensus resolution of disagreements, and Atlas.ti for organization [1]—follows established practice. The combination of deductive coding from prior categories (including the CSER-derived condition attributes [1]) with inductive identification of emergent themes is appropriate for systematically comparing across result types and delivery modes while remaining open to unanticipated patient concerns. Dual coding with consensus resolution is a well-accepted strategy for enhancing trustworthiness in qualitative analysis. The use of the Points to Consider ethical framework as an organizing device that is refined throughout [1] provides additional analytical structure.

### Usability testing and the problem-discovery rate

The UCD sequence is well-structured: three rounds of cognitive usability interviews, each with 28 new participants (14 users and 14 non-users), testing revised prototypes [1]. External consultant heuristic walkthroughs between rounds 2 and 3 provide an additional quality check [1]. The cognitive interview approach—participants think aloud while completing tasks such as identifying recommended next steps from a report, with the assessment addressing both the experience users expect, desire, and value, and whether they can perform tasks [1]—is a well-established method for formative usability evaluation.

The critical qualification concerns the problem-discovery rate. The proposal claims that 14 users per participant type per round will yield "a minimum discovery rate above 98%, with a mean of 99.6%" [1], citing Faulkner (2003). Faulkner's empirical data, however, show that these figures correspond to sample sizes of n=40–50, not n=14. Faulkner's Table 2 reports the following discovery rates from 100 random samples at each sample size [15]:

| Sample size | Minimum % found | Mean % found |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | 55 | 85.6 |
| 10 | 82 | 94.7 |
| 15 | 90 | 97.1 |
| 20 | 95 | 98.4 |
| 40 | 98 | 99.6 |

With 14 users, the performance would fall between the 10-user and 15-user rows: the expected minimum discovery rate is approximately 85–90%, and the mean approximately 95–97%. The claimed figures of "above 98%" minimum and "99.6%" mean actually correspond to the entire 84-participant pool in Faulkner's study, not a single round's 14 users per type. The proposal appears to have applied the cumulative discovery rate across all rounds to each participant type individually.

This represents a meaningful overstatement, not a fatal design flaw. Fourteen users per type remains a conventional and defensible sample for iterative usability testing, substantially exceeding the widely cited five-user rule. It will identify the majority of usability problems. The three-round iterative structure partially compensates, since problems missed in one round may be detected in another, though the rounds test revised rather than identical prototypes, and problems introduced by revisions may go undetected. The heuristic walkthroughs by external consultants between rounds 2 and 3 [1] provide an additional detection mechanism that partly compensates. Nevertheless, the proposal should accurately represent the inferential scope: "the main usability problems" will not be identified with near-certainty at this sample size, and some subtle problems—which Faulkner notes may have the most serious implications [15]—may be missed.

## Feasibility

**The integrated plan is logically sequenced and the institutional setting provides appropriate infrastructure, but the two-year timeline has two genuine compression points that create schedule risk, and the recruitment demands—particularly achieving Aim 2's four-category sampling with demographic stratification—are ambitious within the available windows. The project is broadly feasible, though delivery depends on efficient early-stage recruitment and a lean execution team with limited slack.**

### Schedule structure and compression points

The proposed eight-quarter schedule sequences Aim 1 interviews (Q1–Q2), Aim 1 analysis (Q2–Q3), Aim 2 interviews (Q3–Q4), Aim 2 analysis (Q4–Q5), prototype creation plus round 1 testing (Q5–Q6), revision plus round 2 with consultant review (Q6–Q7), and revision plus round 3 (Q7–Q8) [1]. The critical path runs through Aims 1–2 before prototype work can begin, since these aims supply the generative research that informs prototype content and design [1]. This dependency is logically sound but leaves the later phases vulnerable to early delays.

Two compression points warrant attention.

The first is the overlap of Aim 1 analysis with the launch of Aim 2 data collection in Q3. The proposal states that Aim 2 protocols will be "piloted and informed by Aim 1" [1], meaning preliminary rather than finalized Aim 1 results are needed to shape Aim 2 materials. This is a realistic dependency judgment, but if Aim 1 recruitment encounters delays in Q1–Q2, the analysis outputs needed to inform Aim 2 protocols could be pushed later, compressing Aim 2 collection into less than two full quarters.

The second and more significant compression point is the Q5–Q6 block, which must accommodate completion of Aim 2 analysis, synthesis of findings from both aims into design specifications, creation of approximately six to eight prototypes spanning Epic-style and blue-sky designs [1], piloting of the cognitive interview protocol, recruitment of 28 participants, conduct of 28 one-hour in-person cognitive interviews, and the round 1 usability analysis. This is a substantial amount of work to complete within six months, especially with a team that relies on Korngiebel and a graduate assistant for prototype revision and usability analysis [1]. The schedule bundles the entire prototype-creation-and-first-test cycle into a single two-quarter window with no visible buffer.

A further consideration is that round 3 occupies the final two quarters (Q7–Q8). Any major redesign needs emerging from round 3 cannot be tested within the grant period. This is an inherent limitation of fitting three iterative rounds into a two-year grant rather than a design flaw, but it means the final prototypes will not have been tested after the last round of changes. The absence of a documented contingency for schedule slippage—for example, whether round 3 could be abbreviated, whether lower-priority prototypes could be deferred, or whether prototype creation could begin in parallel with Aim 2 analysis using preliminary findings—leaves the project exposed to cascading pressure.

### Recruitment pathways and demands

The project draws on multiple recruitment channels: UW clinical and translational informatics services, the eCare team, relevant clinical departments, and—for Aim 3 non-users—the Harborview Medical Center Family Medicine Clinic, a safety-net setting with low eCare enrollment and substantial minority representation [1]. The advisory board includes Frederick Chen from Harborview Family Medicine [1], providing a direct link to that recruitment site. The overall rate of approximately 5–7 participants per month is not intrinsically unrealistic for a well-resourced academic medical center with EHR-based identification.

However, the eligibility criteria narrow the available pool. Aim 1 participants must have received both genetic and non-genetic results through eCare, been UW Medicine patients for at least 24 months, and used at least one eCare function in the preceding 12 months [1]. Aim 2 requires patients who received specific genetic result types within the preceding 12 months [1]. Achieving the demographic stratification targets—age, gender, minimum minority representation—within each aim's recruitment window while also meeting these clinical eligibility criteria represents a genuine challenge.

Aim 2's requirement of exactly ten participants in each of four genetic result categories—pharmacogenomic, hemochromatosis, colorectal cancer risk, and negative—adds further recruitment complexity. The pool of patients who received each specific result type through eCare within 12 months and meet demographic strata may be limited. The proposal describes these categories as "starting points, not fixed classifications" [1], which suggests flexibility, but the sampling targets are explicit.

The proposal anticipates the demographic challenge: "If underrepresented groups are disproportionately non-users, recruitment will prioritize these groups through informatics searches, direct clinic approaches, and same-day data collection" [1]. This contingency plan is sensible but depends on the same informatics infrastructure and clinic workflows already supporting the primary recruitment effort. The Harborview channel for Aim 3 non-users is particularly important for reaching underrepresented groups, but recruiting 42 non-users from a single clinic within approximately 12 months (Q5–Q8) with demographic stratification warrants close attention during project monitoring.

### Institutional and team resources

The institutional setting provides real strengths for delivery. UW Medicine's Epic-based eCare infrastructure and clinical and translational informatics services support participant identification and EHR compatibility review. Ten advisors across the board, prototype consultants, and mentoring group, eight of whom are clinicians [1], supply clinical and technical oversight at key points.

The lean execution team—Korngiebel and a graduate assistant handling prototype revision and usability analysis, with Korngiebel and Robins leading qualitative analysis supported by two coders [1]—is sufficient for medium-fidelity prototypes appropriate to cognitive walkthroughs. Professional prototype design support is not separately budgeted; this is a manageable constraint for an R21 producing formative prototypes, but it concentrates the technical production burden on a small team during the already-dense Q5–Q8 period. The proposal does not specify the graduate assistant's design or prototyping skills, which are critical to the Aim 3 timeline.

## Ethics and safety

**The proposal identifies the relevant potential risks to participants and describes proportionate management measures. The study is minimal-risk research of an interview-and-prototype nature, and the ethical safeguards are appropriate for the planned activities. No material ethical concerns are raised by the design.**

### Risk identification and mitigation

The human-subjects plan is clearly described and addresses the specific vulnerabilities of research that asks patients to discuss their experiences receiving genetic test results. The principal risks identified are: discomfort discussing inherited risk and family implications, concern about audio recording, possible recognition by others during waiting-room recruitment at clinical sites, and inadvertent disclosure of identifying information about relatives [1].

Each risk is matched with a concrete mitigation. Participants will be informed during consent discussions about procedures, confidentiality, the voluntary nature of participation, the absence of direct clinical benefit from the research, and their right to stop at any time [1]. They will be reminded not to provide identifying information about third parties, and any such details inadvertently supplied will be removed from recordings and transcripts [1]. Participants experiencing distress will be encouraged to discontinue and directed to assistance if needed.

### Data management and regulatory provisions

The data management provisions are appropriate for the sensitivity of the material. Transcripts will be de-identified, and information needed for incentive administration will be stored separately from study data. Study data will be kept on secure servers or password-protected computers, with physical materials locked and access restricted to the research team. Data will be destroyed at study close [1]. These measures are consistent with standard practice for qualitative health research involving protected health information.

The plan to seek a HIPAA waiver for access to information used in identifying eligible patients and an IRB waiver of written consent documentation is reasonable [1]. Obtaining written consent for an interview study would create a link between participant identity and study records that the waiver of documentation avoids, reducing rather than increasing privacy risk. The $50 incentive for an approximately one-hour session is within the normal range for this type of research and is unlikely to constitute undue inducement for the target population.

### Scope constraints and equity considerations

The scope of the research is appropriately constrained to minimize burden and avoid generating data beyond the study's purpose. No children will be enrolled, and no incidental clinical findings are expected from an interview and prototype study that does not involve biological sample collection, new clinical testing, or access to medical records beyond the information needed for participant identification [1]. The restriction to English-speaking participants is a limitation with respect to generalizability, but it is explicitly acknowledged and justified by the current language availability of the eCare system at UW Medicine [1]; the proposal states that later work is intended to develop reports with and for non-English-speaking patients, which is an appropriate developmental sequence.

The stratified recruitment plan, with explicit minimum targets for underrepresented groups and a dedicated recruitment channel through a safety-net clinic, addresses distributive justice in research participation rather than merely in the eventual clinical application [1]. The evolving Points to Consider framework explicitly includes justice and equity as one of its three organizing domains, with questions addressing technology literacy, health literacy, additional support needs, and resource allocation [1], ensuring that equity considerations are integrated into the ethical analysis rather than treated as an afterthought.

Taken together, the ethics and safety provisions are well-considered and proportionate to the activities and the participant population. The combination of procedural safeguards (waivers, de-identification, separate storage), participant protections (informed consent process, right to withdraw, distress protocols), and substantive ethical commitments (equity-focused sampling, justice domain in the framework) addresses the material risks while aligning with the proposal's broader normative aims.

## Management

**The core team covers the essential expertise, and the advisory structure is unusually well-developed, but the project relies on a lean day-to-day execution capacity that becomes a material risk during the compressed prototype-and-testing phase. The schedule is logically phased and the deliverables are appropriate, though a documented contingency for cascading delays is absent.**

### Team composition and expertise coverage

The investigative team brings together the four domains needed for this work. Korngiebel has training in formative research, electronic prototyping, and usability testing; Robins contributes extensive qualitative-methods experience; Fullerton provides genomic bioethics expertise; and Amendola adds genetic counseling and clinical-sequencing experience [1]. Korngiebel's prior work interviewing clinicians about returning Lynch syndrome screening results through a portal supplies direct preliminary experience with the research question [1], and the proposal builds logically from that finding. The team composition matches the stated aims: qualitative data collection and analysis for Aims 1 and 2, prototype development and iterative usability testing for Aim 3, and ethical framework refinement throughout.

### Advisory and oversight structure

The advisory and oversight structure provides substantial external quality assurance. The advisory board comprises five members spanning clinical care (Chen, Harborview Family Medicine), ambulatory IT (Fellner), medical genetics (Jarvik), laboratory medicine informatics (Shirts), and external biomedical informatics (Peterson, Vanderbilt). Two external electronic prototype consultants—Welch (MUSC) and Williams (Geisinger)—bring experience from other institutions developing patient-facing genomic tools. A three-person mentoring committee adds bioethics (Burke), biomedical informatics (Tarczy-Hornoch), and medical education (Carline) oversight [1]. Across all groups, ten advisors are available, eight of whom are clinicians [1].

The board meets twice yearly with consultation as needed, and the external consultants conduct heuristic walkthroughs between usability rounds 2 and 3 [1]. This creates defined quality checkpoints at critical junctures: after generative research informs initial prototypes, and before the final usability round. The institutional infrastructure—UW Medicine's eCare, clinical and translational informatics services, and Harborview's Family Medicine Clinic—provides the necessary recruitment channels and EHR compatibility review [1].

### Schedule and deliverables

The schedule is logically phased. Aim 1 interviews and analysis occupy Q1–Q3, Aim 2 interviews and analysis Q3–Q5, and Aim 3 prototype creation with three testing rounds Q5–Q8 [1]. The deliverables are appropriate for the two-year R21 scope: downloadable prototypes, the Points to Consider framework, and publications describing patient preferences and portal use [1]. These are framed as foundations for subsequent work—clinician co-design, multilingual report sets, multi-site implementation, and health-outcome studies—rather than overclaimed within the grant period.

### Day-to-day execution capacity as the principal risk

The principal management risk is the lean day-to-day execution team. Qualitative analysis is led by Korngiebel and Robins with two coders [1]; prototype revision and usability analysis fall to Korngiebel and a graduate assistant [1]. For a project involving up to 164 participants (up to 180 including pilots) [1] across three aims with intensive interview and usability methods, this is a limited force.

The two-coder approach is methodologically sound, but the compressed schedule means both coders must complete analysis within tight windows, particularly during the Q3 Aim 1 analysis/Aim 2 interview overlap. The graduate assistant's role in prototype revision is critical to the Aim 3 timeline, yet the proposal does not specify the assistant's design or prototyping skills, nor is professional prototype design support separately budgeted. This is feasible for medium-fidelity prototypes suitable for cognitive walkthroughs, but it concentrates the technical production burden on a small team during the already-dense Q5–Q8 period.

A delay in Aim 1 recruitment would cascade through this lean structure without documented contingency for schedule slippage—for example, whether round 3 could be abbreviated, whether lower-priority prototypes could be deferred, or whether prototype creation could begin in parallel with Aim 2 analysis using preliminary findings. The advisory board's twice-yearly meetings provide oversight cadence but do not supply surge capacity for day-to-day execution. These are manageable constraints in a well-functioning team, but they warrant close monitoring of early recruitment milestones and the Q5–Q6 prototype-and-testing window.

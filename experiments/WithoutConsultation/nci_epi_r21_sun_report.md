# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project aims to develop a novel statistical framework to estimate the immune-cell composition of bulk tumors by jointly modeling gene expression and DNA methylation data. The planned work includes creating likelihood-based deconvolution methods that can adapt blood-derived reference profiles to the tumor microenvironment, quantify estimation uncertainty, and propagate this uncertainty into prognostic survival models. The most valuable expected knowledge is a validated, open-source computational tool and a comprehensive analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), which together would significantly advance the ability to study the tumor immune microenvironment in large, existing epidemiological cohorts.

The proposal’s main strengths lie in its clear methodological innovation—specifically, the synergy between expression and methylation to address transcriptional activity differences and the principled joint modeling of composition and survival—and a well-matched team with computational and domain-specific expertise. The analysis plan is logically sequenced and leverages publicly available data, eliminating data-access barriers. The primary limitations are technical and inherent to the exploratory stage: the log-normal and normal distributional approximations for mixed signals may be coarse, the identifiability of cell-size/transcriptional-activity scaling factors is stated but not fully resolved, and the deconvolution of up to 28 cell types with reference data is ambitious. Additionally, the validation against a true gold standard is constrained, as is the case for the entire field.

While the project’s methodological ambitions carry identifiable statistical risks concerning distributional approximations and identifiability, these are characteristic of high-gain exploratory work and are appropriately scoped within the R21 mechanism. The planned validation framework—despite intrinsic limitations of ground truth in tumor deconvolution—provides a reasonable multipronged approach to gauge performance. The substantial potential to create a new, principled tool for integrative TIME analysis and the clarity of the computational workplan outweigh the remaining technical uncertainties, making the current proposal supportable.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

The question the proposal addresses is both scientifically important and translationally relevant: how to recover the immune-cell composition of a bulk tumor sample from widely available molecular data, and how to use that composition for prognostically useful models without ignoring the uncertainty inherent in its estimation [1]. The attainable contributions—an open-source statistical framework that jointly models gene expression and DNA methylation, accompanied by a systematically characterized pan-cancer TCGA resource—would fill a genuine gap in the cancer genomics toolbox. The expected products are likelihood-based estimation methods, a software implementation, and empirical analyses that can inform future epidemiological and translational studies of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) [1].

The proposal’s long‑term goal is to create TIME biomarkers for defining tumor subtypes and predicting immunotherapy response [1]. The present exploratory project appropriately limits its scope to composition estimation and prognostic survival signatures, making the work tractable within an R21 mechanism while laying the foundation for those larger ambitions [1]. The immediate value is methodological: the field currently lacks a principled approach that combines expression and methylation in a single deconvolution model, re‑estimates cell‑type molecular profiles to accommodate blood‑reference versus tumor‑microenvironment differences for both modalities, and propagates deconvolution uncertainty into downstream survival analysis [1].

**The practical value is amplified by the choice of input data.** Gene expression and DNA methylation arrays are the two most abundant omics modalities in epidemiological and clinical cohorts, yet they are rarely analyzed together for immune deconvolution. A method that can operate on either or both modalities—and, once trained, deconvolve new samples from expression alone, methylation alone, or both [1]—would make TIME analysis accessible to a much larger set of existing studies than is currently possible. The planned pan‑cancer analysis across TCGA, beginning with colon and lung cancer and extending across cancer types [1], would provide a systematically generated resource that other investigators could use to calibrate immune‑based subtyping hypotheses.

Two aspects of the attainable knowledge are worth noting. First, the proposal explicitly aims to estimate multiplicative cell‑size and transcriptional‑activity factors by comparing RNA‑derived and methylation‑derived composition estimates [1]. If successful, this would address a well‑documented bias in expression‑based deconvolution: bulk RNA proportions reflect transcript abundance rather than cell number, a discrepancy that can be substantial for cell types such as neutrophils [2]. This is a scientifically valuable conceptual advance in its own right, beyond any prognostic application. Second, the joint survival‑omics model in Aim 2 treats cell fractions as latent variables shared between the omics and survival components, avoiding the well‑known attenuation bias that occurs when estimated fractions are naively plugged into a Cox regression as fixed covariates [1]. This principled treatment of measurement error in a generated regressor is a sound statistical contribution, even if the incremental improvement in the cross‑validated concordance index proves modest in absolute terms.

The value is appropriately judged against the exploratory R21 stage. The work will not deliver a clinically validated biomarker or a definitive comparison across dozens of cancer types; it will establish whether the proposed joint‑modeling framework is feasible, how it performs relative to existing methods under controlled and semi‑realistic benchmarks, and what further development is warranted. That is a legitimate and useful scientific contribution. The principal limitation is that the framework’s practical utility depends on how much the combined expression‑methylation signal improves deconvolution accuracy over single‑modality methods—a question that only the proposed work can answer.

## Innovation

The proposal adds three genuinely novel elements to the deconvolution literature, though each builds on identifiable precedents whose relationship to the planned work the proposal partially acknowledges. The most distinctive contribution is the joint expression–methylation likelihood framework, for which no existing method provides a direct parallel. Two other advances—re-estimation of methylation reference profiles from bulk tumor data and propagation of deconvolution uncertainty through a shared-parameter survival model—fill gaps that single-modality methods have not addressed, even though related capabilities exist on the expression side.

**Joint expression–methylation deconvolution represents the clearest gap in published methods.** Current deconvolution tools operate on single molecular modalities: CIBERSORTx and BayesPrism use gene expression; MethylCIBERSORT uses methylation beta values with a fixed blood-derived reference; EPIC-ATAC addresses chromatin accessibility [3]. The comprehensive review by Ferro Dos Santos et al. covering 25 methylation deconvolution methods confirms that none combines expression and methylation in a unified framework [4]. MOADE processes both modalities jointly but uses an autoencoder architecture rather than an explicit likelihood model, representing a fundamentally different approach [5]. The proposal’s strategy—linking the two modalities through shared latent cell fractions, using methylation to anchor cell-number proportions while expression captures transcriptional activity, and exploiting cross-modality discrepancies to infer cell-size scaling factors [1]—is methodologically original. Once cell-type profiles are estimated from training data containing both modalities, the framework can deconvolve new samples from either modality alone [1], a practical advance with no current equivalent.

**Re-estimation of cell-type methylation profiles from bulk tumor data is a novel capability on the methylation side.** For expression, both CIBERSORTx and BayesPrism already re-estimate cell-type profiles to accommodate reference–tissue mismatches: CIBERSORTx solves for sample-specific expression profiles via non-negative least squares given estimated fractions [6], while BayesPrism jointly infers the posterior distribution of both fractions and cell-type-specific gene expression, explicitly marginalizing out differences between single-cell references and bulk data [7]. On the methylation side, however, existing methods—MethylCIBERSORT, HEpiDISH, EpiDISH variants, and reference-free approaches such as Tsisal/TOAST—all use fixed references or operate without references entirely [8] [9] [10]. The proposal’s coordinate-ascent algorithm that alternates between estimating fractions and re-estimating both expression and methylation profiles [1] extends the profile-refinement concept to methylation for the first time.

**The joint survival–omics model treats cell fractions as latent variables shared across the omics and survival likelihoods, avoiding the generated-regressor bias inherent in two-stage approaches** [1]. BayesPrism provides posterior distributions over fractions [7] but leaves downstream modeling to the user; no existing deconvolution tool embeds the uncertainty directly into a survival model. The compositional log-ratio formulation with the zero-sum constraint [1] and the fused-lasso penalty to stabilize coefficients for molecularly similar cell types [1] represent additional, albeit more incremental, innovations within the survival-analysis component.

| Capability | Existing precedent | What the proposal adds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Re-estimate expression profiles from bulk tumor data | CIBERSORTx [6], BayesPrism [7] | Extends to methylation through joint likelihood |
| Methylation deconvolution | MethylCIBERSORT [8], HEpiDISH, Tsisal/TOAST [9] | Iterative profile refinement from tumor data |
| Joint expression–methylation framework | MOADE (autoencoder-based) [5] | Explicit likelihood model with moment-matching |
| Uncertainty propagation to survival | BayesPrism (posterior over fractions only) [7] | Shared-parameter joint model embedding deconvolution in survival inference |

The overall innovation rests on a genuine methodological advance whose individual components have identifiable precedents but whose integration—particularly the joint expression–methylation likelihood with cross-modality calibration of transcriptional activity—constitutes a non-incremental step beyond current tools. The distinction is that the proposal does not merely combine existing deconvolution methods for two modalities; it constructs a new estimation framework whose architecture is driven by the biological relationship between the two data types, with the explicit goal of separating cell-number from transcript-abundance contributions. The exploratory R21 stage is appropriate for establishing whether this integration yields sufficient practical gains over single-modality methods to warrant further development.

## Rigor

The research design is methodologically sound for an exploratory R21 grant, combining explicit model specification with a multi-pronged validation strategy that is proportionate to the stage of development. The principal strengths are the clear formulation of the likelihood framework, the logical sequencing of aims, and the appropriate use of prediction-based evaluation. The design's limitations—distributional approximations whose adequacy has not been fully demonstrated under realistic departures, unresolved identifiability conditions for cell-size factors with many cell types, and the inherent absence of ground truth for tumor deconvolution—are characteristic of the field rather than specific oversights, and the proposal acknowledges the most consequential of them.

**The likelihood framework for Aim 1 is laid out with unusual clarity for a proposal at this stage.** The model's four information sources—tumor expression, tumor methylation, purified-cell expression, and purified-cell methylation [1]—are combined in a joint likelihood whose components are explicitly specified. The coordinate-ascent algorithm that initializes reference-cell parameters from purified-cell data and then alternates between estimating fractions given molecular profiles and re-estimating profiles given fractions [1] is a natural computational response to the high-dimensional latent-variable structure. The preliminary work provides initial calibration: simulated mixtures of five log-normal distributions yielded more accurate fraction estimates under a log-normal generalized linear model than under normal linear regression [1], and a methylation known-mixture experiment with five cell types showed maximum-likelihood residual sum of squares of 0.029 versus 0.043 for nonnegative least squares [1]. These results are encouraging but test the method under its own assumptions—a limitation the proposal partially addresses through the planned validation strategy [1].

**The validation design for Aim 1 is appropriately layered**, combining internal consistency checks, orthogonal technology benchmarks, and comparisons with established methods. Simulated mixtures constructed from measured cell-specific expression and methylation will test whether the algorithm recovers known mixing proportions under controlled conditions [1]. Flow-cytometry benchmarks provide an independent measurement technology that is not subject to the same modeling assumptions as molecular deconvolution. Published measurements of immune cells isolated from tumor tissue [1] address the central concern that blood-derived references may differ from tumor-infiltrating cell profiles, providing a direct test of whether the iterative coordinate-ascent correction moves the references toward genuine tumor-context values. This triplet of validation approaches is well-judged: no single component is definitive, but together they address complementary aspects of accuracy. The field's own reviews confirm that creating a gold-standard dataset for tumor deconvolution remains an unresolved challenge [11] [12], and the proposal's strategy reflects the current state of the art in what can feasibly be achieved [11].

**Aim 2's evaluation framework is correctly oriented toward prediction rather than hypothesis testing**, with cross-validated C-index as the primary endpoint [1]. Comparing the joint model against both the conventional two-stage approach and against single-modality variants [1] directly tests whether propagating deconvolution uncertainty and combining modalities improves prognostic performance. The exploration of alternative datasets beyond TCGA [1] is essential because TCGA survival data are often heavily censored and may not provide sufficient events to identify the joint model's parameters. The compositional log-ratio formulation with the zero-sum constraint [1] follows established practice in compositional data analysis [13] [14]. The fused-lasso penalty [1] is a creative regularization device whose empirical value will be assessed against simpler alternatives within the cross-validation framework.

**Two interrelated statistical concerns affect the strength of inference that the current design can support**, though neither is disqualifying for an exploratory grant. First, the log-normal approximation for the sum of log-normal expression components via moment-matching [1] is a pragmatic choice whose adequacy depends on the variance heterogeneity across cell types in real tumors. The proposal's preliminary validation used simulated mixtures that satisfied the log-normal assumption [1]; the planned validation under realistic departures and with orthogonal benchmarks will need to characterize when the approximation breaks down. Methods that model both mean and variance of cell-type-specific expression typically limit themselves to two or three components to maintain identifiability [11]; the proposal's ambition to handle 10–28 cell types [1] raises the stakes for this approximation. The methylation normal mixture is less concerning, given the exact linearity of the mixture moments [1] and supporting preliminary evidence [1].

Second, the cell-size/transcriptional-activity factor estimation—one of the proposal's most innovative contributions—is specified only in conceptual terms [1]. The condition that the number of tumor samples exceed the number of cell types is necessary but not sufficient; the proposal does not fully specify how the scaling factors enter the expression likelihood relative to the methylation-identified fractions, what normalization identifies the scale, or whether the factors can be recovered under realistic noise. The CSsingle method provides a practical precedent that such correction is feasible [15], but the proposal's likelihood-based approach introduces a higher identifiability bar that warrants more detailed treatment in the work plan.

The TCGA limitation—that sampling criteria may underrepresent highly immune-infiltrated tumors [1]—is appropriately acknowledged and would affect the generalizability of composition patterns rather than the validity of the method itself. The overall design provides a coherent path from model development through internal and external validation to prognostic evaluation, with the appropriate caveats about what each validation component can and cannot establish. For an exploratory R21, this represents a well-structured research plan whose inferential limitations are inherent to the problem rather than to the design.

## Feasibility

The project is feasible within an R21 timeline and resource envelope, primarily because it is a fully computational methods-development effort built on publicly available, well-characterized data and supported by a team with directly relevant expertise. The principal uncertainties are methodological rather than logistical—the adequacy of the log-normal mixture approximation, the identifiability of cell-size factors with many cell types, and the convergence behavior of the coordinate-ascent algorithm—and these are appropriate risks for an exploratory grant whose purpose is to establish proof of concept.

**All required input data are already in hand and publicly accessible.** The expression reference data comprise more than 800 purified-cell samples covering 28 cell types from published datasets [1]; the methylation references combine Illumina 450k data from six studies [1]; and the primary application data—TCGA expression, methylation, and survival for multiple cancer types—are openly available with no new data-generation or access-negotiation dependencies [1]. The bulk of the work is computational model fitting and validation, not data acquisition. The one external dependency is pre-estimated tumor purity from copy-number data [1], a routine input that the investigators' prior work has already addressed.

**The team possesses the requisite technical expertise.** Wei Sun contributes statistical genomics, copy-number, expression analysis, and software development experience; Michael Wu contributes methylation and association-method expertise; and the clinical collaborators, Peters and Houghton, provide domain guidance for colorectal- and lung-cancer applications respectively [1]. The institution provides the statistical and computing resources needed for TCGA-scale analysis [1]. The commitment to open-source R packages with computationally intensive components in C or C++ and Rcpp integration [1] reflects established practice in the investigators' field and ensures that the software deliverables are technically achievable.

**The two-year timeline—Aim 1 in year one, Aim 2 in year two** [1]—is ambitious but credible for a computational methods grant. The heavy conceptual and implementation work is concentrated in Aim 1 (likelihood specification, coordinate-ascent estimation, feature-selection robustness, validation against simulated mixtures, flow cytometry, and existing methods). Aim 2 extends the framework to survival outcomes by adding a survival likelihood component, implementing the compositional log-ratio and fused-lasso penalties, and conducting cross-validated prognostic evaluation. Because Aim 2 builds directly on Aim 1’s estimated parameters—the omics likelihood and the latent fraction structure remain the same—the sequential structure is efficient. The risk is that delays in Aim 1 (e.g., convergence difficulties with many cell types, or unexpected poor performance of the log-normal approximation under realistic scenarios) would compress Aim 2’s evaluation; this is a manageable risk for an R21 where the primary deliverable is the framework itself rather than a fully optimized survival signature.

**The feasibility risks are methodological rather than logistical, and they are correctly scoped.** The three principal risks are: (a) the Fenton–Wilkinson log-normal approximation may introduce unacceptable bias when multiple cell types with heterogeneous transcriptional activity are present at non-trivial fractions; (b) the cell-size/transcriptional-activity factors may not be identifiable from the planned sample sizes without stronger prior constraints than the proposal currently specifies [1]; and (c) the coordinate-ascent algorithm may converge slowly or to local optima when Q approaches 28 cell types, given the high-dimensional latent space [1]. Each of these risks is addressable within the grant period through simulation studies and progressive refinement: the investigators can systematically evaluate the approximation’s adequacy under varying numbers of cell types and variance heterogeneity, can implement sensitivity analyses with different identifiability constraints on the scaling factors, and can adopt algorithmic safeguards (multiple random restarts, tempered updates) if convergence proves problematic. The proposal’s validation strategy—simulated mixtures for internal consistency, flow-cytometry benchmarks for orthogonal measurement agreement, and TIME-isolated cell profiles for tumor-context relevance [1]—provides the diagnostic tools needed to detect and characterize these failures should they occur.

The only feasibility concern that merits attention is the absence of a detailed fallback plan for the cell-size factor estimation. The proposal identifies this as a contribution contingent on the condition that the number of tumor samples exceeds the number of cell types [1], but it does not specify what normalization or reference constraint will identify the scale, nor how the scaling factors will enter the expression likelihood relative to the methylation-identified fractions. This is a specification gap rather than an infeasibility: the CSsingle method’s three-stage computational strategy [15] and the known empirical magnitude of the mRNA-content bias [2] demonstrate that the problem is tractable. Addressing this gap early in Aim 1—through simulation studies that vary the magnitude of transcriptional-activity differences and assess factor recovery—would be a prudent use of the exploratory period. The overall feasibility is strong for an R21: the data, team, timeline, and computational resources are adequate, and the methodological risks are inherent to the innovative aims rather than indicative of an inadequately specified plan.

## Ethics and safety

The project meets the highest standard for data ethics and participant protection in a secondary-analysis study, and no material safety risks arise from the proposed computational work. The study uses only existing, publicly available, de-identified omics and clinical datasets; there is no new recruitment, no direct participant contact, and no collection of identifying information [1]. The plan is ethically proportionate, appropriately managed, and consistent with the applicable regulatory classification.

The proposal states that the data sources include TCGA, ENCODE, and published immune-cell reference datasets, all of which are de-identified and publicly accessible [1]. The investigators confirm that the study qualifies for Exemption 4 under their institutional review framework and that they will not seek identifying information [1]. This classification is standard for secondary analyses of de-identified public data and is correctly applied here. The data storage arrangements—designated secured machines accessible only to project personnel [1]—provide routine but adequate protection against unauthorized access. No broader community consultation, consent amendments, or benefit-sharing arrangements are required because the work does not interact with original participants or their communities, and the proposal’s explicit recognition that no direct benefit to those participants is anticipated [1] is an honest statement of the study’s scope rather than an ethical shortcoming.

The residual re-identification risk from TCGA and similar genomic datasets has been discussed extensively in the bioethics literature, but it is negligible in this context for two reasons. First, the proposal works with bulk-tumor molecular profiles and derived cell-fraction estimates, not individual-level germline variants or other identifying features. Second, the investigators’ institutional commitment not to seek identifying information and to operate within the existing data-use agreements of the source repositories provides a further safeguard. No environmental, laboratory, animal, or community-based safety considerations apply. The work is entirely computational and does not involve hazardous materials, field operations, or interventions.

The single consideration worth noting—not as a shortcoming but as a boundary condition—is that the eventual translational use of TIME biomarkers derived from this work (for example, in immunotherapy response prediction or tumor subtyping) would require separate ethical review in the context of any prospective clinical application. That future contingency lies outside the scope of the proposed exploratory methods development and does not affect the ethical assessment of the current plan. The present study is a fully computational secondary analysis that meets the applicable standards for data ethics and privacy; no safety concerns of any material kind have been identified.

## Management

The management plan is clearly structured, appropriately resourced, and supported by a team whose expertise is directly matched to the proposed tasks. The two-year timeline is ambitious but credible for a fully computational methods-development project; the principal management risk is the tight coupling between aims, which would require prompt resolution of Aim 1’s methodological challenges to preserve sufficient time for Aim 2’s evaluation.

**The division of responsibilities is logical and draws on complementary strengths.** Wei Sun leads the statistical genomics and expression-modeling components, drawing on established expertise in copy-number analysis and software development; Michael Wu contributes methylation and association-method expertise; and the clinical collaborators, Ulrike Peters and McGarry Houghton, provide domain-specific guidance for colorectal- and lung-cancer applications respectively [1]. This configuration covers the three essential competencies—statistical method development, methylation data analysis, and cancer-biology context—without unnecessary duplication. The institution supplies the statistical and computing infrastructure required for TCGA-scale analysis [1], and the heavy computational components will be implemented in C or C++ with Rcpp integration, a standard and well-supported development pathway [1].

**The schedule allocates Aim 1 to year 1 and Aim 2 to year 2** [1], a sequential arrangement that reflects the logical dependency: the survival model in Aim 2 builds on the deconvolution framework and latent-fraction structure estimated in Aim 1, so any instability or delay in the foundation propagates directly to the later aim. This coupling is inherent to the project design and is appropriately managed through the exploratory scope—the grant aims to establish feasibility, not to deliver an optimized clinical tool. The publication and open-source software commitments [1] are explicit and well-integrated into the timeline, ensuring that the deliverables are defined from the outset rather than treated as afterthoughts.

The management plan operates at the level of detail appropriate to a computational R21. No itemized budget, personnel effort allocation, or formal project-governance structure is described in the supplied materials, but these administrative elements are not required for a scientific evaluation of whether the stated expertise, schedule, and resources can deliver the planned work. The absence of named postdoctoral or student personnel is not a deficiency: the PIs’ direct involvement in method development and implementation is a common and credible arrangement for statistical grants of this scale. Should the work reveal an unexpected need for additional programming support, the institutional computing environment and the use of standard languages (R, C/C++) would facilitate timely recruitment.

The single management consideration worth raising is the timeline’s sensitivity to early-stage methodological hurdles. If the coordinate-ascent algorithm encounters convergence difficulties with the planned 10–28 cell types, or if the log-normal moment-matching approximation requires substantial refinement after validation against realistic benchmarks, the year-1 window could be compressed. The proposal does not describe a formal contingency—such as a reduced cell-type panel for the initial release or a staged validation sequence—but this is a minor planning gap rather than a threat to feasibility, because the investigators can make these adjustments within the exploratory framework without external dependencies. The two-year structure, the directly relevant expertise, and the absence of data-access or equipment bottlenecks together make the management plan sufficient for the stated aims.

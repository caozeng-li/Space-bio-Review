# Project review

## Overall assessment

S-MODE proposes a coordinated airborne and in situ investigation of the hypothesis that submesoscale (0.2–25 km) ocean dynamics make important contributions to vertical exchange of heat, carbon, nutrients, and oxygen in the upper ocean. The campaign will simultaneously deploy three aircraft (carrying the DopplerScatt surface-current radar, the PRISM hyperspectral ocean-color imager, and the MASS lidar-wave-SST package), a research vessel, Wave Gliders, Seagliders, Lagrangian floats, and approximately 100 surface drifters in a nested survey pattern offshore of San Francisco. Two 25-day intensive operations periods in contrasting spring and fall conditions will target kilometer-scale fronts and eddies identified in near-real time from the airborne measurements. The project’s most valuable expected contribution is a benchmark observational dataset that, for the first time, co-locates synoptic maps of surface currents, temperature, ocean color, and directional wave spectra with direct vertical-velocity measurements and subsurface surveys of individual submesoscale features across two seasons [1]. This dataset is expressly designed to test high-resolution model predictions, improve interpretation of satellite measurements, and advance understanding of the mechanisms by which submesoscale circulation ventilates the upper ocean.

The proposal is exceptionally well-supported by demonstrated instrument capabilities, a mature team, and a carefully reasoned experimental design. All major instruments are at TRL 6 or above and have been flown in the proposed aircraft configurations (DopplerScatt in the Gulf of Mexico, PRISM on the ER-2, MASS in 16 prior campaigns) [1]. The nested sampling strategy—DopplerScatt daily maps over approximately 75 × 300 km guiding ship, glider, and float operations to active fronts—is a logical and well-articulated integration of remote sensing and in situ methods. Multiple complementary vertical-velocity estimates (Lagrangian floats, Wave Glider divergence array, Omega equation) provide cross-validation for the central dynamical question. The proposal also explicitly identifies a threshold mission (one 14-day IOP and a pilot) that delivers the core structural description and surface-current relationship (Goals 1 and 3), providing a credible descope option [1].

The principal material limitations are structural: (a) the limited number of frontal realizations (approximately three in the threshold, roughly six in the baseline) constrains statistical characterization of the surface-to-depth current relationship across wind, wave, and stratification conditions, and limits the power of the air-sea interaction tests in Goal 2; (b) the Goal 4 vertical-flux proxy W\_D = D × h must be calibrated from the same in situ data it is intended to extend, so it cannot provide fully independent flux quantification [1]; and (c) the separation of submesoscale circulation from internal waves, while addressed through sustained sampling and a control-float strategy, is not guaranteed and represents a recognized scientific risk [1]. These limitations are openly discussed in the proposal and are mitigated by the concurrent high-resolution modeling framework [1].

Taken together, the exceptional scientific ambition and the unique multi-platform design support a strong overall judgement. The proposal targets a defining problem in physical oceanography—how submesoscale fronts move heat, carbon, and nutrients between the surface and the interior—with a measurement strategy that no prior campaign has achieved. The main limitations are inherent to the problem: three or even six frontal realizations cannot fully sample the environmental parameter space, the Goal 4 flux proxy cannot independently quantify flux, and the separation of submesoscale circulation from internal waves remains a fundamental observational challenge. The proposal acknowledges these limitations, frames its most ambitious goals as exploratory, and provides a modeling framework to extend the observational findings. The strengths of the threshold mission justify support; the baseline mission enables the richer seasonal and process comparisons that give the project its full scientific value.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**The scientific value of S-MODE is excellent (A).** The project addresses a first-order question in physical oceanography—whether submesoscale fronts and eddies make important contributions to vertical exchange of heat, carbon, nutrients, and oxygen between the upper ocean and the interior—with a measurement strategy that has no observational precedent. The central contribution will be a benchmark dataset that simultaneously combines airborne DopplerScatt surface current mapping, PRISM hyperspectral ocean color, MOSES/MASS sea surface temperature (SST) and directional wave spectra, Lagrangian float direct vertical velocity measurements, Wave Glider horizontal divergence profiles, and ship/glider subsurface surveys, all targeting the same submesoscale features across two contrasting seasons.

**The question is well-posed and consequential.** Submesoscale motions connect nearly two-dimensional mesoscale flow to three-dimensional turbulence and can produce substantial buoyancy and biogeochemical fluxes, yet their contribution to net vertical exchange remains uncertain because numerical models are sensitive to resolution and parameterization [1]. The proposal demonstrates that two widely used models of the California Current—ROMS at 500 m spacing and MITgcm at approximately 2 km spacing—disagree in their relative prominence of eddies and fronts and in the seasonal contrast between March and September [1]. This disagreement motivates observations that can test the structure, evolution, and exchange processes directly.

### The descriptive contribution

The descriptive contribution—documenting the three-dimensional temperature, salinity, velocity, vorticity, divergence, and optical structure of submesoscale features and their mesoscale setting—is achievable and valuable in its own right. DopplerScatt provides synoptic surface current maps at approximately 1 km effective resolution over roughly 50 × 100 km daily, a capability that did not exist in the earlier LatMix, OSMOSIS, or D'Asaro et al. (2018) campaigns [1]. The simultaneous remote sensing of SST (0.01 K precision), ocean color (30 m resolution), and directional wave spectra co-located with current maps means that the physical structure of fronts can be compared directly with their biological and air-sea interaction signatures at submesoscale resolution for the first time.

The two-season design provides direct leverage on the seasonal modulation of submesoscale activity, about which models disagree. April and September provide contrasting mixed-layer depths and atmospheric conditions [1]. Callies et al. (2015) established that submesoscale energy is much stronger in winter than summer [2], and S-MODE can test whether the relationship between surface signatures and vertical exchange changes seasonally—a finding that would directly constrain model parameterizations.

### The process understanding contribution

The process understanding contribution is more conditional but scientifically ambitious. Goal 1 tests whether the observed three-dimensional structure matches the predictions of frontogenesis and mixed-layer instability theories, using the combination of surface divergence/vorticity from DopplerScatt with direct vertical velocity from Lagrangian floats and the Wave Glider divergence array [1]. The prior LatMix experiment confirmed that the asymmetric vorticity distribution observed in winter mixed layers—sparse strands of strong cyclonic vorticity embedded in a weak anticyclonic background—matches high-resolution numerical model predictions [3], but that study could not simultaneously measure vertical velocity, biogeochemical properties, and air-sea fluxes at the same features.

Goal 2 tests specific air-sea interaction mechanisms—current- and SST-induced stress modification, vorticity-modified Ekman pumping, and down-front versus up-front wind forcing—that have been studied in models but never simultaneously constrained by the full suite of relevant observations at these scales [1]. DopplerScatt's simultaneous wind-and-current mapping is unique among existing observing capabilities for this purpose. Wineteer et al. (2024) have now demonstrated, using S-MODE data, that DopplerScatt resolves wind divergence and vorticity at submesoscale wavelengths, revealing atmospheric vertical motions reaching approximately 10 km/day that are inconsistent with classical turbulence theories [4].

Goal 3 addresses the practical question of how DopplerScatt surface currents (top few centimeters) relate to currents throughout the boundary layer. At fronts, where “strong density and velocity gradients introduce less understood behavior,” the proposal acknowledges that models may be needed for interpretation [1]. The relationship between radar current measurements and currents within and beneath the surface boundary layer is essential for interpreting any satellite surface current measurement, and even a null result—showing the relationship is more complex than current models represent—would be scientifically valuable.

### The methodological contribution of Goal 4

The Goal 4 flux proxy W\_D = D × h, where D is surface horizontal divergence from DopplerScatt and h is a vertical length scale calibrated from in situ data [1], is appropriately framed as exploratory. Because h is calibrated from the same measurements it seeks to extend, the proxy cannot independently quantify vertical tracer flux. However, the validation strategy—combining in situ flux estimates, model simulations, and proxy-tracer covariance conditioned on vorticity and strain—can establish the conditions under which surface divergence contains information about vertical exchange.

Balwada et al. (2021) demonstrated, using idealized simulations, that conditioning vertical tracer flux on surface vorticity and strain effectively decomposes the flow into regions where strain-dominated submesoscale processes play an outsized role in ventilation [5]. The conditional mean of vertical velocity in their analysis closely resembled the conditional mean of surface divergence, suggesting that a kinematic proxy based on surface divergence can capture the spatial organization of vertical flux in a statistical, conditioned sense [5]. Whether this holds in real frontal environments with heterogeneous divergence profiles is precisely the question S-MODE can address. The proposal's cautious characterization of Goal 4 as “the most exploratory goal” and its acknowledgment that “complete flux quantification is difficult, but even partial progress would advance understanding of vertical transport” [1] is appropriate.

### Practical value and dissemination

The practical value of the dataset for model evaluation is credible. The proposed nested simulations at 2 km, 250 m, and 30 m resolution will be run concurrently, and the ensemble of observed features will be used to assess model physics [1]. The project's data products, including DopplerScatt Level-3 vorticity, divergence, and strain fields, PRISM chlorophyll and particulate organic carbon, and MASS directional wavenumber spectra, will be delivered to the NASA archive within six months of each campaign [1], supporting the broader NASA ocean, wind, biology, and satellite-validation communities [1]. The field site's overlap with a planned SWOT fast-repeat crossover region further extends the dataset's utility for satellite calibration and validation [1].

## Innovation

**The innovation of S-MODE is excellent (A).** The project will produce a dataset with no observational precedent: simultaneous, co-located airborne maps of surface currents, hyperspectral ocean color, infrared SST, and directional wave spectra at submesoscale resolution, nested with direct Lagrangian float vertical velocity measurements, Wave Glider divergence profiles, and ship/glider subsurface surveys, all targeting the same kilometer-scale fronts and eddies across two contrasting seasons. No prior field campaign has combined these measurement types to test submesoscale vertical exchange mechanisms, and the integration of DopplerScatt's synoptic current mapping with in situ vertical velocity measurements represents a qualitative advance over what earlier experiments achieved.

### What prior campaigns established and left unresolved

The LatMix experiment (2011–2012) provided the first in situ full velocity gradient tensor at O(1 km) scale using two-ship synchronous ADCP surveys in the North Atlantic, revealing the asymmetric vorticity distribution in winter mixed layers and the seasonal transition in submesoscale energy spectra from k⁻² in winter to k⁻³ in summer [3] [2]. However, LatMix had no airborne remote sensing, no direct vertical velocity measurements from Lagrangian floats, and no biogeochemical measurements.

The OSMOSIS experiment (2012–2013) deployed nine moorings and five Seagliders at the Porcupine Abyssal Plain for a full annual cycle, providing the first statistical description of submesoscale turbulence properties across seasons. Yet OSMOSIS was fundamentally Eulerian—it could not target and follow individual evolving features—and had no airborne current mapping and no direct vertical velocity measurements.

The D'Asaro et al. (2018) Gulf of Mexico study demonstrated strong convergence of approximately 200 drifters into a 60 × 60 m region, with directly measured vertical velocities of 10⁻² m s⁻¹ at fronts—“much larger than the 10–100 m day⁻¹ typically inferred by indirect methods” [6]. This was a single short deployment with no airborne current mapping, no seasonal contrast, and no biogeochemical context.

### Distinctive novelties of the S-MODE design

Three aspects of S-MODE's design are distinctively novel. First, **the measurement combination itself**: DopplerScatt's simultaneous wind and current mapping at approximately 200 m posting [1], co-located with PRISM ocean color (30 m resolution, 350–1,050 nm spectral range) [1] and MASS directional wave spectra from hundreds of meters down to decimeter wavelengths [1], provides a four-way simultaneous view of submesoscale fronts—dynamics, biology, temperature, and waves—that has never been assembled. The MASS wave measurements at submesoscale fronts are particularly novel; Vrećica et al. (2021) demonstrated that wave breaking, whitecap coverage, and breaking-induced momentum flux are enhanced by an order of magnitude at a single submesoscale front, with the modulation occurring over just a few meters [7]. This was a single-front study near Hawaii; S-MODE will extend such observations to multiple frontal types and forcing conditions across two seasons.

Second, **the adaptive Lagrangian sampling strategy**: DopplerScatt daily maps over approximately 75 × 300 km will locate active fronts in near-real time, guiding ship, glider, float, and Wave Glider assets to track individual features for approximately three days each [1]. This adaptive targeting, proven feasible by the 2017 Gulf of Mexico DopplerScatt demonstration [1], addresses a fundamental limitation of prior Eulerian arrays and single-platform Lagrangian deployments, neither of which could capture the spatial context and evolution of individual features.

Third, **the systematic evaluation of a surface-divergence flux proxy** (Goal 4): while the proxy's calibration from in situ data limits its independent flux quantification, the nested validation strategy—comparing proxy-tracer covariance with direct flux estimates, model simulations, and mesoscale Omega-equation estimates [1]—represents the first systematic attempt to determine how much vertical flux information is encoded in surface kinematic measurements. The conditioned analysis approach demonstrated by Balwada et al. (2021), showing that strain-dominated regions play an outsized role in ventilation [5], provides a conceptual framework that the S-MODE observations can test with real data.

## Rigor

**The rigor of S-MODE is good (B).** The nested sampling design, with DopplerScatt guiding in situ assets to active features, is well matched to the inference goals for structural characterization (Goal 1), air-sea interaction testing (Goal 2), and surface-to-depth current intercomparison (Goal 3). Multiple complementary vertical velocity estimates—Lagrangian floats, Wave Glider divergence array, and Omega-equation estimates—provide cross-validation for the central dynamical question, and the use of both water-following (frontal) and isopycnal-following (background) Lagrangian floats [1] is a thoughtful internal-control strategy. The concurrent high-resolution modeling framework supplies a genuine bridge for interpreting discrepancies [1].

The overall design is proportionate to the project's stated goal: producing a benchmark dataset that can discriminate between model predictions and evaluate process hypotheses, rather than definitively quantifying the global importance of submesoscale exchange. Three structural limitations temper the rating.

### Limited number of frontal realizations

The threshold mission provides approximately three submesoscale feature surveys over 14 days; the baseline extends this to roughly six features across two seasons [1]. These numbers are insufficient to characterize the surface-to-depth current relationship (Goal 3) or the wind-front interaction mechanisms (Goal 2) as statistical functions of wind stress, wave properties, and stratification.

The difficulty is that three features from a single season cannot span the environmental conditions that the proposal itself identifies as controlling the surface-to-depth relationship: wind stress, wave properties, density stratification, and horizontal scale [1]. Submesoscale kinetic and potential energy exhibit strong seasonal cycles, being substantially larger in winter than summer [2]. Rascle et al. (2020) showed that submesoscale fronts can be only 50 m wide with convergence and cyclonic vorticity reaching 100f—values that “far exceed previously documented values” [8]. Zhu et al. (2024) found vertical velocities of 170 m/day at a single front, with heat transport three times larger than eddy-rich climate models predict [9]. These studies demonstrate that frontal current structure is highly variable from one feature to another, yet the proposal does not articulate a statistical framework for how many realizations would be needed to characterize the relationship at a specified confidence level.

For Goal 2 specifically, the down-front versus up-front wind forcing mechanism requires multiple realizations of different wind-front alignment conditions for comparable frontal structures. With approximately six feature realizations over two seasons, the statistical basis for testing this mechanism is thin. The proposal's analysis strategy is appropriately framed as hypothesis testing through comparison with theoretical predictions rather than statistical quantification [1], which is realistic for a first campaign at these scales.

The concurrent high-resolution models partially mitigate the limited-realization limitation: they can explore parameter space beyond what the observations sample, once validated against the observed features [1]. For Goal 3, the proposal explicitly acknowledges that at fronts “high-resolution simulations may be needed to interpret the discrepancies” [1]—both an honest acknowledgment of uncertainty and a partial admission that three feature surveys alone cannot yield a purely empirical characterization.

Published S-MODE results have confirmed the feasibility of the airborne–in situ comparison approach for Goal 3. Airborne optical current retrievals (DoppVis) were validated against Wave Glider ADCP measurements with bias of −0.014 m/s and RMS deviation of 0.052 m/s (R² = 0.96) [10]. This establishes the measurement technique even if it does not expand the number of realizations.

### The Goal 4 flux proxy cannot provide independent flux quantification

The flux proxy W\_D = D × h, where h is a vertical length scale “inferred from in situ vertical velocity and radar divergence, and separately assessed in numerical models” [1], faces a fundamental circularity. If h is determined by regressing float-measured vertical velocity w against co-located DopplerScatt divergence D, then within the calibration region W\_D = D × (w/D) = w by construction—the proxy exactly reproduces the in situ vertical velocity at calibration points. The spatial extrapolation adds value only if the D-to-w relationship captured by h is spatially robust across the mesoscale domain, a property that submesoscale front heterogeneity may violate.

The proposal's validation strategy—checking the proxy against model simulations where the full three-dimensional w field and the true flux are known [1], testing statistical stability and convergence [1], and comparing with mesoscale Omega-equation estimates—is well-suited to establishing the conditions under which surface divergence carries flux information. In the nested model simulations, one can separately compute the error from the h parameterization, the error from using surface rather than depth-dependent divergence, and the error from using surface rather than depth-dependent tracer concentrations. This decomposition would identify which term most limits the proxy and guide future efforts. The proposal's own characterization of Goal 4 as “the most exploratory goal” [1] is appropriately cautious, and the validation strategy is well-matched to making the partial progress it acknowledges is the realistic target.

### Separation of submesoscale circulation from internal waves

The proposal explicitly recognizes that submesoscale scales overlap with internal tides and inertial currents, and that “a snapshot of small-scale variability is therefore insufficient to attribute an observed vertical velocity to submesoscale circulation” [1]. Its multi-pronged separation strategy combines sustained repeated measurements over several weeks to separate process time scales [1], a control float deployed outside frontal features “following an isopycnal beneath the mixed layer” to provide context for internal-wave and tidal motions [1], and dynamical budget analysis via the momentum equations [1].

The threshold mission's three feature realizations are unlikely to sample sufficient tidal and inertial phases for clean statistical separation. A single isopycnal-following float outside the front provides only one realization of the background wave field at one location; internal tide amplitude and phase vary spatially, particularly in the California Current where topographic generation is inhomogeneous. Johnson et al. (2020), by the same team (D'Asaro, Shcherbina), observed that large values of vorticity, divergence, and strain “may result from a combination of frontal dynamics and IGWs within the ML” and that these signals “are decoupled from an internal wave field observed at depth” [11]. This decoupling means a float below the mixed layer may not fully capture the wave field within the mixed layer where the frontal dynamics operate.

Furthermore, Paul and Mahadevan (2025) demonstrate that near-inertial waves and submesoscale currents interact nonlinearly: the effective inertial frequency is modulated by submesoscale vorticity, causing the waves “to be out of phase” and generating “periodic convergence and divergence in the surface layer, particularly at fronts” [12]. This means the wave and submesoscale signals are not simply superposed; their interaction produces net vertical transport that cannot be cleanly decomposed into separate contributions through simple subtraction of a control float record. This redefines the target from “separating” to “characterizing the interaction,” which the proposal's modeling and interpretation framework can partially support.

Published S-MODE results have partly circumvented this challenge through a dynamical argument. Torres et al. (2024) demonstrate that the divergence tendency term in the momentum budget is much smaller than nonlinear terms, which “strongly suggests that internal gravity waves, usually explained by linear dynamics, have a very weak impact on the velocity field analyzed in this study” [13]. This finding—that the observed flow is dominated by nonlinear advection rather than linear wave propagation—provides a scientifically meaningful approach to the separation problem that was not fully articulated in the proposal but represents the kind of analysis the project can deliver. The baseline mission's additional realizations and seasons remain important for the robustness of this conclusion.

### Wave Glider divergence-to-vertical-velocity method

The Wave Glider 1 km square array is proposed to measure horizontal divergence as a function of depth, with integration of divergence supplying “a second estimate of vertical velocity” alongside the Lagrangian floats [1]. The proposal does not present a formal error budget, but available evidence supports the method's viability.

The dominant error source is ADCP velocity uncertainty. Grare et al. (2021), from the same Scripps group participating in S-MODE, report that Wave Glider ADCP comparisons with shipboard ADCP yield RMSE of approximately 0.04 m/s for 15-second ensembles [14]. Critically, the authors note that motion compensation applied after ensemble averaging introduced heading-correlated errors, and they subsequently revised the strategy to correct each individual ping before averaging—the approach planned for S-MODE. With this improved strategy, current profiles “do not exhibit any correlations with the heading of the Wave Glider” [14].

For a 1 km square array with 15–30 minute averaging, single-platform velocity uncertainty can approach 0.003–0.005 m/s, yielding divergence precision of (0.6–1.0) × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹—below the proposed 2 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹ threshold. Integrating this divergence from the surface to 70 m, with conservative assumptions about vertical error correlation, yields vertical velocity uncertainties of 1–3 mm/s. This is marginal but meaningful against the expected submesoscale signal of approximately 1 cm/s [1]. Published S-MODE observations suggest divergence-derived vertical velocities can reach approximately 250 m/day at 40 m depth [13], confirming that the method can detect physically relevant signals.

The pilot campaign, which includes the four Wave Gliders and is explicitly intended to test “agreement between velocity and gradient measurements” [1], provides the appropriate venue to characterize the noise floor before the main IOPs. The planned validation against a bottom-mounted ADCP during S-MODE, referenced by Grare et al. (2021) [14], is essential and commendable.

## Feasibility

**The feasibility of S-MODE is excellent (A).** The measurement systems are mature, the platform accommodations are well within stated capabilities, the operations plan includes explicit descope options and substantial schedule margins, and the pilot campaign provides a dedicated test of the integrated observing system before the main intensive operations periods. No critical technological development is required, and the identified risks have credible mitigations.

### Instrument readiness

The most important feasibility evidence is the demonstrated readiness of the airborne instruments. DopplerScatt, the central targeting sensor, has already produced wide-swath wind and current retrievals and maps of surface vorticity and divergence in the Gulf of Mexico [1]. PRISM has operated on the ER-2 and other aircraft; MASS has participated in 16 campaigns over seven years; and MOSES has been used in two Gulf of Mexico campaigns [1]. Table 3 of the proposal shows that all airborne instruments meet or substantially exceed their scientific requirements: DopplerScatt offers current precision of 0.0215 m/s over 5 km against a 0.09 m/s requirement, MASS lidar provides 2–4 cm per-pulse wave-height accuracy against a 10 cm requirement, and MOSES delivers 0.01 K noise-equivalent temperature difference against a 0.05 K threshold [1]. These margins are conservative.

The oceanographic instruments are similarly proven. The Wave Glider payloads draw directly on the configurations validated by Grare et al. (2021) at Scripps, including the improved ping-by-ping motion compensation strategy that eliminates heading-correlated ADCP errors [14]. The Lagrangian floats with high-rate ADCPs have been deployed successfully in earlier frontal studies [1], and Seagliders carrying CTD, ADCP, and microstructure sensors have a mature operational track record [1]. The proposal does not rely on instrument development or untested integration.

### Platform accommodations

Table 5 confirms that DopplerScatt and MOSES use only 497 lb of the King Air's 772 lb capacity, occupy 34 ft³ of 205 ft³ available, and require 1,700 W of 6,580 W available at 28,000 ft—margins of 55%, 510%, and 287% respectively [1]. MASS on the Twin Otter has even larger margins. The ER-2 accommodation for PRISM is flight-tested [1]. Alternative aircraft are identified for each payload if the preferred platform becomes unavailable, though the proposal notes that PRISM on a Twin Otter would reduce coverage [1]. The requested 79 ship days on an ocean-class UNOLS vessel are within standard scheduling practice for a two-campaign field program, and the vessel is nominally specified as R/V Sally Ride [1].

### Risk management

The operations design includes explicit risk planning. The proposal identifies five principal operational risks—fog and weak winds, airspace closure, airborne instrument anomaly, airborne instrument performance, and glider/Wave Glider loss—and assigns each a consequence and likelihood rating with defined residual values after mitigation [1]. The campaign-duration margin of 200% for weather [1] provides substantial schedule buffer. Airspace coordination is planned early, and the region was selected partly because no nearby warning areas complicate flight operations [1]. Early-campaign ocean-platform losses can be replaced from reserve [1]. The proposal schedules quarterly risk reviews [1]. While weather and platform loss cannot be eliminated, these mitigations are proportionate and specific.

### The pilot campaign and descope option

The pilot campaign functions as a formal risk-reduction step. It includes all aircraft systems, a research vessel, four Wave Gliders, and approximately 30 drifters, omitting only the Seagliders and Lagrangian floats for which the team already has extensive operational experience [1]. The pilot will test “platform/shore interoperability, space-time sampling, and agreement between velocity and gradient measurements” [1]. Surface drifters substitute for the frontal float during this limited operation. The pilot is scheduled for April 2020 with September 2020 as a backup, providing a full year before the first main IOP in April 2021 to process pilot data, improve algorithms, and correct any interoperability problems [1]. This staged approach is a material strength: if the pilot reveals that the Wave Glider divergence array cannot achieve the needed precision, or that the temporal coincidences between the three aircraft create insurmountable scheduling conflicts, there is time to adjust the main-campaign plan.

The threshold mission provides an explicit descope option. The threshold consists of a 10-day pilot and one 14-day IOP with at least seven DopplerScatt flights, five matching SST and PRISM scenes, seven mesoscale glider surveys, and three submesoscale feature surveys [1]. This reduces flight and ship time by approximately half while still delivering the core Goals 1 and 3 [1]. The existence of this option means the project is not an all-or-nothing proposition; a budget reduction or a single-season weather disaster would not eliminate the scientific return.

## Ethics and safety

**The ethics and safety arrangements are excellent (A).** The S-MODE investigation uses established airborne, ship, and autonomous-platform operations with well-defined safety procedures, poses no novel ethical concerns, and includes specific environmental mitigations.

The investigation involves no human subjects, no animal research, and no sensitive data. The measurements are physical and optical observations of the ocean surface and upper water column—surface currents, temperature, salinity, ocean color, wave spectra, wind, and biogeochemical variables—together with standard meteorological bulk-flux measurements from the ship and Wave Gliders [1]. No interventions, sampling of protected species, or activities affecting the seabed are proposed. The field site is offshore of San Francisco, extending approximately 300 km into international waters [1], away from populated areas, sensitive coastal habitats, and busy inshore traffic. The region was selected in part because it lacks nearby military warning areas that could produce airspace closures [1], and early coordination with air traffic control is specified as a mitigation for the airspace-closure risk [1].

Aircraft and ship operations follow their respective established safety regimes. The three aircraft—NASA King Air B200, NASA ER-2, and a Twin Otter DHC-6—are operated under NASA and contractor flight safety protocols. The aircraft accommodation table confirms that all payloads remain within the platforms' mass, volume, power, altitude, and endurance envelopes with comfortable margins [1], and that the instruments have been previously flown in these configurations [1]. The ship component requests an ocean-class UNOLS vessel, nominally R/V Sally Ride, which operates under UNOLS safety standards.

Environmental impact is addressed through two specific measures. First, the surface drifters are specified as CARTHE-style biodegradable instruments [1], consistent with the design that uses environmentally benign materials. This is an appropriate precaution for a campaign deploying approximately 100 drifters in each IOP plus additional drifters in the pilot [1]. Second, the Seagliders, Wave Gliders, and Lagrangian floats are all recoverable platforms that are retrieved after deployment, with telemetered data enabling continued monitoring and active recovery planning during operations [1]. Lost instruments—acknowledged as a risk with consequence rating 1 (minimal) and likelihood 3 (25–60%) [1]—are not chemically hazardous, and the proposal notes that early-campaign losses may be replaced from reserve, implying a finite total deployment rather than an escalating risk of debris accumulation.

Campaign logistics are scheduled to reduce the most significant environmental hazard to airborne operations: coastal fog. The April and September campaign windows were selected in part to avoid the worst seasonal fog [1], reducing both flight safety risk and the scientific risk of lost observing days. The 200% campaign-duration margin for weather [1] further reduces pressure to fly in marginal conditions.

No ethical or safety concerns are raised by the proposed measurements themselves. The remote sensing instruments emit at standard frequencies and power levels for their classes (Ka-band radar at 35.75 GHz for DopplerScatt [1], passive optical and infrared imagers for PRISM, MASS, and MOSES [1]), and the shipboard and autonomous-platform sensors are conventional oceanographic instruments. The data management plan indicates that all Level-2 data will be publicly available through the designated NASA archive within six months of each campaign [1], and no privacy, confidentiality, or proprietary data issues arise from the physical oceanographic observations.

In summary, S-MODE operates entirely within standard oceanographic field-research safety practice. The environmental mitigations are proportionate and specific: biodegradable drifters, recoverable platforms, seasonal scheduling to avoid hazardous fog, and schedule margins that reduce pressure for high-risk operational decisions. The existing safety frameworks of NASA flight operations and UNOLS ship operations provide the operational safety infrastructure.

## Management

**The management arrangements are good (B).** The PI and named leads bring complementary, proven expertise to the project's three principal domains—airborne remote sensing (Rodriguez), in situ observations (Shcherbina), and process analysis and modeling (D'Asaro)—and the management structure sensibly divides these functions under a central PI and a Science Investigation Manager (Matthias) [1]. The team's collective track record includes leadership of the LatMix and D'Asaro et al. (2018) campaigns, development and operation of the DopplerScatt and MASS instruments, and extensive experience with Seagliders, Lagrangian floats, and Wave Gliders in the very configurations proposed [1]. This is not a newly assembled consortium learning to work together; the core collaboration has been tested in prior field programs, and the pilot campaign provides a further integration step before the main IOPs [1].

### Management structure

The management structure, illustrated in Figure 6.1, separates aircraft measurements, in situ observations, and data analysis/modeling under the PI, with NASA Ames Earth Science Project Office (ESPO) providing field-deployment support [1]. This is a standard and appropriate model for a NASA Earth Venture Suborbital investigation. The inclusion of a dedicated Science Investigation Manager to coordinate across the three technical areas is a practical strength, given the operational complexity of simultaneously deploying three aircraft, a research vessel, four Wave Gliders, five Seagliders, and Lagrangian floats with near-real-time adaptive targeting.

### Staffing and schedule

Six postdoctoral researchers are planned for two-year appointments, staggered to support early modeling and campaign optimization, rapid analysis of the first IOP to inform the second, and later focused work on air-sea interaction, Wave Glider and wave data, and integrated frontal structure [1]. Two science workshops per year are anticipated during the main analysis phase, providing regular synthesis checkpoints [1]. The postdoctoral complement and the named senior investigators' fractional commitments are appropriate for a project spanning aircraft operations, in situ fieldwork, modeling, and multi-year analysis.

The schedule is realistic and appropriately staged. Project start in July 2019 allows approximately nine months for preparatory modeling (including the OSSE), pilot planning, instrument integration, and readiness reviews before the April 2020 pilot [1]. A full year separates the pilot from the first main IOP in April 2021, providing time to process pilot data, perform intercomparisons, improve algorithms, and adjust the experiment plan [1]. The two main IOPs define the principal data-collection phase, with data deliveries to the NASA archive beginning six months after each campaign and continuing through March 2022 [1]. The final two years (2022–2024) are reserved for full scientific analysis, synthesis, and publication, including final reprocessing, model-observation comparison, and project close on 30 June 2024 [1]. This sequencing respects the inherent latency between field-data collection, calibration, and publication-quality analysis.

### Data management and remaining considerations

The data-management plan assigns clear responsibilities. Each experimental group is responsible for its own products, with named leads for each major data stream—Shcherbina for in situ measurements, Perkovic-Martin for DopplerScatt Level-0 to Level-2 data, Rodriguez for DopplerScatt Level-3 fields, Mouroulis for PRISM products, and Lenain for MASS products [1]. The PI and Science Investigation Manager oversee delivery to the designated NASA data center (PO.DAAC), with public release of Level-2 data within six months of each campaign [1]. Level-3 products such as MASS directional wavenumber spectra and breaking statistics have later delivery dates (up to September 2023), appropriately reflecting their greater processing complexity [1].

Two considerations moderate the rating. First, the proposal does not provide an itemized budget or specify institutional cost-sharing arrangements. The narrative resource plan—79 ship days, aircraft accommodations within stated platform capabilities, and the instrument inventory—is specific and internally consistent, but a full financial assessment is not possible from the supplied documentation alone. Second, the operational coordination across three aircraft types with different speeds, altitudes, and endurance characteristics is acknowledged as a scheduling challenge [1], and the temporal coincidence requirement of within one hour for SST and ocean-color overlap with current mapping [1] imposes a non-trivial coordination demand that the pilot campaign must validate. The explicit identification of alternative aircraft if preferred platforms are unavailable [1] indicates that the team has considered fallback options, though a shift of PRISM from the ER-2 to a Twin Otter would sacrifice swath coverage and disrupt the fast-sampling pattern.

These reservations do not undermine the fundamental feasibility of the management plan. The team is demonstrably qualified, the structure is appropriate, the schedule provides adequate time for the identified work, and the data-delivery commitments are specific and assigned. The pilot campaign and existing collaboration history provide further confidence that the coordination challenges are manageable.

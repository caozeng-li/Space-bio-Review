# Project review

## Overall assessment

This proposal investigates how developing T lymphocytes influence the growth and organization of thymic stromal cells—the less understood reverse direction of a reciprocal developmental interaction. Using an inducible model of thymic growth in IL7R-deficient mice transplanted with wild-type bone marrow progenitors, the project will map dynamic stromal gene expression across five defined growth stages and identify stromal receptors that correspond to lymphoid ligands. Computational separation of stromal from lymphoid expression, combined with curated receptor-ligand databases, literature mining, and multi-field prioritization, will generate a searchable, annotated collection of candidate lymphoid-to-stromal signaling interactions. The most valuable expected contribution is a systematic, stage-resolved expression resource that provides a discovery platform for subsequent mechanistic studies of thymic stromal biology. A small number of high-priority candidates will receive initial biological validation through existing mouse models, establishing proof of concept without claiming full mechanistic dissection.

The project is well supported by the laboratory's previously published methods for stromal expression mapping and the non-myeloablative transplantation model, which together provide a validated technical foundation. The recovery of known stromal signals in prior studies demonstrates that the computational separation approach adequately captures biologically meaningful stromal expression, though it carries systematic blind spots: inability to resolve stromal cell subtypes, uncertainty for genes expressed at comparable levels in both compartments, and sensitivity to threshold settings. The sampling design—five stages with three independently pooled microarrays per condition—is a pragmatic screening configuration consistent with published pooling recommendations, but provides limited statistical power for individual gene-level inference. The prioritization pipeline's multi-field strategy avoids single-filter brittleness, yet the co-citation and STRING metrics may favor well-studied interactions from other fields over genuinely novel thymus-specific signals, creating an unresolved tension with the discovery objective. The schedule allocates 6-8 months to kinetics and sample collection, 6-8 months to analysis, and the remainder to biological testing, but the heavy informatics workload rests primarily on a single postdoctoral fellow with only 10% specialist support, and the dependency of the entire sampling plan on a yet-undetermined growth trajectory introduces schedule risk without an explicit contingency.

Weighed together, the strengths in question significance, methodological foundation, resource adequacy, and pragmatic experimental design support a positive assessment of the project's core deliverables. The expression resource and the prioritized candidate collection are achievable and would constitute a valuable contribution to thymus biology. The principal limitations—constrained statistical power, stromal subtype resolution, the tension in the prioritization pipeline between discovering novel signals and relying on existing literature, and the schedule compression for Aim 4 validation—do not fundamentally undermine the project's stated exploratory goals. The biological testing in Aim 4 is appropriately scoped as initial validation and does not overpromise mechanistic depth. The project addresses a genuine knowledge gap with methods that have been validated in the announcing laboratory's prior work, and the staged design—kinetics, expression mapping, candidate identification, prioritization, and preliminary testing—is logically coherent. A more explicit contingency for delayed kinetics and a clearer inventory of existing computational infrastructure would improve confidence in schedule feasibility, but the current plan is sufficient for an R21 exploration. The project merits support for its achievable contribution to understanding lymphoid-stromal communication in the thymus.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**The question addresses a genuine and important knowledge gap in thymus biology, and the attainable products—a dynamic stromal expression atlas, a prioritized receptor-ligand candidate collection, and initial biological validation—are well matched to the exploratory scope of the proposed work.**

The thymus supports a continuous stream of T lymphocyte development through stromal signals that regulate progenitor specification, proliferation, differentiation and selection. The reciprocal signaling direction—how developing lymphoid cells instruct stromal growth, organization and survival—has received substantially less systematic attention despite clear evidence that loss of lymphoid cells alters thymic architecture and function [1]. The project targets this under-characterized side of a fundamental developmental interaction. The question is biologically significant not only for understanding normal organ homeostasis but also for conditions in which thymic function is compromised, such as aging, cytotoxic therapies or hematopoietic transplantation. The question is distinct from embryonic specification of the thymic anlage and initial lineage divergence, which have been the focus of many developmental studies [1]. By concentrating on postnatal growth and maintenance, the project fills a specific gap rather than retracing well-covered territory.

The primary scientific value of the proposal lies in its planned descriptive resource rather than in testing a single mechanistic hypothesis. The five-stage expression time course across a physiological growth response will produce a region-resolved (cortical and medullary) map of stromal gene dynamics, which the field currently lacks [1]. Because the stromal estimation procedure compares microdissected tissue with matched purified lymphoid populations at each time point, the resulting profiles control for biological variation between animals and stages while attributing the residual signal to the stromal compartment [1]. Prior application of this method recovered known stromal signaling molecules including Notch ligands, IL-7, kit ligand, Cxcl12 and MHC-associated genes, demonstrating that the computational separation identifies biologically meaningful stromal expression [1]. A subsequent study using the same approach in a castration-induced regrowth model revealed that approximately one in three cortical stromal genes changed with age, compared with only 3–4% of lymphoid genes, and correctly predicted increased medullary B-cell infiltration that was confirmed histologically [2]. These precedents establish that the expression resource can generate testable biological inferences beyond simple cataloguing.

The candidate receptor-ligand collection adds a second layer of value. By anchoring stromal receptor identification in measured expression and requiring corresponding lymphoid ligand expression at matching stages, the approach provides a biologically grounded filter upstream of computational prioritization [1]. The proposal explicitly states that the searchable database will preserve individual evidence fields—co-citation, STRING evidence, expression dynamics, signal intensity and available mutants—rather than collapsing them into a single score, allowing subsequent users to apply their own weighting [1]. This design choice enhances the resource's flexibility and reusability. The collection is expected to include both known thymic crosstalk signals, confirming the method's validity, and candidates without prior thymic association, which together serve as a discovery platform for the applicant's laboratory and the broader community.

The biological validation in Aim 4 is appropriately scoped as proof of concept. The planned readouts—thymic size and cellular composition, combined with marrow transplantation tests for lymphoid ligand candidates—can establish whether disruption of a candidate interaction produces a detectable phenotype in the organ-growth setting [1]. The proposal does not overpromise mechanistic depth: distinguishing effects on stromal proliferation, differentiation and survival is explicitly deferred to subsequent work, which is appropriate for an R21 mechanism [1]. The value of this component is conditional on the output of Aims 1–3, but the core resource deliverables do not depend on Aim 4's success.

The principal limit on scientific value is resolution rather than fundamental validity. The stromal estimation method cannot distinguish among epithelial, fibroblast, endothelial and other mesenchymal populations within a microdissected region [1]. Expression changes therefore reflect composite stromal behavior rather than responses of defined cell types, and subsequent histological identification is needed for attribution. The explicit exclusion of mechanistic dissection—which cellular process is affected, through which intracellular pathway, in which stromal cell type—means the resource provides a starting point for hypothesis generation rather than a completed causal account. These limits are consistent with the exploratory R21 mechanism and do not diminish the contribution of a systematic, stage-resolved expression atlas and a searchable candidate collection to a field where such resources are currently absent.

## Innovation

**The project makes a genuine and well-defined innovative contribution by systematically addressing the neglected lymphoid-to-stromal signaling direction, using an experimental design that integrates several established methods into a novel discovery pipeline. The innovation is methodological, conceptual and resource-oriented rather than resting on a single breakthrough finding, and this profile is appropriate for the exploratory R21 mechanism.**

Several features of the project, taken together, distinguish it from prior work.

First, the question itself—identifying the signals by which developing lymphoid cells instruct stromal growth after the thymic primordium has formed—remains under-characterized despite decades of work on the reverse stromal-to-lymphoid direction. The proposal correctly notes that evidence for lymphoid influence on stromal organization exists, including experimental thymic crosstalk and hematopoietic reconstitution studies, but systematic identification of the mediating molecular interactions has not been undertaken [1]. By concentrating on postnatal growth and maintenance following an induced growth response, rather than on embryonic specification or static comparison of normal and deficient states, the project fills a specific gap in the field's understanding [1].

Second, the experimental design combines elements that have not previously been assembled for this purpose. The inducible growth model—transplanting wild-type bone marrow progenitors into IL7R-deficient recipients without conditioning—is itself a methodological strength with a specific innovative rationale: avoiding irradiation or chemical ablation protects the stromal tissue that is the object of investigation [1]. Previous work from the Petrie laboratory demonstrated the non-myeloablative transplantation approach (Porritt et al., 2003) and the computational stromal expression-mapping method (Griffith et al., 2009), but these have not been integrated to track stromal responses across defined phases of a growth trajectory induced by lymphoid reconstitution [1] [3]. The sampling design—five biologically meaningful stages from baseline through growth initiation, log-phase expansion and peak—represents a deliberate temporal resolution that goes beyond static comparisons of normal versus deficient states.

Third, the receptor-ligand identification and prioritization pipeline introduces creative elements that extend routine expression analysis. The thymus-term co-citation adjustment—dividing the receptor-ligand co-citation score by the thymus co-citation score to identify well-established interactions with limited thymic study—is a conceptually interesting metric that has not been widely applied in this context [1]. The dual receptor identification strategy, combining the curated Human Plasma Membrane Receptome (approximately 900 receptors and 600 ligands) with a complementary Gene Ontology-based Boolean search, provides both specificity and breadth [1]. The multi-field prioritization that preserves individual evidence scores rather than collapsing them into a single ranking allows users to apply their own weighting criteria and makes the database more versatile than a fixed-rank list [1].

The innovation should be understood in the context of what the project realistically claims. It does not propose a new method for stromal expression estimation—that method is published. It does not claim to discover a previously unknown signaling pathway—the candidates identified will include known molecules. Instead, the innovation lies in the systematic application of validated tools to a question that has been difficult to address experimentally, producing a resource that the field currently lacks: a stage-resolved map of stromal receptor dynamics during physiological lymphoid-driven growth, connected to candidate lymphoid ligands through transparent, multi-criteria prioritization. The explicit framing of Aim 4 as proof of concept rather than a complete mechanistic account [1] reinforces that the project's primary innovative contribution is the discovery platform itself rather than any single validated interaction.

Two qualifications are appropriate. First, the individual components—expression microarrays, receptor-ligand database matching, literature co-citation mining, STRING integration—are established techniques individually. The innovation resides in their integration and application to the specific biological question, not in novel computational algorithms. Second, the discovery objective faces an inherent tension with the prioritization pipeline's reliance on existing literature evidence. Well-studied interactions from other fields may outrank genuinely novel thymic signals if co-citation and STRING metrics dominate the ranking [1]. The thymus-term ratio partially addresses this but cannot fully resolve it. These qualifications do not negate the innovative contribution; they situate it appropriately as a resource-building exploration rather than a claim of methodological breakthrough.

## Rigor

**The research design is appropriately matched to the project's exploratory goals, and the core inference—that combining region-matched expression measurements with a staged growth response can nominate candidate lymphoid-to-stromal signaling interactions—is well supported by prior validation of the component methods. The design carries systematic limitations in statistical resolution, stromal cell-type attribution and the extent of causal inference, but these are consistent with the stated scope of an R21 discovery resource and are substantially mitigated by transparent acknowledgement and multi-layered filtering.**

### Stromal expression estimation: validated approach with defined blind spots

The central methodological choice—estimating stromal gene expression by comparing microarray signals from microdissected cortical or medullary tissue with signals from purified lymphocytes of the corresponding region—is the foundation on which the entire project's inferential structure rests. This approach, published by the Petrie laboratory (Griffith et al., 2009, *Immunity*), is a paired subtractive comparison: at each time point, three independently prepared tissue microarrays are compared with three microarrays from the matched lymphoid population, and the residual signal is attributed to stroma [1]. The method uses the median calculated signal as the default threshold for classifying a stromal gene as expressed, with the 40th or 60th percentile available to alter stringency [1].

The method has been validated on its own terms. The proposal reports that previous mapping recovered known stromal-expressed signaling molecules including Notch ligands, IL-7, kit ligand, Cxcl12 and MHC-associated genes [1]. The Griffith et al. (2011) *Aging Cell* study included a formal validation of calculated versus measured stromal expression [2], and the approach generated testable predictions—notably increased medullary B-cell infiltration with age—that were confirmed histologically [2]. These precedents demonstrate that the method can independently identify biologically meaningful stromal signals.

The method's systematic blind spots are real but largely manageable for the project's purpose. Genes expressed at comparable levels in both stromal and lymphoid compartments will have their stromal contribution underestimated or missed entirely, because the tissue signal approximates the lymphoid signal and the residual falls below the detection threshold. This is unlikely to affect most bona fide receptors—few receptors are equally expressed in two such different lineages—but could affect certain adhesion molecules or widely expressed surface proteins. The method cannot distinguish among different stromal cell types (cortical epithelial, medullary epithelial, fibroblast, endothelial) within a microdissected region, a limitation the proposal acknowledges with the appropriate caveat that histological methods can subsequently identify the specific cell type [1]. Lymphocyte purification involves enzymatic and mechanical dissociation that may alter expression of certain genes, particularly stress-response and immediate-early genes, introducing uncertainty for transcripts sensitive to isolation procedures. The IL7R-deficient thymus contains residual endogenous lymphoid cells whose contribution changes as wild-type donor progenitors expand across the growth trajectory, but the proposal's paired design—each lymphoid profile is matched to its own tissue sample—accounts for this within each comparison rather than across time points, which is a sound analytic choice [1]. Overall, false negatives—missing real stromal receptors expressed at low levels or shared with lymphocytes—are more likely than false positives, and the multi-stage prioritization pipeline provides additional filters that reduce the risk that an incorrectly attributed gene survives to biological testing.

### Sampling design: pragmatic screening with limited statistical power

The statistical framework of the sampling design defines the precision of the resulting expression resource. The main experiment examines five stages with three independently pooled microarrays per condition, each pool combining RNA from three mice [1]. This yields nine individual mice contributing to each condition but only three independent measurements for estimating between-pool variance, which is the effective sample size for any formal comparison across time points. Published recommendations on pooling in microarray experiments (Kendziorski et al., 2005) specifically support pooling when fewer than three arrays are used per condition, and findings that inference for most genes is not adversely affected by pooling are directly relevant here [4]. The Takele Assefa et al. (2020) extension to RNA-seq confirms that for high within-group variability, small pools are effective to reduce variability and a pool size of 2–3 can be adequate [5].

With n=3 pools per group across five time points, a time-course ANOVA has limited power to detect moderate temporal changes after multiple-testing correction across tens of thousands of probesets. The proposal implicitly acknowledges this by relying on the median calculated signal as the default expression threshold, by using Affymetrix present/absent calls as an orthogonal filter, and by employing multiple evidence fields in Aim 3 rather than a single statistical cutoff [1]. The design can identify stromal genes showing substantial temporal changes—particularly those with large fold changes relative to pool-to-pool variation—and can resolve clustered temporal patterns such as genes peaking at growth initiation versus at the peak, using hierarchical clustering with k-means and visual inspection [1]. It cannot reliably distinguish subtle trajectories, cannot resolve stromal cell-type contributions, and cannot determine whether expression changes reflect altered gene regulation in existing cells or changes in stromal cell-type proportions. These are compositional as well as regulatory questions that the design does not address, and the proposal does not claim to resolve them within this exploratory scope.

### Dependence on the growth trajectory

The entire sampling plan depends on a yet-undetermined growth trajectory. The five stages—untreated baseline, growth initiation, log-phase growth, approximately 36 hours before the peak, and the peak or approximate steady state—are defined relative to a growth curve that the proposal illustrates with a theoretical figure rather than observed data [1]. The laboratory has experience characterizing growth kinetics in related systems, both in the non-myeloablative transplantation model (Porritt et al., 2003) and in castration-induced regrowth (Griffith et al., 2011) [3] [2]. However, the Griffith et al. (2011) study revealed that the castration-induced regrowth peak was transient—cellularity returned to baseline within two weeks of reaching its maximum [2]. If the IL7R transplantation model shows similarly rapid overshoot and decline, the biologically meaningful phases may be compressed or partially overlapping, complicating the clean stage separation on which the temporal analysis depends. Age-related involution continues during the experiment, meaning the "peak" and "steady state" stages may blend growth-induced changes with ongoing degenerative processes [1]. The design includes two preliminary kinetic series to map the trajectory empirically before the main expression experiment, which is a well-conceived approach [1]. The inferential consequence of any trajectory uncertainty is not that the resource would be invalid, but that the temporal resolution of the resulting expression atlas may be coarser than the five discrete stages imply, and genes responding to closely spaced biological events may not be cleanly separated.

### Prioritization pipeline: multi-field strategy with inherent tensions

The prioritization pipeline in Aim 3 adopts a multi-field strategy that avoids single-filter brittleness, but the evidence fields as a group do not directly test functional relevance to lymphoid-to-stromal signaling. The fields listed in the proposal—co-citation frequency, thymus-term co-citation, ANOVA q-values, signal-intensity decile, STRING evidence, and available mouse mutants—measure proxies: literature attention, expression dynamics, signal reliability and practical tractability [1]. Only the expression-based requirements from Aim 2—that the receptor is detected in the stromal estimate and the ligand in the lymphoid population at the corresponding stage—directly address the direction of signaling.

The co-citation metric captures existing scientific attention rather than functional importance in the thymus. Well-studied pairs such as TNF superfamily members will score highly regardless of their thymic roles. The thymus-term ratio—dividing the receptor-ligand co-citation score by the thymus co-citation score—is designed to favor well-known interactions with limited thymic study, which is a creative approach but may surface well-studied interactions from other fields with incidental thymic expression rather than genuinely novel thymic signals. STRING evidence integrates multiple channels including co-expression and text mining, which may introduce circularity with the project's own expression data and does not distinguish intercellular signaling from intracellular complex membership.

The manual review stage—which will consider known roles in proliferation, differentiation or survival and practical availability of mouse models—is an important safeguard against the limitations of purely computational filters [1]. However, the proposal provides no systematic criteria for how manual judgments will be integrated with quantitative scores or how disagreements between fields will be resolved, which means the actual prioritization logic cannot be fully evaluated in advance. For an R21 exploratory grant, this degree of flexibility is not disqualifying—the staged process of reducing a large candidate set before focused qualitative evaluation is sensible—but it does mean the pipeline's performance characteristics are uncertain. The strongest positive feature is the requirement that each candidate must pass the Aim 2 filter of matched stromal receptor and lymphoid ligand expression, which provides a necessary, though not sufficient, condition for functional relevance.

### Biological validation: appropriate causal scope

The biological validation in Aim 4 provides the strongest causal evidence the project can generate, but its inferential scope is deliberately limited. The planned readouts—thymic size and cellular composition, combined with marrow transplantation tests for lymphoid ligand candidates—can establish whether disruption of a candidate interaction produces a detectable phenotype in the organ-growth setting [1]. The marrow transplantation assay is a particularly elegant design feature: if mutant marrow fails to induce growth in IL7R-deficient recipients, the defect is localized to the hematopoietic compartment, strengthening the inference that the signal operates in the lymphoid-to-stromal direction.

However, thymic size is a composite readout that integrates numerous processes—progenitor recruitment, T cell development, stromal proliferation, survival and differentiation—and a change in size cannot distinguish which process is affected. Germline mutants may affect thymic development through mechanisms unrelated to the specific lymphoid-stromal interaction being tested, and conditional mutants depend on the availability of floxed alleles for the selected candidates, which is not guaranteed a priori. The proposal explicitly acknowledges that more extensive studies distinguishing effects on stromal proliferation, differentiation and survival are outside this exploratory project's scope [1]. This limitation is appropriate for the R21 mechanism and does not reflect a design flaw; it reflects a deliberate scoping decision that matches the project's stated goals.

## Feasibility

**The core research plan (Aims 1–3) is achievable within the two-year R21 timeframe, given the laboratory's validated methods, established shared facilities, and adequate animal numbers, but the schedule carries real risks from three sources: the yet-undetermined growth trajectory, the heavy informatics workload concentrated on a single postdoctoral fellow, and the structural dependency of Aim 4 on the output of earlier aims.**

### Growth trajectory determination and sample collection

The growth trajectory determination is the critical first step on which all subsequent sampling depends. Two kinetic series are planned totaling approximately 216 mice, with the first surveying nine time points at four-day intervals and the second providing finer resolution around key transitions [1]. The laboratory has direct experience characterizing growth kinetics in both the non-myeloablative transplantation model (Porritt et al., 2003) and castration-induced regrowth (Griffith et al., 2011) [3] [2].

The Griffith et al. (2011) study showed that castration-induced regrowth peaked approximately 20 days after surgery but was transient, with cellularity returning to baseline within two weeks [2]. If the IL7R transplantation model exhibits similar overshoot and rapid decline, the distinction between the planned "approximately 36 hours before the peak" and "peak or approximate steady state" stages may be compressed or partially overlapping [1]. Age-related involution continues during the experiment, further complicating clean stage separation [1]. The allocated 6–8 months for kinetics and sample collection is adequate under favorable conditions but provides limited buffer if the trajectory differs substantially from the theoretical curve or if mouse-to-mouse variability requires additional replicates. No explicit contingency plan is described [1].

The main expression experiment itself—45 male recipients across five stages, microdissection of cortical and medullary regions, lymphocyte purification, RNA extraction, and 60 microarrays—is labor-intensive but within the demonstrated capabilities of the laboratory. The proposal shows successful RNA recovery (approximately 10 ng) from a single 20 μm section of an atrophic thymus from a 12-month-old mouse, which is only slightly larger than the thymus of a four-week-old IL7R-deficient recipient [1]. The shared facilities for flow cytometry, laser microdissection, and microarray processing are established and accessible [1].

### Informatics workload

The informatics workload presents the most serious feasibility concern. The postdoctoral fellow must execute stromal expression estimation using the published deconvolution procedure, time-course ANOVA with multiple-testing correction, Receptome-based receptor-ligand matching (approximately 900 receptors, 600 ligands), GO-based Boolean receptor and ligand list generation, automated PubMed and PubMed Central co-citation mining with gene symbol aliases, STRING evidence integration, hierarchical clustering with k-means, and construction of a searchable, multi-field database that preserves individual evidence scores [1].

The proposal itself acknowledges the informatics bottleneck, noting that "intensive data-processing work" is anticipated around months 7–12 and that the informatics specialist Mohammad Fallahi "does not have capacity to absorb that workload alone" [1]. The specialist's role is defined as 10% effort for training and specialist assistance, with the explicit expectation that the postdoctoral fellow will learn the computational methods and execute the majority of the work [1]. While the laboratory has existing computational scripts [1] and Fallahi has prior experience with the laboratory, the proposal does not inventory which components of the planned analysis pipeline are already built versus those that must be newly developed. The automated literature co-citation mining with exclusion filters for large-scale genomic studies, the STRING integration, and the construction of the sortable database represent significant software engineering beyond routine microarray analysis. A postdoctoral fellow performing the experimental work in parallel cannot realistically build these pipelines from scratch within the 6–8 month analysis window without substantial pre-existing infrastructure. The feasibility of the informatics component therefore depends heavily on how much code already exists from the Griffith et al. (2009) study.

### Structural dependency of Aim 4

Aim 4 faces a structural dependency problem. Candidates cannot be finalized before months 12–14, after which acquiring mutant mice from repositories or collaborators, expanding breeding colonies, crossing to hCD2-Cre or Foxn1-Cre lines, and obtaining IACUC amendment approval requires a minimum of 3–4 months [1]. This leaves at most 4–8 months for experimentation—sufficient for thymic size and composition analysis in perhaps one or two mutant lines. The proposal acknowledges this scope limitation and correctly frames Aim 4 as establishing "initial biological relevance" rather than mechanistic dissection [1]. The dependency on unknown candidates means Aim 4 could fail to produce validation not because the hypothesis is incorrect, but because top-ranked candidates lack available floxed alleles with the appropriate Cre lines, or because breeding timelines extend beyond the grant period. The core deliverables—the expression time course and the prioritized candidate database—do not depend on Aim 4's success, which appropriately insulates the project's primary scientific contribution from this risk.

## Ethics and safety

**The animal use is ethically justified, the model choice is well reasoned, and the proposed animal numbers and welfare provisions are proportionate to the scientific goals, though the deferred specification of Aim 4 requirements introduces a manageable procedural gap.**

The central ethical question for this project is whether the use of an intact animal model is necessary and whether the harm to animals is minimized and justified by the expected scientific gain. The proposal provides a clear justification on both counts. The biological question—how developing lymphoid cells influence stromal growth and organization in the thymus—cannot be studied in a cell culture system because thymic stromal organization depends on the three-dimensional architecture of the organ, and the interaction between migrating lymphoid progenitors and regionally specialized stromal cells occurs in a spatial context that cannot be replicated in vitro. The proposal explicitly states that the justification for an animal model is the investigation of thymic function and organization as an intact organ [1].

The choice of the IL7R-deficient mouse model reflects a deliberate effort to minimize harm while maximizing scientific information. The transplantation protocol uses no myeloablative conditioning—no irradiation or cytotoxic chemicals—which specifically avoids injury to the stromal tissue that is the object of investigation [1]. This design choice has both a scientific rationale (protecting the very tissue to be studied) and an ethical benefit (sparing animals the morbidity associated with conditioning regimens). The use of young (four-week-old) mice means the recipients have an already formed thymus, consistent with the project's stated focus on postnatal growth and maintenance rather than embryonic development [1].

The animal numbers are itemized for each experimental component and are reasonable for the proposed work. The main expression experiment requires 45 male recipients (five stages, three independently pooled samples per stage, three mice per pool), with 90 IL7R-deficient mice budgeted to obtain those males and 90 sex-matched wild-type donors [1]. Each of the two preliminary kinetic series is budgeted at 108 mice. The total of 420 mice for Aims 1–3 represents a considered balance between adequate biological replication—the three independent pools per condition provide a minimum of three independent measurements for variance estimation—and responsible use of animals. The proposal notes that where feasible, marrow from a donor can serve more than one recipient, reducing donor numbers [1]. The pooling strategy itself reduces the total number of arrays required while maintaining biological coverage, consistent with the efficiency goals that underpin reduction principles.

Aim 4 presents a structural challenge for prospective animal-use specification because the candidates to be tested, and therefore the specific mouse models, are unknown at the outset [1]. The proposal estimates fewer than 200 additional animals and states that an IACUC amendment will specify the number after candidate selection. This is a defensible approach for a discovery-driven project where the validation experiments cannot be fully specified in advance, but it does mean that the complete animal-use picture is not available for review. The estimate appears conservative relative to typical breeding and phenotyping experiments for one or two mutant lines, and the proposal's explicit commitment to amend the protocol before proceeding provides an institutional safeguard. The conditional models using the laboratory's existing hCD2-Cre and Foxn1-Cre lines [1] are likely to involve tissue-specific rather than whole-body gene disruption, which can reduce unintended developmental effects and the number of animals needed for interpretation.

The welfare provisions are described in appropriate detail. Veterinary oversight is specified. Anesthesia is required for procedures causing more than momentary distress, with monitoring through recovery and at least daily for the subsequent week. Humane endpoints—intervention and euthanasia for severe discomfort—are established [1]. These measures are commensurate with standard practice for murine transplantation studies and reflect appropriate attention to animal welfare.

Data are to be communicated through publications and presentations and made available by acceptance of the main findings, subject to institutional conditions including a possible 60-day intellectual-property review delay [1]. Unique mouse resources, if produced, will be shared under applicable NIH resource-sharing requirements. The specific presentation format and any necessary access restrictions will be determined when the data are generated, which is a common and acceptable approach for an exploratory project where the data structure cannot be fully specified in advance.

## Management

**The management arrangements are basically adequate for delivering the core resource deliverables (Aims 1–3), but the plan places heavy operational pressure on a single postdoctoral fellow and provides insufficiently specified informatics infrastructure, creating a concentration of risk that is only partly mitigated by the stated supervision and specialist support.**

The principal investigator, Howard Petrie, has direct and published expertise in all the core methods on which the proposal depends—the non-myeloablative transplantation model (Porritt et al., 2003), the computational stromal expression-mapping approach (Griffith et al., 2009), and the characterization of thymic growth kinetics in both transplantation and castration-induced regrowth settings (Griffith et al., 2011) [3] [1] [2]. The proposal states that Petrie will supervise the biological and informatic work, train the postdoctoral researcher, oversee protocols and resources, and review raw data before publication [1]. This level of PI involvement is appropriate for a project in which the experimental and computational methods have been established in the same laboratory, and it provides reasonable assurance that methodological decisions during execution will be informed by direct experience.

The shared facilities on which the work depends—flow cytometry and sorting, laser microdissection, microarray processing, and computational infrastructure—are described as established and accessible, with the laboratory having prior experience using them [1]. The availability of the Human Plasma Membrane Receptome database with mouse microarray identifiers, supplied by the Receptome group, is documented, and existing computational scripts provide a starting point for the analysis [1]. These elements represent concrete resources rather than aspirational commitments.

The critical management concern is the concentration of both experimental and computational work on a single full-time postdoctoral fellow. The experimental workload—two preliminary kinetic series totaling approximately 216 mice, the main expression experiment involving 45 recipients and 60 microarrays, microdissection, lymphocyte purification, RNA preparation, and the Aim 4 validation experiments—is substantial. The computational workload is equally demanding and includes several novel elements beyond the published stromal estimation procedure: automated PubMed and PubMed Central co-citation mining with exclusion filters for large-scale genomic studies, STRING evidence integration, and construction of a sortable, multi-field database that preserves individual evidence scores [1]. The specialist's 10% effort is primarily for training, with the expectation that the postdoctoral fellow will learn the computational methods and execute the majority of the work [1]. The proposal does not inventory which components of the planned analysis pipeline are already built versus those that must be newly developed. The automated literature mining, the STRING integration, and the construction of the searchable database represent significant software engineering tasks. A postdoctoral fellow performing the experimental work in parallel cannot realistically build these pipelines from scratch within the 6–8 month analysis window without substantial pre-existing infrastructure.

A secondary management concern is that the dependency of Aim 4 on the output of Aims 1–3 means the specific mouse mutants, breeding strategies and Cre lines cannot be specified in advance [1]. The proposal commits to an IACUC amendment after candidate selection, and the laboratory already holds the hCD2-Cre and Foxn1-Cre lines [1]. These are appropriate procedural measures, but they mean the final experimental phase cannot be planned in detail at the outset, which introduces coordination uncertainty during the compressed final months of the grant.

The schedule allocates 6–8 months to kinetics and sample collection, 6–8 months to analysis and prioritization, and the remaining approximately 8–12 months to refinement and biological testing [1]. This sequencing is logical, but the management plan provides no explicit contingency for the most likely disruption—delayed growth trajectory determination—which would compress all downstream work. The absence of a named contingency or buffer in the personnel allocation means that schedule recovery would depend on the postdoctoral fellow absorbing additional pressure, which is a fragile arrangement for a plan that already concentrates responsibility on a single trainee. The overall management is adequate for Aims 1–3 under favorable conditions but lacks the redundancy or explicit contingency that would strengthen confidence in execution under less favorable conditions.

# Project review

## Overall assessment

This R03 project investigates whether six circulating MHC class I-like proteins—MICA, MICB, ULBP1, ULBP2, ULBP3, and ULBP4—measured before clinical diagnosis are prospectively associated with incident colorectal cancer (CRC), and seeks to identify their demographic, lifestyle, and genetic determinants. Using existing proteomic, genomic, and cancer outcome data from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) cohort, with approximately 12,000 cancer-free participants and 360 incident CRC cases at baseline, the study will assess protein levels at a single time point (Aim 1a); short- and long-term changes in protein levels before diagnosis (Aim 1b); and correlates of protein levels and their trajectories (Aim 2). A secondary Mendelian randomization exploration is also proposed.

The central question—whether circulating MHC class I-like proteins measured years before clinical diagnosis predict incident CRC—addresses a genuine biological and clinical gap. The existing literature overwhelmingly documents elevated soluble MICA and MICB in patients with established disease, where higher levels associate with advanced stage, metastasis, and poorer survival across breast cancer [1], head and neck cancer [2], lymphoma [3], hepatocellular carcinoma [4], and pancreatic cancer [5] [6]. Those studies cannot distinguish whether elevated soluble NKG2D ligands are a consequence of the tumor itself or a pre-existing host characteristic that precedes malignancy. The proposal reframes the question prospectively—do levels measured in initially cancer-free individuals predict future CRC risk?—and this shift from prognostic to etiologic inquiry is conceptually important.

The most valuable expected knowledge resides in three areas that extend meaningfully beyond previously published work. The six-protein panel including ULBP1–4 alongside MICA and MICB provides the first prospective data on circulating ULBP family members and incident CRC. Second, Aim 1b—the analysis of short-term (approximately three-year) and long-term (approximately twenty-year) changes in protein levels as predictors of incident CRC—represents a genuinely novel analytic contribution. Third, Aim 2's systematic characterization of demographic, lifestyle, and genetic determinants of protein levels and their trajectories across all six proteins will provide the quantitative exposure-disease context needed to interpret the epidemiological associations. The study is appropriately positioned as an exploratory R03 generating descriptive and associative evidence that can prioritize targets for subsequent mechanistic and translational investigation [7].

The principal limitations tempering the judgement are as follows. The team's published Wang et al. (2023) paper has already reported baseline MICA/MICB associations with CRC in the same ARIC cohort [8], substantially narrowing the novelty of Aim 1a for those two proteins. The factor-score approach aggregates six proteins with distinct shedding mechanisms, receptor down-modulation capacities, and release pathways into a single composite, which is adequate as a screening tool but masks protein-specific biology. The marginal structural Cox model for Aim 1b lacks detail on weight specification and positivity assessment. SomaScan measures total circulating immunoreactivity without distinguishing proteolytically shed soluble protein from exosome-associated forms, qualifying mechanistic interpretation. The Mendelian randomization secondary aim proposes instrument discovery and causal estimation in the same dataset without adequate treatment of winner's curse, weak-instrument bias, or MHC-region pleiotropy, making causal claims from this analysis unreliable.

On balance, the core observational aims are feasible and likely to produce useful descriptive and associative evidence. The novel contributions—ULBP1–4, protein trajectory analyses, and systematic determinant characterization—are worthwhile at an exploratory stage. Conditional support is warranted, subject to specific methodological refinements that bring the inferential scope into alignment with the study's actual capabilities and prior published evidence.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

**Scientific value is good.** The question—whether circulating MHC class I-like proteins measured years before diagnosis predict incident colorectal cancer—is biologically and clinically important, and addresses a gap distinct from the existing literature on protein levels after cancer diagnosis. The proposal reframes the inquiry from prognostic to etiologic, which aligns with the long-term goal of informing CRC prevention and early detection [7].

The most valuable expected knowledge contributions are threefold. First, the six-protein panel including ULBP1–4 alongside MICA and MICB provides the first prospective data on circulating ULBP family members and incident CRC. ULBP proteins differ from MICA/B in their membrane attachment—GPI-anchored for ULBP1 and ULBP3, transmembrane for ULBP4, and variable for ULBP2 [9]—as well as in their release mechanisms, which range from preferential exosomal to metalloprotease-mediated shedding [9], and in their tissue expression patterns (ULBP4 shows strong bias toward tissues of ectodermal origin) [10]. No prior study has examined whether circulating ULBP1–4 levels measured before diagnosis associate with subsequent CRC, although ULBP2 gene expression has shown prognostic value in colon adenocarcinoma using TCGA data [11].

Second, Aim 1b—the analysis of short-term and long-term changes in protein levels as predictors of incident CRC—represents a genuinely novel analytic contribution. The team's published Wang et al. (2023) paper collected Visit 3 measurements but used them only for split-sample reliability assessment [8], not as a change exposure. The proposed marginal structural Cox models using protein trajectories with follow-up beginning after the change interval [7] address temporality in a way no prior study of these proteins has attempted. If rising protein levels over a three-year interval predict subsequent CRC independently of static baseline levels, that would strengthen the case that these proteins reflect early carcinogenic processes rather than stable host characteristics.

Third, Aim 2's systematic characterization of demographic, lifestyle, and genetic determinants of protein levels and their trajectories across all six proteins [7] will provide the quantitative context needed to interpret the epidemiological associations, including the proportion of protein variation explained by common genetic variants. The bi-racial composition of ARIC (Black and White adults from four US communities) [7] also allows assessment in populations not well represented in prior MHC class I-like protein studies [7].

The incremental contribution must be assessed against the team's published findings in the same cohort. Wang et al. (2023), with the PI as co-author, already measured sMICA and sMICB by SomaScan at ARIC Visit 2 in 10,834 cancer-free participants with 312 incident CRC cases, reporting that lower sMICA levels associated with lower CRC risk in males (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.49–0.96) but not in females, and that sMICA and sMICB levels were not associated with CRC risk overall in the full cohort [8]. That study's dataset substantially overlaps with the current proposal's stated 12,589 participants and 360 cases at Visit 2 [7]. The baseline MICA/MICB–CRC association component of Aim 1a is therefore largely a confirmatory extension of published work, although the proposal adds ULBP1–4 and a factor-score approach that combines all six proteins. The value of Aim 1a thus depends critically on whether the ULBP1–4 proteins and the composite factor score reveal associations beyond those already reported for MICA and MICB alone.

The study is appropriately positioned as an exploratory R03 generating descriptive and associative evidence rather than definitive clinical tools. The investigators explicitly plan follow-on validation with standard methods and replication in an independent cohort such as PLCO before any screening or risk-prediction application [7]. This staging is consistent with the R03 mechanism's scope and with the known limitations of aptamer-based proteomic measurements for these particular proteins. The combination of repeated measurements (up to three time points across two decades), rich covariate and genomic data, and bi-racial representation supports the generation of epidemiologic evidence that can prioritize targets for subsequent investigation.

## Innovation

**Innovation is good but contains important overlaps with the team's published work in the same cohort.** The most original contributions extend beyond any previously reported findings, but the baseline MICA/MICB–CRC association component draws on already-published work from this group.

The project's innovative elements are best understood relative to distinct bodies of prior work. The existing cancer literature on circulating MHC class I-like proteins is overwhelmingly derived from patients already diagnosed with malignancy. Studies across hepatocellular carcinoma [4], breast cancer [1], diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [3], pancreatic cancer [5] [6], and head and neck cancer [2] have consistently associated higher soluble NKG2D ligands with advanced disease and poorer prognosis, but cannot determine whether protein elevation precedes or follows tumor development. The proposal's prospective framing—measuring protein levels years before diagnosis in initially cancer-free individuals—represents a genuine conceptual shift from prognostic to etiologic inquiry.

For the ULBP family specifically, the innovation is clearer. ULBP1–4 proteins differ from MICA and MICB in membrane attachment (GPI-anchored for ULBP1 and ULBP3, transmembrane for ULBP4, and variable for ULBP2) [9], release mechanisms (preferential exosomal versus metalloprotease-mediated shedding) [9], and tissue expression patterns (ULBP4 shows strong bias toward ectodermal tissues) [10]. These biological differences give ULBP1–4 the potential to reveal distinct or complementary risk information. Ruan et al. (2021) demonstrated that ULBP2 gene expression has prognostic value in colon adenocarcinoma using TCGA data [11], but no prior study has examined circulating ULBP protein levels prospectively in relation to incident CRC. The proposal's six-protein panel therefore opens genuinely new descriptive territory.

The protein trajectory analysis in Aim 1b is likewise entirely novel. The Wang 2023 paper [8] collected SomaScan measurements at both Visit 2 and Visit 3 but used the Visit 3 data only for split-sample reliability assessment [8], not as a change exposure. The proposal's marginal structural Cox models using Visit 2–3 protein change as the exposure with follow-up beginning at Visit 3 [7] represent a methodological and substantive advance.

**The innovation is qualified by the published baseline MICA/MICB findings.** Wang et al. (2023, with the PI as co-author) reported in the same ARIC cohort (10,834 participants, 312 CRC cases) that lower sMICA levels associated with lower CRC risk in males (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.49–0.96) but not in females, and that sMICA and sMICB levels were not associated with CRC risk overall in the full cohort [8]. This dataset substantially overlaps with the proposal's stated 12,589 participants and 360 CRC cases [7], and the key baseline MICA/MICB–CRC tests in Aim 1a are therefore partly confirmatory. The proposal's incremental contribution for these two proteins rests on the addition of ULBP1–4 to the panel and the factor-score approach combining all six proteins into a single exposure. This is a meaningful extension but represents a narrower degree of originality than the proposal's framing suggests.

The secondary Mendelian randomization aim is a novel exploration in concept, but its capacity to contribute original causal knowledge is substantially constrained, as addressed under Rigor. The follow-on validation plan mentioning standard methods such as ELISA [7] is appropriately deferred to subsequent work and does not form part of the current proposal's innovation claim.

## Rigor

**Rigor is moderate; the design supports the intended inferences with important qualifications.** The study employs a well-characterized prospective cohort with validated cancer ascertainment, pre-specified analysis plans with appropriate covariate adjustment, sensitivity analyses for occult disease, and statistical power calculations [7]. However, several methodological choices constrain the strength of the biological and causal conclusions that can be drawn.

**The factor-score approach aggregates biologically diverse proteins.** The primary analytic framework—a factor score derived from principal-axis factor analysis of the six log-transformed protein concentrations [7]—is a defensible dimension-reduction strategy for an exploratory R03, given the anticipated inter-protein correlations of 0.4–0.55. However, this approach aggregates proteins with distinct membrane attachments (GPI-anchored ULBP1/ULBP3 versus transmembrane MICA/MICB/ULBP4, with ULBP2 variable) [9], release mechanisms (exosomal versus ADAM10/ADAM17-mediated shedding) [9] [12], and capacities for NKG2D down-modulation (MICA promotes more rapid receptor internalization than ULBP2) [13].

A significant factor-score association with CRC would establish that the shared component of NKG2D ligand elevation precedes clinical disease—valuable descriptive evidence—but cannot distinguish which proteins or release pathways drive the signal. The risk of opposing effects cancelling in the composite is real. The investigators' own published data show that sMICA and sMICB were uncorrelated at Visit 2 (r = 0.01) [8], indicating that at least some protein pairs behave independently. The exploratory individual-protein analyses [7] partially mitigate this concern, but with 360 CRC cases, power for protein-specific comparisons is limited relative to the primary factor-score hypothesis powered at HR 1.24 per SD [7]. A null factor-score result could reflect cancellation of opposing effects rather than true absence of association, and the individual-protein analyses are underpowered to resolve that ambiguity definitively.

**The marginal structural Cox model for Aim 1b requires additional specification.** The approach is conceptually well-motivated—it aims to address time-dependent confounding by covariates such as BMI and smoking that change during the same interval as the protein levels [7]. However, the proposal provides insufficient methodological detail to evaluate its feasibility. The protein change constitutes a continuous single-interval exposure rather than a repeatedly assessed treatment, raising distinct positivity concerns that are not addressed. Schomaker et al. (2024) note that positivity is typically violated with continuous interventions [14], and Spreafico (2025) demonstrates that even minor near-positivity violations can substantially destabilize IPTW estimators, inflating variance and bias [15].

The proposal describes no plan for diagnosing extreme weights, assessing covariate balance in the weighted sample, applying truncation thresholds, or comparing IPTW results with standard multivariable regression. The short-term analysis (11,340 participants, 306 CRC cases) should support IPTW estimation with a moderate number of covariates, but the long-term analysis (6,538 participants, 56 CRC cases) is particularly vulnerable: effective sample size is further reduced by weighting, and the survivor cohort introduces selection bias that IPTW alone cannot address because the weights do not account for the determinants of survival to Visit 5.

**The Mendelian randomization secondary aim is the most methodologically constrained component.** The proposal describes instrument discovery and causal estimation within the same ARIC dataset using a simple `ivprobit` comparison in Stata [7], with a conventional F-statistic above 10 as the criterion for adequate instrument strength. This design suffers from several problems that are not acknowledged or addressed.

Instrument discovery and MR estimation in the same dataset conflate discovery bias with causal estimation. Winner's curse produces overestimation of variant-protein associations, inflating the F-statistic and biasing the MR estimate. In a one-sample setting with a binary outcome, the relative bias of the 2SLS estimate approximates 1/E(F) on the log odds ratio scale toward the confounded observational estimate [16], meaning an F of 10 produces approximately 10% relative bias. The conventional F \> 10 threshold was developed for two-sample MR where bias is toward the null; in one-sample MR, bias is toward the confounded observational estimate and is more consequential.

For ULBP1–4, no pQTLs are cited and none are known from published literature. For MICA, the A5.1 microsatellite explains a modest portion of variance (1.24–1.38-fold per allele in controls) [6], but the variance explained—the critical parameter for MR instrument strength—is not reported. For MICB, the proposal lists six candidate SNPs [7] but provides no evidence that any of these associate with circulating MICB levels. Kuliesius et al. (2025) report that only 4.6% of SomaScan-measured proteins had genome-wide significant cis-pQTLs in a sample of 200 [17]. The Cadiou et al. (2026) preprint notes that newly assessed proteins on the expanded SomaScan platform were less likely to harbor cis-pQTLs than established secretome proteins (15% versus 28%), consistent with lower plasma concentrations and predominantly intracellular localization [18]. MICA, MICB, and ULBP1–4 are not classical secreted proteins; they are shed or released from cell membranes, and the proportion of their circulating variance attributable to common genetic variants remains uncharacterized.

The MHC genomic context further complicates the analysis. MICA and MICB reside in the MHC region on chromosome 6p21, one of the most gene-dense and pleiotropic regions of the human genome [19]. Luo et al. (2026) demonstrated that MHC variants have extensive trans-regulatory effects on hundreds of plasma proteins through HLA-mediated mechanisms [19]. The `ivprobit` approach with a single weighted genetic score cannot readily address horizontal pleiotropy, and no sensitivity analyses (MR-Egger, weighted median, MR-PRESSO) are mentioned. The six-month allocation for this aim (months 19–24) shared with manuscript preparation [7] is insufficient for the iterative work that rigorous MR would require. This component is better characterized as exploratory genetic association analysis rather than formal Mendelian randomization.

**SomaScan measurement characteristics qualify mechanistic interpretation.** The assay measures total circulating immunoreactivity in relative fluorescence units [7] without distinguishing proteolytically shed soluble ectodomains from exosome- or vesicle-associated protein. The proposal's biological rationale depends on the distinction between membrane-bound and soluble shed MHC class I-like proteins [7], and the literature indicates that exosome-associated NKG2D ligands are more potent down-modulators of the NKG2D receptor than cleaved soluble molecules [9]. SomaScan cannot distinguish these forms, meaning the measurements reflect "total circulating MHC class I-like protein immunoreactivity" rather than specifically the immune-evasive shed form. This limitation qualifies mechanistic claims but does not prevent identification of prospective associations.

The platform's technical performance is adequate for exploratory use. Published ARIC data demonstrate usable precision for sMICA and sMICB measured by SomaScan (split-sample reliability coefficients 0.93 at Visit 2 and 0.88 at Visit 3) [8]. The proposal's reported median CV of 5% and ICC of 0.96 from a pilot in 42 ARIC participants [7], and the version 4 lifetime median CV of 6.2% across 220 runs and 660 replicate QC samples [7], are consistent with Candia et al. (2024) reporting median interplate CV of 4.5% [20]. However, no protein-specific performance data are provided for the six target proteins, and individual aptamer performance can vary substantially.

**The batch separation across visits introduces technical uncertainty for Aim 1b.** Visit 3 and Visit 5 SomaScan assays are completed while Visit 2 assays are described as "underway" [7], meaning the three visits were necessarily measured in separate batches. The proposal describes standard within-batch quality control—hybridization-control normalization, within-plate median-signal normalization, calibrators, 26 pooled plasma controls in triplicate, and approximately 5% split-specimen duplicates [7]—but provides no cross-batch harmonization strategy. Candia et al. (2024) report median interbatch CV of 8% versus intrabatch interplate CV of 4.5%, noting that calibrators alone do not suffice to remove cross-batch effects, and recommend bridge samples or bioinformatic approaches such as ComBat [20]. The Wang et al. (2023) paper using these same ARIC SomaScan data [8] analyzed each visit cross-sectionally and thus sidestepped the interbatch comparison issue; Aim 1b directly confronts it. The storage-time analysis in Candia et al. provides partial reassurance that variability increase in samples stored 2–30 years was not significant for most SOMAmers [20], but this finding applies to paired replicates within the same batch, not separate batches measured at different times.

These limitations do not prevent the study from generating useful descriptive and associative evidence. The core observational aims—particularly the ULBP1–4 baseline associations and the short-term protein trajectory analysis—are supported by adequate sample sizes, validated outcome ascertainment, and pre-specified sensitivity analyses for occult disease (excluding diagnoses within two or five years of sampling), effect modification (by sex, race, colon versus rectum, and follow-up length), and missing data [7]. The principal inferential constraints are that the study cannot distinguish which specific proteins or release mechanisms underlie any observed associations, and that the MR component cannot support causal claims as currently configured.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility is good; the project can be carried out as an analytical study of existing data, with a manageable technical dependency on cross-batch proteomic normalization.** The ARIC cohort provides the essential platform: frozen plasma specimens already collected and stored, SomaScan proteomic measurements completed or underway at three visits, deep genotyping (GWAS and whole-genome sequencing through TOPMed and Centers for Common Disease Genomics for approximately 12,000 participants) [7], validated cancer ascertainment through 2015 via state-registry linkage and active follow-up [7], and decades of harmonized lifestyle and clinical covariate data. No new participant contact, specimen collection, or assay development is required. The project is principally a data analysis effort, consistent with the R03 mechanism's scope.

**The analytical sample sizes are adequate for the primary short-term analyses.** At Visit 2, the plan specifies 12,589 cancer-free participants with 360 incident CRC cases; at Visit 3, 11,340 cancer-free participants with 306 CRC cases [7]. The power calculation gives 80% power at two-sided α = 0.05 to detect HR 1.24 per SD of the continuous factor score for Aim 1a and HR 1.26 per SD of factor-score change for Aim 1b [7]. For Aim 2, the power analysis yields 80%-power detectable mean differences of 2.81 pg/mL at baseline and 3.00 for Visit 2–3 change [7]. The detectable effect sizes are modest, and the study is powered around the factor score rather than individual proteins, which is realistic given the anticipated inter-protein correlations.

**Two dependencies warrant attention. The first is cross-batch normalization of SomaScan measurements.** As discussed under Rigor, Visit 2, Visit 3, and Visit 5 assays were necessarily run in separate batches [7]. For Aim 1a (baseline protein levels) and single-visit analyses within Aim 2, batch separation is irrelevant because each analysis uses a single time point. For Aim 1b (change scores between visits), systematic batch offsets could be mistaken for biological change. The proposal should specify a cross-batch normalization strategy—bridge samples, ratio-based calibration using the pooled plasma controls, or ComBat—before change scores are computed. The Wang et al. (2023) paper analyzed each visit cross-sectionally [8], so this issue was sidestepped; Aim 1b directly confronts it.

**The second dependency concerns the long-term trajectory analysis (Visit 2 to Visit 5 change).** With only 56 incident CRC cases available at Visit 5 [7], statistical power is low, and the survivor cohort that reached Visit 5 is a selected subpopulation of participants aged approximately 62–86. The marginal structural Cox model cannot address the selection bias introduced by two decades of attrition, competing mortality, and loss to follow-up that determined who contributed Visit 5 samples. This analysis should be treated as exploratory and its estimates interpreted descriptively; it cannot support causal inference about twenty-year protein trajectories. The short-term Visit 2–3 change analysis (306 CRC cases) is the more informative and statistically reliable component of Aim 1b.

**The Mendelian randomization secondary aim's execution is technically possible with available ARIC data but methodologically constrained**, as addressed under Rigor. The proposal's computing infrastructure (SAS and Stata) [7] is adequate for all planned analyses. The 24-month schedule—months 1–3 for IRB and data extraction, months 4–12 for Aim 1, months 13–18 for Aim 2, and months 19–24 for the secondary aim and manuscript preparation [7]—is a reasonable timeline, though the schedule does not explicitly budget time for cross-batch normalization, which should be completed before Aim 1b change-score analyses begin.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety arrangements are excellent and entirely proportionate to the study design.** The project is a secondary analysis of existing, de-identified data and stored frozen plasma specimens from the ARIC cohort. It involves no new participant contact, no additional data collection, no intervention, and no experimental manipulation.

The proposal specifies that investigators receive only coded research IDs, with direct personal identifiers and the identity linkage remaining at the ARIC Coordinating Center [7]. This separation ensures that individual-level data accessible to the analytical team cannot be re-identified without access to the coordinating center's mapping file. Privacy protections cover transmission, handling, and distribution of the study data, consistent with standard NIH and institutional requirements for secondary research using existing cohort resources. The project will operate under institutional IRB arrangements, with months 1–3 allocated to IRB arrangements and data extraction [7]; because the study uses pre-existing, de-identified data, it is likely to qualify for expedited review or exemption under the Common Rule.

The data-sharing plan is appropriately structured. Descriptive and association results will be shared under NIH policy, while access to the underlying study dataset follows ARIC leadership and publication-committee policies [7]. This two-tier approach correctly recognizes that the investigators are data users rather than data stewards: they can share their analytical results freely but do not have authority to redistribute the ARIC dataset. This arrangement respects both the original participant consent for use of specimens and data in cancer research and the cohort governance structure that protects participant privacy.

The bi-racial composition of ARIC (Black and White adults from four US communities) [7] means the study will generate data relevant to populations historically under-represented in MHC class I-like protein research [7]. This is an ethical strength—the secondary use of existing diverse cohort data avoids the recruitment burdens and opportunity costs of a new study while addressing a knowledge gap. No material risks to participants, communities, or the research team are evident. The work carries the minimal informational risk inherent in any analysis of de-identified health data, and the described data-handling procedures are standard and adequate.

## Management

**Management arrangements are adequate for the proposed analytical study.** The project is principally a secondary data analysis within an established cohort infrastructure. It requires coordination of existing proteomic, genomic, and cancer outcome data rather than new participant recruitment, specimen collection, or assay development. The described arrangements are proportionate to the R03 mechanism and the scope of work.

The team combines relevant expertise in the three domains required for this project. The Principal Investigator, Anna Prizment (University of Minnesota), has direct prior experience with the specific data resources: she is a co-author on the Wang et al. (2023) paper that analyzed SomaScan-measured sMICA and sMICB in ARIC and linked these to incident CRC [8], and she was senior author on the Onyeaghala et al. (2017, 2019) studies of MICA polymorphisms, soluble MICA levels, and pancreatic cancer risk [5] [6]. This prior work demonstrates familiarity with the biomarker biology, the ARIC data structure, the SomaScan platform, and the analytic approaches—Cox regression, genetic association analysis—that form the core of the proposal. The proposal also references collaboration encompassing molecular cancer epidemiology, computational genetics, and biostatistics [7], appropriately matching the skill requirements for Aim 1 (prospective association modeling), Aim 2 (genetic and lifestyle determinant analysis), and the secondary Mendelian randomization exploration.

The 24-month schedule is realistic for the described analytical work [7]. Months 1–3 are allocated to IRB arrangements and data extraction—a reasonable window given that ARIC is an established resource with defined data-access procedures. Aim 1 analyses (baseline protein associations with CRC and protein change analyses) occupy months 4–12, providing nine months for the most analytically intensive component, which includes factor analysis, Cox regression with covariate selection, sensitivity analyses for occult disease, effect modification exploration, and marginal structural Cox models. Aim 2 analyses (determinants of protein levels and trajectories) fill months 13–18, with six months for multivariable linear regressions, mixed models, and genetic association analyses across all six proteins. The secondary aim and manuscript preparation share months 19–24, allowing manuscript writing to overlap with the final analytical work. This phasing is sequential but appropriately front-loads the core Aims 1–2 that constitute the main deliverable; the secondary MR aim has a shorter allocation consistent with its exploratory status.

The computing and data resources are adequate. The proposal specifies SAS and Stata for descriptive, survival, mixed-model, and instrumental-variable analyses [7], which are standard tools capable of all planned procedures. The ARIC data infrastructure—including proteomic measurements at up to three visits, GWAS and whole-genome sequencing genotypes, and cancer outcomes through 2015 [7]—already exists and does not require new generation. The investigators access these data through the established ARIC data distribution and governance structure [7].

The proposal does not include an itemized budget, detailed personnel roles, or individual CVs. This limits the evidence for evaluating whether the allocated effort is sufficient for each task and whether all listed expertise is committed at the necessary level. However, the R03 mechanism typically supports the scale of analytical work described, and the combination of a PI with demonstrated ARIC–MICA experience and described collaborators in biostatistics and computational genetics gives reasonable confidence that the core analytical competencies are covered. The principal management consideration—whether the 24-month R03 timeframe can accommodate the methodologically rigorous execution of the marginal structural model (requiring weight model specification, positivity diagnostics, and sensitivity analyses) and the exploratory genetic analyses across six proteins—is a question of analytical ambition within the funded scope rather than a deficiency in the team or resources described.

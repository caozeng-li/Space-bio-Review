# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project combines historical and epidemiologic methods to analyze approximately 15,000 eugenic sterilization recommendations from nine California state institutions between 1921 and 1952. The research asks which demographic groups were disproportionately recommended for sterilization, how those patterns varied across institutions and time, and how patients and families experienced and contested these procedures. The most valuable contribution is the construction of a population-based quantitative account using census microdata to estimate institutionalization and sterilization rates across groups defined by surname, age, gender, nativity, and diagnosis. The qualitative analysis of consent, resistance, and diagnostic language complements these patterns and provides historical depth relevant to contemporary ethical, legal, and social issues in genetics and reproductive health.

The proposal is strongly supported by the quality and scale of its primary data, the demonstrated feasibility of the census-linked analytical approach in a published pilot study, and the complementary expertise of the investigative team in history and epidemiology. The quantitative design, while mostly descriptive and associative, is appropriate to the research question and the historical data’s constraints. The known limitations are well characterized and managed: decennial census snapshots, lack of individual-level 1950 microdata, and the moderate sensitivity of Spanish-surname identification are all acknowledged with plans for constructing comparable variables across heterogeneous sources. The main limitations affecting the judgement are that the qualitative aim, while well-framed, provides less methodological detail than its interpretive claims warrant, and the companion digital archive (Aim 4) is a dissemination pathway dependent on separate external funding rather than a self-contained project deliverable. Neither limitation undermines the core analytical value of Aims 1–3.

The foremost strength is the quantitative infrastructure: a 212-variable REDCap dataset and a census-linked analytical framework that, at the time of the proposal, were new for California’s eugenic sterilization program. The pilot analysis demonstrates a working model, and the subsequent published outcomes of this same project confirm its productivity. These contributions are valuable despite being primarily descriptive and associative rather than causal. The main trade-off concerns scope and attribution: the qualitative aims systematize evidence but largely revisit themes already examined in Stern’s prior scholarship, and the digital archive deliverable is a dependent dissemination pathway rather than an internally funded scientific output. For an R21 exploratory grant, the analytical plan for Aims 1–3 is well-conceived and proportionate. **The project offers a worthwhile contribution and a sufficient route to its main analytical goals, and support is recommended.**

## Scientific value

**The project addresses a historically significant and ethically resonant question with systematic evidence of a kind that was absent for California at the time of the proposal.** California performed approximately 20,000 of the roughly 60,000 documented eugenic sterilizations in the United States during the twentieth century—about one-third of the national total [1]. Despite this scale, detailed empirical study of the populations affected was limited to qualitative narratives and descriptive summaries. This proposal’s primary contribution is the construction of a population-based quantitative account that links roughly 15,000 individual sterilization recommendations from nine state institutions (1921–1952) with census microdata to estimate institutionalization and sterilization rates across demographic groups defined by surname, age, gender, nativity, and diagnosis [1].

The attainable knowledge is both descriptive and contextual. The quantitative aims will describe who was recommended for sterilization, which groups were disproportionately affected, how patterns varied among institutions, superintendents, and periods, and whether associations previously suggested in the qualitative literature—concerning Spanish-surnamed people, teenagers, women, and immigrants—hold when examined with population denominators [1]. The qualitative aim will examine the records’ accompanying correspondence, letters of protest, consent documents, and diagnostic language to illuminate how consent operated under coercive conditions, how families resisted, and how institutional classifications changed over time [1]. This dual approach means the project will produce both population-level patterns and individual-level accounts of experience and contestation, each informing the interpretation of the other [1].

**The research fills a genuine documentary gap.** While descriptive statistics and historical narratives from other jurisdictions had identified important patterns, California’s extensive surviving sterilization records had not previously been studied with systematic demographic methods [1]. The 212-variable REDCap instrument constructed from digitized medical forms, authorizations, rosters, and correspondence represents a uniquely rich data resource [1]. The pilot analysis at Pacific Colony, comparing 1,003 recommendations against 1,068 institutional residents enumerated in the 1940 census, demonstrates the kind of finding the full study can produce: adjusted odds ratios of 2.07 (95% CI 1.65–2.60) for Spanish surname and 3.21 (2.67–3.86) for age under 18, after accounting for gender [1].

A realistic appraisal must acknowledge that the companion digital archive described under Aim 4 is not an independent scientific deliverable of this R21. Its further development is explicitly designated as “a parallel project for separate humanities support” [1]. The substantive knowledge contribution resides in Aims 1–3. This does not diminish the value of the analytical work; it clarifies what the R21 itself will produce.

**The knowledge produced has implications beyond the history of eugenics.** Understanding how coercive reproductive practices were carried out through institutional routines, diagnostic categories, and constrained consent has direct bearing on contemporary discussions of reproductive autonomy, medical mistrust, and the application of genetic and genomic technologies to vulnerable populations [1]. The project’s finding that Latina women experienced particularly disproportionate sterilization—a result subsequently confirmed by Novak et al. 2018—is a concrete example of how historical demographic analysis can inform understanding of structural inequities in public health [2]. **Overall, the project offers a valuable descriptive and contextual contribution to a significant historical question, graded B with high confidence.**

## Innovation

**The proposal introduced a genuine methodological extension beyond the PI’s prior historical scholarship by bringing systematic population-based quantitative analysis to a topic previously examined through qualitative, narrative methods.** At the time of submission, Stern’s published corpus on California eugenic sterilization consisted primarily of the historical monograph *Eugenic Nation* (2005) and the archival study Lira & Stern (2014), both of which analyzed patient resistance, institutional practices, and racialized constructions of “feeblemindedness” without population denominators or formal statistical comparisons [1]. The broader literature on California’s program included Wellerstein’s 2011 descriptive chapter, but no study had linked individual sterilization records with census microdata to estimate institutionalization and sterilization rates across demographic groups. The proposal’s core innovation was the plan to construct census-derived denominators from complete-count microdata (1920, 1930, 1940) and apply Poisson regression to test whether Spanish-surnamed people, teenagers, women, and immigrants faced disproportionate sterilization risk [1].

The methodological novelty is most clearly demonstrated in the pilot analysis. Comparing 1,003 sterilization recommendations from 1935–1944 against 1,068 institutional residents enumerated in the 1940 census yielded adjusted odds ratios of 2.07 for Spanish surname (95% CI 1.65–2.60) and 3.21 for age under 18 (2.67–3.86) [1]. These were the first such adjusted comparisons for California, and they validated an approach that the proposal planned to extend to all nine institutions over the full 1921–1952 period. The proposal correctly distinguished these logistic models from the Poisson regression planned for the expanded population-based work [1].

The subsequent published outcomes of this same project—Novak et al. (2018) in the *American Journal of Public Health* and Kaniecki et al. (2023) in *Social Forces*—confirm the productivity of the approach but also clarify what the proposal itself contributed as a plan. Novak et al. used 17,362 recommendation records linked to census microdata, Poisson regression with a random effect for institution, and Spanish-surname identification to report IRR = 1.59 (95% CI 1.48–1.70) for Latina women and IRR = 1.23 (1.15–1.31) for Latino men [2]. Kaniecki et al. extended the identical census-linked Poisson methods to Asian-born immigrants, reporting IRR = 2.00 (1.61–2.48) for Asian-born women [3]. Both papers acknowledge R21-HG009205-01 funding [3]. These publications are the realized output of the quantitative infrastructure this R21 proposed to build. They should be understood as outcome evidence of the proposal’s productive design, not as prior art that diminishes the proposal’s originality at the time of assessment.

**The qualitative Aim 3 represents a more incremental extension.** The questions it asks—how consent was requested under coercive conditions, how families resisted, how institutional diagnostic language changed over time [1]—are closely aligned with themes already examined in *Eugenic Nation* and Lira & Stern (2014). The approximately 50 handwritten patient letters from Patton State Hospital [1] constitute genuinely new primary material, and the scope across nine institutions represents a systematic extension beyond earlier case-focused analyses. However, the interpretive contribution is primarily one of scale and evidentiary breadth rather than a fundamentally new historical argument.

**Aim 4, the companion digital archive, is not an independent scientific deliverable.** Its further development is explicitly described as “a parallel project for separate humanities support” [1], and the R21 team includes no digital-humanities collaborators. While the archive represents a valuable dissemination pathway, its inclusion as a co-equal aim inflates the apparent scope of the project’s innovation. **The genuine originality resides in the quantitative infrastructure and population-based analyses of Aims 1 and 2, graded B with high confidence.**

## Rigor

**The quantitative design is methodologically sound and well-matched to the descriptive and hypothesis-generating inferences it pursues.** The proposal’s approach—linking approximately 15,000 individual sterilization recommendations with census microdata to estimate rates, applying Poisson regression with institutional population denominators, and using a consistent Spanish-surname indicator across heterogeneous historical sources—is appropriate for the research questions and the constraints of the available data. The design does not claim to establish causal mechanisms; it aims to test demographic associations and generate population-based descriptions that the qualitative analysis can contextualize, and the methods support those inferences [1].

**The principal data sources are well characterized and their limitations are acknowledged explicitly.** The proposal documents which variables are available from which sources and in which years: recommendation records provide individual-level demographic and diagnostic data (with diagnosis available only from 1935 onward), census microdata provide individual-level institutional resident counts for 1920, 1930, and 1940 decennial snapshots, census reports provide tract-level aggregates for 1950, and institutional annual reports supplement these with aggregate population characteristics [1]. The known constraints—decennial rather than annual institutional population counts, the absence of individual-level 1950 microdata, and the need to interpolate between census years—are recognized, and the proposal plans to use institutional reports to fill some of the resulting gaps [1]. These constraints limit precision, particularly for the later years of the program (1946–1952), but do not prevent the identification of broad demographic patterns, which is the project’s central quantitative goal.

**The Spanish-surname identification strategy is defensible and internally consistent.** The proposal plans to apply the same Census Bureau surname list (Word & Perkins 1996) to both recommendation records and census microdata, with manual review for misspellings, so that the indicator used in the numerator and denominator is identical [1]. This addresses the fundamental problem that census racial and ethnic classifications changed substantially across 1920 (Mexican birthplace), 1930 (Mexican racial category), and 1940 (no Mexican category) [1] [4]. The method’s principal limitation—moderate sensitivity, particularly for women due to patrilineal surname transmission—is well characterized in the literature: contemporary validation studies report sensitivity of approximately 82–95% for males and 67–82% for females [5]. High specificity means false positives are rare, and the resulting bias in within-institution comparisons is likely conservative, biasing odds ratios toward the null. The proposal’s stated intent to construct comparable variables rather than assume interchangeability reflects appropriate awareness of these issues [1]. It would be strengthened by a sensitivity analysis comparing surname-based results with alternative identification approaches where available, but this is a refinement rather than a requirement for validity.

**The analytical plan distinguishes clearly between two levels of inference.** Within-institution comparisons (using institutional residents as denominators) will estimate whether particular demographic groups were disproportionately selected for sterilization recommendation among the institutionalized population. Population-level comparisons (using county and state census aggregates approximating catchment areas) will examine whether those same groups were disproportionately institutionalized in the first place [1]. This two-level design is appropriate because institutionalization and sterilization reflect different decision processes, and the proposal does not conflate them. The analytical methods—Poisson regression for rate estimation, logistic regression for the pilot, and descriptive tabulation of temporal and institutional variation—are standard and appropriate for count data with population offsets [1].

**The qualitative plan identifies the right corpus and the right historical questions, but the methodological specification is thin for its interpretive ambitions.** Aim 3 proposes to code forms, correspondence, refusal letters, and consent documents to interpret patient resistance, the meaning of consent, and changing diagnostic categories [1]. The corpus is extraordinary—approximately 50 handwritten patient letters from Patton State Hospital, all cases of documented refusal, and the full set of accompanying correspondence [1]—and the PI brings deep expertise in the relevant historical context. The plan correctly situates the qualitative work as complementary: “Numerical associations and close reading of the documents will jointly support interpretation, rather than reduce all records to demographic counts” [1].

However, the proposal does not specify a coding framework, a document selection logic beyond complete enumeration of refusal cases, procedures for ensuring interpretive reliability, or a concrete workflow for integrating REDCap-structured variables with open-ended qualitative reading. The interpretive claims proposed—about “the meaning and limits of consent” and “patient and family resistance” [1]—are substantively ambitious, and without clearer analytical procedures the findings risk remaining illustrative rather than systematically grounded. For an R21 exploratory grant where the qualitative work serves to contextualize the stronger quantitative aims, this thinness is proportionate but not ideal. **The overall design is well-conceived for an exploratory R21, with the quantitative aims representing the project’s primary epistemic contribution, graded B with high confidence.**

## Feasibility

**The project is demonstrably feasible: the principal data are in hand or immediately accessible, the analytical approach has been successfully piloted, and the investigative team possesses the necessary complementary expertise to execute Aims 1–3 within the 24-month timeline.** The primary risk concerns Aim 4, whose completion depends on external resources beyond the R21’s control; this affects the scope of the deliverable but not the core analytical work.

**The data foundation is already constructed.** The team has located the microfilm reels, digitized the records, obtained approvals from the California Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects and the University of Michigan biomedical IRB, and developed a de-identified, HIPAA-compliant REDCap dataset containing 212 variables for each of approximately 15,000 records [1]. The proposal states that data entry for all records is planned for completion before the main analytical phase begins [1]. The census microdata for 1920, 1930, and 1940 are available through the University of Michigan’s Institute for Social Research, and the team has already used them to construct institutional resident counts for the Pacific Colony pilot [1]. The companion dataset of institutional reports and tract-level 1950 census data is similarly accessible [1]. The data availability table systematically documents which variables are obtainable from which sources in which years, demonstrating that the team has a clear understanding of what can and cannot be constructed [1].

**The pilot analysis at Pacific Colony provides direct proof of feasibility.** Comparing 1,003 sterilization recommendations from 1935–1944 against 1,068 institutional residents enumerated in the 1940 census, the team successfully executed the proposed analytical workflow: extracting institutional population denominators from census microdata, linking them with recommendation records using a consistent Spanish-surname indicator, and estimating adjusted associations via logistic regression [1]. The resulting odds ratios—2.07 for Spanish surname (95% CI 1.65–2.60) and 3.21 for age under 18 (2.67–3.86)—are plausible in magnitude and statistically robust, validating the approach before scaling to the full nine institutions [1]. The proposal correctly notes that the planned Poisson models for the population-based analysis differ from these pilot logistic models, but the underlying data handling, linkage, and variable construction are directly transferable [1].

**The team composition aligns well with the project’s dual quantitative and qualitative demands.** Stern contributes deep historical expertise on eugenics in California. Kardia and Harlow contribute epidemiologic and quantitative expertise, and the existing secure-computing environment at the University of Michigan School of Public Health provides the necessary infrastructure for data management and analysis [1]. The published outcomes from this same team—Novak et al. (2018) and Kaniecki et al. (2023)—confirm that the census linkage and Poisson regression can be carried out at scale [2] [3].

**The 24-month schedule is realistic** for the analytical tasks described [1]. Year 1 covers census companion dataset construction, descriptive statistics, initial hypothesis testing, and qualitative coding; Year 2 continues analyses, examines institution-specific patterns, and prepares publications. The parallel arrangement of quantitative and qualitative work is sensible, allowing emerging quantitative patterns to guide qualitative investigation [1].

**The critical feasibility limitation concerns Aim 4.** The companion digital archive’s further development is explicitly described as “a parallel project for separate humanities support” [1]. The R21 team includes no digital-humanities collaborators, the budget and implementation schedule contain no resources or milestones for Omeka development, and the proposal provides no assurance—such as a letter of collaboration or timeline for the parallel project—that the external humanities effort will proceed. This means Aim 4 is infeasible as an R21 deliverable. **For the core analytical work of Aims 1–3, however, the data, methods, team, and timeline are all in place, and the pilot analysis and subsequent published outcomes provide strong evidence that the work can be carried out successfully. The criterion is graded B with high confidence; material problems affect one aim whose contribution to the project’s scientific value is minimal.**

## Ethics and safety

**The ethical arrangements are thorough, proportionate to the material risks, and supported by the necessary institutional approvals.** This is retrospective research on historical records with no direct contact with living subjects. The principal ethical considerations concern privacy, confidentiality, and the respectful treatment of records documenting a coercive reproductive program that targeted vulnerable populations, including minors.

The proposal documents approval from both the California Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects and the University of Michigan biomedical IRB [1]. The records include identifiable information about adults and children—recorded ages span approximately 7 to 58—and people with intellectual or psychiatric diagnoses [1]. The core mitigation is de-identification: the analytical dataset will separate demographic and clinical variables from identifiers, and the identifier used during data entry will not be included in exported data or reports [1].

**Access controls are multi-layered and well-specified.** Team members must be included on the IRB protocol and complete research-responsibility, confidentiality, and REDCap training before gaining access [1]. Digitized source files reside on a password-protected server and are stored in a locked safe when not in active use [1]. Coded data remain on protected computers, identifiable source information will not be distributed outside the study or published, and any physical transport of records will use a secure carrier [1]. These arrangements reflect standard institutional practice for sensitive historical data and are appropriate to the risks.

**The resource-sharing plan** provides for release of suitable data after acceptance of the principal findings, with identifiers removed and disclosure risk minimized [1]. Requests and distributions will be coordinated and documented by the project manager, using institutional data-use agreements as appropriate. The plan’s commitment to sharing is explicitly subject to the privacy restrictions on the historical records, which is a necessary and appropriate constraint.

No material risks to the environment, animal subjects, or operational safety are present. The research involves no laboratory procedures, field interventions, or direct human contact. **The ethical protections are comprehensive and well-matched to the nature of the research, graded A with high confidence.**

## Management

**The management arrangements are well-constituted for the core analytical work of Aims 1–3, with complementary expertise, adequate resources, and a realistic timeline.** The one structural weakness is the assignment of Aim 4 as a co-equal project aim when its completion depends entirely on externally funded, separately staffed work outside the R21’s control.

**The investigative team brings precisely the expertise the project requires.** Stern, the PI, is a leading historian of eugenics in California with prior archival experience using the same institutional records and published scholarship on Mexican American resistance to sterilization (Lira & Stern 2014) [1]. Kardia and Harlow, the collaborators, contribute epidemiologic and quantitative skills essential for the census-linked Poisson regression, REDCap database management, and demographic analysis that constitute the project’s primary methodological contribution [1]. The existing secure-computing environment at the University of Michigan School of Public Health and the team’s demonstrated experience constructing the 212-variable REDCap instrument provide the necessary infrastructure without requiring new capital investment [1].

**Responsibilities are distributed appropriately.** Stern leads the historical contextualization and qualitative analysis of Aim 3; Kardia and Harlow guide the quantitative design and analysis of Aims 1 and 2 [1]. The proposal states that a project manager will coordinate data requests and distributions under the resource-sharing plan [1], though the manager’s qualifications and allocation are not detailed. For an R21 mechanism where the investigative team itself performs the analytical work, this level of specification is adequate.

**The 24-month schedule is logically structured and realistically paced.** Year 1 (September 2015–August 2016) covers construction of the census companion dataset, descriptive statistics, initial hypothesis testing, and the beginning of qualitative coding. Year 2 (September 2016–August 2017) continues hypothesis testing, examines institution-specific variation, deepens the qualitative analysis, and prepares publications [1]. Dissemination through conferences, lectures, and manuscript submission begins during Year 1 and continues throughout, which is sensible for a project whose analytical findings will emerge incrementally. The parallel arrangement of quantitative and qualitative work—with emerging quantitative patterns guiding qualitative investigation—reflects a deliberate mixed-methods integration rather than a sequential layering of unrelated activities [1].

**The critical management gap concerns Aim 4.** The companion digital archive using Omeka is listed as one of four co-equal aims [1], yet its further development is explicitly designated as “a parallel project for separate humanities support” [1]. The R21 team comprises Stern, Kardia, and Harlow—none of whom are digital-humanities specialists—and the implementation schedule contains no activities, milestones, or resource allocations related to Omeka development [1]. The proposal provides no letter of collaboration from the digital-humanities collaborators, no timeline for their parallel project, and no contingency plan should that separate funding not materialize. The selection of findings, visualizations, and narratives for the archive is a curatorial activity that Aims 1–3 can supply, but the archive’s construction, launch, and maintenance are external dependencies over which the R21 investigative team has no documented authority or resources. **For the core analytical work of Aims 1–3, the team composition, schedule, and institutional resources are sufficient and proportionate to the R21 mechanism, graded B with high confidence.**

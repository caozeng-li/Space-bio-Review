# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project sets out to determine what patients need and prefer when genetic test results are returned electronically through a patient portal, and to translate those findings into iteratively tested, user-centered design prototypes. The planned work involves three integrated steps: qualitative interviews with patients to compare their experiences receiving genetic and non-genetic results (Aim 1), interviews across a purposively sampled range of genetic result types from pharmacogenomic to cancer-risk findings (Aim 2), and three rounds of usability testing of EHR-based report prototypes with both experienced portal users and non-users (Aim 3). Throughout the process, an ethical “Points to Consider” framework will be iteratively refined. The most valuable expected knowledge is a systematic, evidence-based account of patient-defined thresholds and support needs for electronic genetic result return, delivered in the form of concrete, shareable, and ethically informed design templates. This will directly address a major practical gap in the implementation of genomic medicine.

The proposal is strongly supported by a well-reasoned research design and a team with directly relevant expertise and prior work. The within-subject comparison in Aim 1 is a particularly apt design choice for isolating genetic-result-specific needs, and the iterative UCD process in Aim 3, which includes the often-neglected population of portal non-users, is a strength. The management structure, with its multi-tiered advisory board and well-matched external consultants, inspires confidence. The main limitations that materially affect the judgment are: first, the 10-participant-per-category allocation in Aim 2, while adequate for formative theme identification, provides only a thin basis for cross-category comparison; second, the problem-discovery-rate claims for Aim 3 over-extend the cited statistical model, though the actual sample size remains appropriate for formative refinement. Furthermore, the single-institution, English-language scope bounds the immediate generalizability of the findings, a limit the proposal acknowledges.

Weighing the evidence, **the plan is sound for an exploratory R21 and merits support.** The core study design—systematic qualitative comparison feeding iterative UCD—is genuinely well aligned with the question, and the team has the expertise to execute it. The scientific value of the resulting knowledge, while bounded, is clear and practical. The main limitations are the thin per-category sample in Aim 2 and the overstated confidence in the usability sample size calculations for Aim 3. These factors, however, do not undermine the project’s capacity to deliver on its formative goals; they define the appropriate scope of the conclusions. The ambitious diversity targets and the novel non-user recruitment introduce feasibility risk, but the project has reasonable contingency plans. The plan’s strengths in design, ethical grounding, and management provide a sufficient route to the stated aims, and the deliverables will be a useful, empirically grounded foundation for later-stage clinical and multi-site studies.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**The project addresses a practical and timely question with concrete, useful deliverables, though its knowledge claims are appropriately bounded by its formative, single-institution scope.** The core scientific value lies in producing systematic, patient-grounded evidence on what information and design features people need when genetic test results are returned electronically, and translating that evidence directly into iteratively tested, shareable EHR report prototypes. This addresses a gap that is both clinically pressing—as regulatory requirements for electronic access and the growth of genomic medicine converge—and under-explored in the specific form proposed.

The question the project tackles is well-motivated. Patient portals increasingly provide electronic access to clinical test results, yet genetic findings can carry implications for disease risk, family members, and treatment decisions that standard laboratory result displays are not designed to convey. The proposal correctly observes that genetic results are heterogeneous: a pharmacogenomic finding, a hereditary cancer risk result, and a negative test outcome each raise distinct concerns and support needs [1]. At the same time, portal design that optimizes only for existing frequent users may exclude populations with lower technology access or health literacy [1]. Systematically investigating what patients themselves identify as necessary—rather than importing clinician assumptions about sensitivity—is a sound research premise. The PI’s prior work already demonstrated that clinicians from multiple specialties disagree about which results merit personal return [1]; the proposed patient-centered inquiry is a logical and necessary next step.

The expected knowledge output has several dimensions of practical value. First, the within-subject comparison of genetic and non-genetic portal experiences (Aim 1) can isolate needs that are specific to genetic information rather than reflecting general portal usability frustrations. Second, the purposive sampling across pharmacogenomic, hemochromatosis, colorectal cancer risk, and negative findings (Aim 2) will generate evidence on how concern thresholds and support needs vary across result types—evidence that can directly guide decisions about what accompanying information, follow-up prompts, and delivery mode each result category warrants. Third, the iterative prototype development and testing (Aim 3) will produce concrete design artifacts—downloadable Epic-style and “blue-sky” templates—that health systems can adapt, rather than leaving implementers with only a list of abstract recommendations. The inclusion of portal non-users in usability testing is a specific strength: it surfaces barriers that might otherwise be invisible in a design process centered on experienced users and directly informs the justice and equity domain of the Points to Consider framework [1].

The value is materially constrained by the English-only, single-institution scope. The proposal restricts participation to English speakers because eCare is available only in English at UW Medicine and identifies multilingual report development as future work [1]. This is a legitimate sequencing choice for an exploratory R21, but it means the findings on patient preferences, and the prototypes built from them, will be validated only for English-literate populations within one urban academic health system. Preferences are shaped by cultural frameworks, health literacy, and trust in medical institutions that vary across communities; the large survey by Bruno et al. (2022) found that preferences for electronic return of genetic results differed significantly by income and education [2]. The Harborview safety-net recruitment partially addresses socioeconomic diversity within the English-speaking population, but the knowledge claim is explicitly bounded. The project appropriately positions its outputs as “a basis for subsequent clinical and implementation studies” [1] rather than as broadly generalizable findings, and this is the right framing for the attainable contribution.

## Innovation

**The proposal extends the PI’s own published work in an incremental but well-grounded way; its most distinctive contribution is the integrated combination of comparative qualitative interviews and iterative prototype testing across result types, within a design that includes the often-neglected portal non-user population.** The novelty of each component, and of the synthesis as a whole, must be assessed relative to both the PI’s prior studies and the broader landscape of patient-centered genomic report development.

The PI’s own 2022 paper, based on 59 interviews at UW Medicine, already classified genetic results as “fraught,” “moderately fraught,” and “not fraught,” compared genetic and non-genetic portal experiences, and recommended coversheet-plus-detailed-report formats developed through user-centered design methods [3]. The proposal is explicit that its starting point is the finding that clinicians disagreed about result sensitivity, and that patient-defined thresholds must be investigated [1]. Aims 1 and 2 are therefore best understood as a logical, direct extension of this established research program, not as an entirely new departure.

Three features distinguish the current plan from the 2022 study. First, Aim 1 provides a dedicated within-subject comparison of genetic versus non-genetic results; the earlier work made such comparisons for only about half the sample [3]. Second, Aim 2 systematically samples across four specific genetic result categories with 10 participants each, enabling structured cross-category comparison that the earlier study did not attempt [1]. Third, Aim 3 introduces iterative prototype development and usability testing, which the 2022 paper explicitly called for as a next step [3].

Relative to work from other groups, the proposal’s specific synthesis is not duplicated in any single published study. Several projects have applied user-centered design to genetic result return—MyCancerGene used a multi-step process for a cancer-focused portal [4], Genetics Adviser developed a comprehensive digital application for pre-test education through post-test return [5], and the Geisinger team built and deployed a patient-facing genomic test report within an Epic EHR [6]. However, none of these systematically compared genetic to non-genetic results within subjects, purposefully sampled across a pharmacogenomic–hemochromatosis–cancer-risk–negative taxonomy, or incorporated a dedicated non-user cohort in iterative usability testing. The within-system, within-portal comparison across four result types with equal per-category sampling is the proposal’s most distinctive empirical feature. The combination of Epic-style and “blue-sky” prototypes—one addressing practical compatibility with a widely deployed platform, the other permitting designs unconstrained by current EHR limitations [1]—adds a pragmatic dimension absent from most prior work.

The Points to Consider framework is the element with the strongest claim to novelty but the weakest specification. No published study has iteratively refined an explicit ethical framework for portal-based genetic result return using patient data. The initial tripartite structure—beneficence/nonmaleficence, respect for autonomy, justice/equity [1]—is, however, a standard bioethics checklist, and the proposal does not specify what form the refined framework will take. Its value will depend on how specifically the iterative process translates empirical findings into actionable guidance, a question the project is designed to answer.

The English-only, single-institution scope bounds the novelty claim, as the proposal acknowledges and defers to future work [1]. For an R21 exploratory mechanism, this level of incremental but carefully reasoned advance is proportionate: the project extends a demonstrated research line with systematic design choices that no prior study has combined, while remaining appropriately modest about the reach of its findings.

## Rigor

**The qualitative and usability designs are well-matched to the project’s formative goals, though two specific limitations narrow the strength of the conclusions that can be drawn: the 10-participant-per-category allocation in Aim 2 provides a thin basis for cross-category comparison, and the problem-discovery-rate claims for Aim 3 over-extend the cited statistical model.**

**Aim 1 within-subject comparison is a well-chosen design for isolating genetic-result-specific needs.** By interviewing the same participants about both their genetic and non-genetic results received through the same portal, the design allows participants to articulate contrasts directly, rather than confounding individual differences with result-type effects [1]. This is a stronger approach than recruiting separate groups. The planned semistructured protocol, piloting, dual coding with consensus resolution, and Atlas.ti-supported analysis follow accepted qualitative practice [1]. The target of up to 40 participants, with age, gender, and race/ethnicity stratification [1], provides reasonable headroom for achieving code saturation while ensuring that the design process hears from participants across demographic categories, not only the most frequent portal users [1]. The proposal’s plan to continue recruitment until saturation is reached provides a defensible stopping rule for exploratory research.

**Aim 2’s 10-participant-per-category sampling is adequate for within-category description but limited for between-category inference.** The categories—pharmacogenomic, hemochromatosis, colorectal cancer risk, and negative results—are deliberately chosen to vary along a concern-and-complexity continuum, and the proposal appropriately treats them as “starting points, not fixed classifications” [1]. The qualitative saturation literature indicates that code saturation can occur as early as nine interviews in relatively homogeneous samples with focused questions [7], so identifying themes within each category is feasible. However, with 10 participants per category, apparent differences between categories—for example, pharmacogenomic recipients emphasizing medication relevance while cancer-risk recipients emphasize family communication—may reflect the particular individuals recruited rather than genuine category effects. The proposal’s language—“explore concern thresholds, information needs, counseling needs” [1]—is appropriately tentative.

A further consideration is that saturation is not formally assessed within demographic strata, despite explicit diversity targets. The stratification ensures representation but does not guarantee that subgroup-specific saturation is reached. For an exploratory R21, this is a manageable limitation: the primary purpose is formative theme generation to inform prototype design, not population-level comparison.

**The Aim 3 usability testing follows a standard iterative UCD process with appropriate design choices.** The inclusion of both portal users and non-users (42 each) is a genuine strength: non-users provide a novice perspective and help surface equity concerns that experienced user testing would miss [1]. The three-round structure with fresh participants per round supports genuine iterative improvement, and the inclusion of external consultant heuristic walkthroughs between rounds 2 and 3 adds expert review complementary to user testing [1]. The in-person cognitive interview format with think-aloud task completion is standard practice for eliciting comprehension, navigation difficulties, and content preferences [1].

**However, the proposal overstates the precision of its sample size rationale.** The proposal claims “a minimum discovery rate above 98%, with a mean of 99.6%, for each participant type” [1], citing Faulkner (2003). Faulkner’s study tested 60 participants on a single interface, finding that 20 users yielded a minimum 95% and mean 98.4% problem discovery, while 30 users yielded 97% minimum and 99.0% mean [8]. The proposal’s design differs from these conditions in three important ways: participants see only 3–4 of 6–8 prototypes, so the model applies per prototype, not across the set; the 14 participants of a single type (users or non-users) correspond to approximately 90% minimum and roughly 97% mean discovery under Faulkner’s model, not the claimed “above 98%”; and the model assumes all participants evaluate the same interface. More fundamentally, Faulkner herself cautioned that “each usability problem has its own probability of being found, due to factors such as severity, user traits, product type, level of structure in the test, and the number of users tested” and concluded that “the only clear answer to valid usability testing is that the test users must be representative of the target population” [8].

For formative purposes, the actual sample size—approximately 10–14 evaluations per prototype per round, accumulating to roughly 30–42 across three rounds—is adequate for identifying common usability issues and iteratively refining design. The proposal would be more accurate in stating that the design can detect prevalent usability problems, rather than claiming specific discovery percentages that the model does not fully support under the multi-prototype, split-user-type conditions.

**Ecological validity is appropriately scoped.** The in-person cognitive interviews with laptop prototypes [1] cannot reproduce several features of real portal-based result return: the private home setting, the personal stakes of learning about one’s own genetic risk, the self-directed nature of portal use, and the emotional context of receiving a result notification. These limitations are well-recognized in the field. The proposal itself acknowledges that subsequent work would involve “studies of patient engagement and health outcomes” and that “those later evaluations are distinct from the present formative work” [1]. What the design can conclude—which content elements, navigation structures, and language choices patients find clear, useful, and acceptable—is precisely what the formative stage requires.

## Feasibility

**The two-year schedule is broadly credible, supported by direct evidence from the PI’s own prior work in the same health system, though three concentrated risks merit attention: the compressed prototype-development window, the recruitment of non-portal-users from a safety-net clinic, and the underspecified Epic compatibility review.**

**The generative research timeline is well-supported by prior evidence.** The schedule allocates roughly four quarters to Aims 1 and 2 and four quarters to Aim 3 [1]. The PI’s 2022 study recruited 59 participants from UW Medicine using the same informatics pipeline and completed all interviews in four months [3], providing direct evidence that the Aim 1 and 2 data-collection timelines—two quarters each—are realistic. The prior work used the same Institute for Translational Health Sciences bioinformatics research service, contacted patients by email or phone up to three times, and achieved stratified sampling goals within a single summer [3]. The proposed 80 participants across Aims 1 and 2 represent a slightly larger but comparable recruitment effort within a similar window.

**The Aim 3 schedule is more ambitious.** The two-quarter block allocated to prototype creation and round 1 testing (Q5–Q6) requires creating six to eight prototypes spanning Epic-style and blue-sky designs across multiple result categories, piloting protocols, recruiting 28 participants, and conducting in-person cognitive interviews [1]. Analysis of Aim 2 extends into Q5, overlapping with prototype creation; designers will need to begin work with preliminary rather than finalized findings. The later rounds (Q6–Q8) are better-supported: recruiting 28 new participants per round across four quarters is modest relative to the prior demonstrated recruitment pace.

**Non-user recruitment from Harborview is the most significant feasibility risk.** The proposal targets 42 non-portal-users from Harborview Medical Center’s Family Medicine Clinic, a safety-net setting with relatively low eCare enrollment [1]. Recruiting non-users for in-person interviews about a portal-based tool confronts two hurdles: identifying patients who do not use the portal is inherently harder through EHR-based informatics queries, and motivating participation in a study about a system they do not use requires different outreach strategies. The proposal’s approach—“direct clinic approaches”—is sensible but slower than informatics-based recruitment, and the proposal does not provide enrollment-rate estimates or contingency plans specific to this population. This is the weakest-supported element of the recruitment plan.

**Diversity targets are more demanding than what the prior study achieved.** The 2022 sample was 73% White and 66% female, and the authors noted that underrepresented groups may tend to be non-users or not undergo genetic testing [3]. The proposal’s contingency—that this pattern would “identify questions about equitable access for future study” [1]—is appropriate for an exploratory R21 but means diversity data may be thinner than the stratification table implies [1].

**Two dependencies are underspecified.** The Epic staff compatibility review is mentioned only briefly: “Epic staff will assess compatibility of Epic-style reports” [1]. This is a consequential dependency because Epic compatibility constraints could fundamentally limit which prototype features are implementable. The proposal does not specify when this review occurs or how long it is expected to take; if conducted only after prototypes are built, its findings could require substantial rework with limited schedule margin. The advisory board, while well-constituted [1], will meet only twice yearly [1], yielding approximately four meetings across the project without explicit timing relative to key decision points. These are manageable risks—the PI’s direct prior experience with the same institutional infrastructure, the advisory board’s availability for consultation as needed, and the external consultants’ heuristic walkthroughs between rounds 2 and 3 all provide mitigating capacity—but they represent points where schedule margin is thin.

## Ethics and safety

**The ethical framework and human-subjects protections are sound and proportionate for this exploratory, minimal-risk study. The Points to Consider framework is well-grounded in standard bioethical principles with a defensible plan for iterative empirical refinement. Participant protections address the identified risks with one notable gap: the distress protocol lacks specificity about referral pathways.**

**Framework structure and grounding.** The initial Points to Consider framework is organized under three classical domains—beneficence and nonmaleficence, respect for autonomy, and justice and equity—with each domain containing concrete screening questions relevant to electronic return of genetic results [1]. The questions address worry, family implications, preferred delivery modes, informed decisions, cultural sensitivity, technology access, health literacy, and equitable resource use. This tripartite structure aligns with the principlist approach used in prior ethical analyses of genetic result return, including the eMERGE/CSER consensus framework co-authored by team member Fullerton and advisory board member Jarvik [9]. The framework’s value as currently specified is as an organizing and screening tool—the questions are sensible but generic and could be asked about many forms of health information disclosure. What distinguishes it from a standard checklist is the planned empirical enrichment: the questions are starting points whose answers will be shaped by the interview and usability data collected across all three aims [1].

**Iterative refinement process.** The design sequence creates multiple feedback loops between empirical findings and normative analysis—generative interviews (Aims 1 and 2) inform both the framework and the prototypes; usability testing (Aim 3) feeds back into both the design and the ethical reasoning embedded in it [1]. The inclusion of portal non-users in Aim 3 ensures that the justice and equity domain is pressure-tested against the perspectives of those who might be most disadvantaged by electronic-only approaches [1]. The external consultant heuristic walkthroughs provide an additional independent check on the ethical reasoning [1]. The team includes Fullerton, who brings specific expertise in genomic bioethics, and the mentoring committee includes Wylie Burke, a senior bioethicist [1], providing the normative competence needed. The proposal does not specify a formal method for translating empirical findings into normative revisions, though in practice qualitative empirical bioethics typically proceeds through reflective equilibrium, and the team appears equipped to do so.

**Participant protections: privacy and data security are thorough.** The project will seek a HIPAA waiver for identifying eligible patients and an IRB waiver of written consent documentation; consent discussions will cover procedures, confidentiality, voluntary participation, risks, and the absence of direct clinical benefit [1]. Transcripts will be de-identified, incentive administration data stored separately, study data kept on secure servers with restricted access, and data destroyed at study close [1]. The proposal identifies relevant risks: discomfort discussing inherited risk, anxiety about audio recording, possible recognition during waiting-room recruitment, and inadvertent disclosure of information about relatives [1]. The reminder to participants not to provide identifying information about third parties, with removal of such details if supplied, is a thoughtful measure [1].

**Distress protocol gap.** The proposal states that participants experiencing distress “will be encouraged to discontinue and directed to assistance if needed” [1]. For Aim 2 participants who received inherited colorectal cancer risk results, discussion of family implications could evoke significant distress. The proposal does not identify what “assistance” entails—a referral to a genetic counselor, a crisis resource, or a primary care provider—nor the threshold for activating that referral. This gap is manageable given that participants are discussing results already received, not learning new clinical findings, and no incidental findings are expected from this interview and prototype study [1]. Nonetheless, specifying a referral pathway in the protocol would strengthen the protections.

**English-only scope as an equity consideration.** The restriction to English-speaking participants because “eCare is available in English at UW Medicine” [1] is a genuine equity limitation. Language barriers compound health literacy and technology access barriers, and the justice and equity domain of the framework itself will be informed only by English-speaking participants. The proposal acknowledges this candidly and identifies multilingual report development as planned future work [1]. For an exploratory R21, this sequencing—developing and testing prototypes in one language before investing in cross-cultural adaptation—is methodologically defensible. The inclusion of the Harborview safety-net clinic with substantial minority representation [1] and the stratified recruitment targets [1] demonstrate a genuine effort to capture diverse perspectives within the practical constraints.

## Management

**The management plan is well-conceived and proportionate to the scope of an R21 exploratory grant, with a capable investigative team, a well-structured advisory apparatus, and appropriately formative deliverables. The principal gap is the unspecified assignment of transcription and coding tasks, which represents a manageable rather than critical oversight given the institutional resources available.**

**The investigative team covers the necessary domains.** Korngiebel contributes demonstrated expertise in formative research, qualitative interviewing, and user-centered design, directly established by her 2022 study that recruited 59 participants from the same UW Medicine system using comparable methods [1] [3]. Robins brings extensive qualitative methods experience, essential for the directed content and thematic analysis across Aims 1 and 2 [1]. Fullerton contributes genomic bioethics, grounding the initial Points to Consider framework and its iterative refinement [1]. Amendola provides genetic counseling and clinical sequencing experience, ensuring that result categories and report content remain clinically accurate [1]. No critical domain is obviously missing from the investigative team for the proposed work.

**The three-tier advisory structure is a genuine strength.** The advisory board of five named members spans Harborview Family Medicine (Frederick Chen, providing the safety-net clinical perspective essential for Aim 3 recruitment), UW ambulatory IT (Jane Fellner, covering Epic/eCare implementation knowledge), medical genetics (Gail Jarvik), laboratory medicine informatics and genetics (Brian Shirts), and external biomedical informatics (Josh Peterson from Vanderbilt, bringing eMERGE network experience) [1].

The electronic prototype consultants—Brandon Welch (MUSC) and Marc Williams (Geisinger)—are particularly well-chosen. Williams led the Geisinger team that developed and deployed a patient-facing genomic test report within an Epic EHR using patient-centered design [6], providing the closest direct precedent for this project’s Aim 3. These consultants will conduct heuristic walkthroughs between rounds 2 and 3, adding expert evaluation complementary to the patient think-aloud testing [1]. The mentoring committee—Wylie Burke (bioethics), Peter Tarczy-Hornoch and Jan Carline (biomedical informatics and medical education)—provides senior mentorship in bioethics and informatics [1]. In total, ten advisors span the three groups, eight of whom are clinicians [1], ensuring clinical relevance throughout. The board will meet twice yearly [1]; while this yields approximately four meetings across the two-year project, the availability for ad hoc consultation provides flexibility at key decision points.

**Graduate assistant support is adequate for the specified role, with some tasks unspecified.** The proposal states that “Korngiebel and a graduate assistant will revise the prototypes before the next round” [1], which is appropriate for a graduate assistant working under supervision. However, the proposal does not specify who will perform transcription of up to 80 hour-long interviews or who the two coders referenced in the analysis plan will be—they could be Korngiebel and Robins (who lead the analysis), the graduate assistant plus one investigator, or another combination [1]. The University of Washington is a major research institution where graduate assistants and research coordinators routinely handle transcription and coding, and the R21 mechanism typically supports modest personnel resources. The ambiguity is a minor gap rather than a fatal concern, resolvable during project startup.

**Deliverables are appropriately formative and properly scoped.** The proposal identifies downloadable prototypes (approximately six to eight, including both Epic-style and blue-sky designs), the iteratively refined Points to Consider framework, and publications describing how patients use and wish to use portals for genomic information [1]. These are clearly distinguished from subsequent work—“clinician and technical co-design, multilingual report sets, implementation in multiple settings, and studies of patient engagement and health outcomes”—explicitly identified as “distinct from the present formative work” [1]. This scope discipline is appropriate: an R21 is designed for exploratory and developmental research that establishes feasibility and generates hypotheses. The deliverables align with the project’s acknowledged limitations, particularly the English-only, single-institution scope, and correctly position the prototypes as “a basis for subsequent clinical and implementation studies” [1] rather than as deployment-ready templates.

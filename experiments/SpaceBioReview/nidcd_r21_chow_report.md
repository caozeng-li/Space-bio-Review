# Project review

## Overall assessment

This R21 proposal aims to use fMRI with a continuous-speech paradigm to study brain activity in children aged 5–11 who stutter persistently (pCWS), have recovered from stuttering (rCWS), and do not stutter (CWNS). The planned work uses spatial independent component analysis (sICA) to remove speech-related motion artifacts from BOLD signals, combined with a mixed block/event GLM that separates sustained speech activity from transient stuttering-event responses \[证据2, 证据4\]. The central hypothesis is that persistence is associated with anomalous activity in left-hemisphere speech-motor circuits, whereas recovery involves less anomalous left perisylvian activity and greater right-frontal involvement [1]. The immediate purpose is to distinguish neural activity accompanying fluent speech from activity associated with individual stuttering instances, and to characterize recovery. The theoretical framework distinguishes a left frontotemporal pathway for mapping and monitoring speech, a right-frontal compensatory pathway, and motor execution involving supplementary motor area (SMA), basal ganglia, and motor cortex [1].

The most valuable expected knowledge is the first functional characterization of recovered children during continuous speech—testing whether recovery involves normalization of left-hemisphere speech-motor circuits or compensatory right-hemisphere recruitment—and the first direct childhood data on neural activity during stuttering events. These would complement the team's existing structural MRI findings and extend their published functional work, which included only persistent and control children.

The proposal is strongly supported by the team's demonstrated expertise and published feasibility data. Chow et al. (2023) successfully acquired analyzable continuous-speech fMRI from 40 children aged 5–12 using nearly identical methods, and the team has direct access to an established longitudinal cohort with well-characterized persistence and recovery status \[证据59, 证据25\]. The sICA denoising pipeline has been validated against PET \[证据171, 证据14\], and the theoretical framework is grounded in both adult and childhood structural findings [1]. The principal limitations are: (1) the cross-sectional design cannot establish developmental markers of persistence or recovery, as the proposal acknowledges [1], and treatment history is a confound for which only ad-hoc covariate inclusion is planned; (2) power calculations are based on adult effect sizes and are optimistic for the childhood application, particularly for the smaller recovery effects in Aim 2; and (3) the team's own 2023 study found that most CWS produce fewer than 10 in-scanner stuttering events, directly challenging the feasibility of the event-related analysis in Aim 1b, and scanner noise may suppress stuttering more than the estimated 25% \[证据82, 证据224\].

These limitations do not negate the study's value, but they constrain the inferential strength and the scope of the event analysis. The design can still yield valuable group comparisons for Aims 1a and 2 even if the event analysis is reduced in scope, though the inferences will be more descriptive than mechanistic. On balance, this is a worthwhile exploratory study whose main limitations are well-understood and largely disclosed by the proposers; the plan is appropriate for an R21 mechanism and would advance the field, but the framing of “neural markers of persistence and recovery” should be tempered to reflect what cross-sectional functional contrasts can demonstrate.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

**The central scientific contribution is genuinely valuable: the project would provide the first functional neuroimaging data during continuous speech from children who have recovered from stuttering, directly testing whether recovery involves normalization of left-hemisphere speech-motor circuits or compensatory right-hemisphere recruitment.** This question matters because approximately 80% of children who begin stuttering recover, yet the neural mechanisms underlying this favourable outcome remain unknown at the functional level \[证据8, 证据12\].

Structural imaging from the same longitudinal cohort has already shown that recovered children display increased white-matter growth rates in tracts connecting speech-motor structures, leading the team to suggest that “recovery from stuttering was primarily associated with increased growth rate of white matter volume in tracts connecting these structures, suggesting that these increases reflect a process of compensation” [2]. However, structural measures describe anatomical connectivity without revealing whether a pathway is functionally engaged during speech, under what conditions, or whether its activity relates to stuttering events versus fluent intervals. Functional imaging adds the capacity to distinguish activity during fluent speech from activity during stuttering events within the same individual, test whether right-hemisphere structures that are structurally intact in recovered children are actually functionally recruited during speech, and determine whether the left perisylvian activity reduction seen in persistent children normalizes with recovery. The proposed functional data would therefore complement existing structural findings and could directly inform theories of plasticity that have long-term relevance to intervention development.

The inclusion of a recovered group (rCWS) is the single most important advance over the team's own published work. Chow et al. (2023) successfully acquired continuous-speech fMRI from 22 children with persistent stuttering (pCWS) and 18 controls, demonstrating reduced left ventral premotor cortex activation in pCWS during spontaneous speech [3]. However, that study explicitly noted that “it remains unknown if some of the CWS may have recovered from stuttering after participating in this study” [3]. No published fMRI study using continuous speech has compared recovered and persistent children. The Hosseini et al. (2018) study did include recovered children but employed fNIRS, which lacks whole-brain coverage and cannot access the subcortical speech-motor structures—putamen, thalamus, SMA—that are central to the theoretical framework \[证据149, 证据8\]. The three-group design (pCWS, rCWS, CWNS) would therefore fill a well-defined gap.

The second contribution resides in making stuttering-event-related activity a primary target (Aim 1b) rather than a nuisance regressor. The Chow et al. (2023) study treated stuttering moments as an effect of no interest because “the majority of the participants produced relatively few stuttering moments (\\\<10) in the scanner” and “the statistical power for analyzing stuttering-related activity may not be sufficient” [3]. The adult preliminary data showing increased SMA, putamen, and ventral premotor activity during stuttering events [1] provide a specific, testable hypothesis to test in children. If successful, this would be the first direct characterization of neural activity during stuttering moments in childhood—a state that has only been inferred from adult studies.

These contributions must be calibrated against two constraints. First, the cross-sectional design can identify neural correlates that differ among groups but cannot establish these correlates as developmental markers of persistence or recovery processes, as the proposal itself acknowledges [1]. The study would therefore produce descriptive group contrasts rather than mechanistic evidence about how recovery unfolds. Second, the Aim 1a comparison of fluent speech between pCWS and CWNS has already been published by the same team in 2023, which found reduced left ventral premotor cortex activation in pCWS during spontaneous speech \[证据62, 证据124\]. The proposed replication with a larger sample and additional covariates would be confirmatory rather than novel.

Nevertheless, the recovered-group data and the stuttering-event analysis represent attainable knowledge that no published study has provided. The longer-term objective of informing interventions is appropriately positioned as aspirational. Within the acknowledged limits of a cross-sectional design and an R21 exploratory mechanism, the knowledge contribution is well-defined, clinically relevant, and demonstrably beyond what currently exists in the literature.

## Innovation

**The proposal makes two genuine advances beyond published work—including recovered children and targeting stuttering-event activity—but these are logical extensions of the team's own findings rather than conceptual breakthroughs.** The work extends the applicants' 2023 continuous-speech fMRI study in specific, well-defined directions, and the framing of “markers” slightly overstates what a cross-sectional functional design provides given that the structural literature has already suggested right-hemisphere involvement in recovery.

The most original element is the inclusion of a recovered group (rCWS) in continuous-speech fMRI. As noted above, Chow et al. (2023) studied 22 pCWS and 18 controls but could not address recovery because they lacked a recovered comparison group [3]. The Hosseini et al. (2018) study included a recovered group but used fNIRS, which lacks whole-brain coverage and subcortical access [4]. The Ren et al. (2024) meta-analysis confirms that developmental differences between childhood and adult stuttering—left-hemisphere hypoactivation in children versus right-hemisphere hyperactivation in adults—are a critical emerging theme [5]. The three-group comparison in Aim 2 therefore addresses a question that the field has identified as important but has not answered: where do recovered children fall along this developmental continuum?

The second advance is elevating stuttering-event-related activity from a nuisance regressor to a primary analysis target (Aim 1b). In Chow et al. (2023), stuttering moments were modeled as an effect of no interest because most participants produced fewer than 10 events [3]. The adult preliminary work showing increased putamen, SMA, and ventral premotor activity time-locked to stuttering events [1] provides a specific, testable hypothesis for children. Distinguishing state effects (transient stuttering-event activity) from trait effects (sustained group differences during fluent speech) is a conceptually important separation that the mixed block/event GLM is designed to achieve [1]. Connally et al. (2018) demonstrated that trait, state, and frequency effects in stuttering are spatially dissociable in adults—a finding that reinforces the value of attempting this separation [6]. If successful, Aim 1b would provide the first direct characterization of neural activity during childhood stuttering moments rather than relying on inferences from adult data or from blocked analyses that confound stuttered and fluent speech.

These advances are nonetheless incremental in two respects. First, the Aim 1a comparison of fluent speech between pCWS and CWNS has already been demonstrated with nearly identical methods by the same team \[证据62, 证据124\]; the proposal would replicate this with a larger sample and additional covariates, but the core finding is published.

Second, the hypotheses about recovery are inherited from structural findings rather than representing a novel conceptual direction. The structural imaging literature from this longitudinal cohort (Chang et al., 2015; Chow & Chang, 2017) has already shown that recovered children exhibit greater right inferior frontal white-matter coherence [1] and increased growth rates in speech-motor white matter tracts, suggesting compensation rather than normalization [2]. Similarly, the finding that adult stuttering involves right frontal hyperactivation whereas childhood stuttering does not was explicitly reported in Chow et al. (2023): “This negative result in CWS indicates that the hyperactivation in the right hemisphere observed in AWS may develop later in life and reflect a maladaptive or unsuccessful compensation process” [3]. The proposal extends this by asking whether recovered children show the adult-like right-hemisphere pattern or a normalized left-hemisphere pattern, which is a valuable refinement of an existing hypothesis rather than a new discovery direction.

| Element | Published basis | What this proposal adds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pCWS vs. CWNS fluent speech (Aim 1a) | Reduced left vPMC in pCWS [3] | Replication, larger sample, linguistic covariates |
| Stuttering events (Aim 1b) | Treated as nuisance; \\\<10 events typical [3] | Primary analysis target with specific hypotheses |
| Recovery comparison (Aim 2) | Structural evidence of right IFG compensation [2] | First functional characterization during continuous speech |

The innovation lies not in identifying new questions but in providing the first functional data to test hypotheses that structural imaging has generated and to characterize a population—recovered children—that functional neuroimaging has not yet examined with adequate methods. In an R21 context, where the mechanism is designed for exploratory, hypothesis-generating work that extends prior findings, this incremental advance is appropriate and scientifically valuable.

## Rigor

**The cross-sectional design is appropriate for identifying group-level neural correlates and can yield valuable comparisons for Aims 1a and 2, but the strength of inference is limited by the design's inability to separate developmental processes from group membership, by an under-specified treatment of treatment history, and by optimistic power estimates drawn from adult data.** The proposal itself acknowledges the central limitation: “This design characterizes current group differences; future longitudinal work would track the development of those differences” [1]. Under this honest framing, the study would document neural states associated with persistence and recovery outcomes rather than establishing how those states develop. This is a legitimate scientific contribution for an R21 exploratory mechanism, but the proposal's language of “markers of persistence and recovery” should be understood as describing correlates rather than validated developmental biomarkers.

**The design includes genuine methodological strengths.** Analysts will be blinded to diagnoses [1], reducing expectation bias. Permutation procedures will control multiple-comparison false positives [1], appropriate for the whole-brain analyses planned. The sICA denoising pipeline has been validated against PET for continuous speech \[证据171, 证据14\], and the preliminary work on 50 CWS and 45 CWNS demonstrates that DVARS remained below approximately 1% for movements below 9 mm after denoising [1]. Speech coding using two independent speech-language pathologists with acoustic and visual event identification [1] provides a principled basis for the event regressors. The inclusion of detailed linguistic measures—speech rate, utterance length, semantic diversity (TTR, VocD), and syntactic complexity (DSS, IPSYN)—as covariates [1] addresses potential linguistic confounds that could differ between groups, an improvement over the 2023 study. The within-subject contrast in Aim 1b (stuttered vs. fluent speech within pCWS) partially addresses the design's limitation by isolating state effects—activity specifically associated with stuttering events—from the sustained trait differences that between-group comparisons measure. Connally et al. (2018) demonstrated that trait, state, and stuttering-frequency effects are spatially dissociable in adults who stutter [6]. The mixed block/event GLM [1] is a thoughtful approach to making this separation, explicitly modeling sustained block-level speech activity separately from transient event-related stuttering responses.

**Treatment history is a confound whose analytic treatment is insufficiently specified.** The proposal states that treatment history will be recorded [1] and included as a covariate [1], but the nature of the covariate model is not described. This matters because recovered children are more likely to have received treatment, and fluency-shaping intervention itself alters functional brain activity. Neef et al. (2021) showed that an intensive fluency-shaping intervention modulated functional connectivity of the dorsal laryngeal motor cortex network, with the observation that “the structural dLMC network is unaffected in stuttering, but recruited by stuttering intervention suggests a compensatory involvement of these networks in assisted recovery” [7]. If treatment alters the very functional measures that Aim 2 uses to characterize recovery, then any difference between rCWS and pCWS could reflect treatment-related neural changes, spontaneous recovery processes, or their interaction. Including “treatment type” as a simple covariate with N=20 per group is unlikely to adequately model the widely varying nature, intensity, duration, and timing of treatment across children. Sensitivity analyses examining treatment-naïve subgroups, or reporting within-rCWS comparisons stratified by treatment exposure, would substantially strengthen the interpretability of the recovery-related findings but are not described.

**Age effects require explicit modeling of group-by-age interactions.** The age range 5–11 spans a period of substantial brain development. Chow et al. (2023) found significant age × group interactions: premotor activity during spontaneous speech increased with age in controls but decreased or remained stable in CWS [3]. This non-parallel developmental trajectory means that including age as a linear covariate—as the proposal plans [1]—will not adequately control for age effects unless the interaction is explicitly modeled. The Chow et al. (2023) DCN structural study further showed that patterns of structural anomalies in pCWS differ between preschool-age (3–5) and school-age (6–12) children [2], confirming that pooling ages 5–11 without testing interactions risks obscuring age-dependent effects. With N=20 per group distributed across six years, power to detect age × group interactions is limited, but the proposal does not specify whether such interactions will be tested.

**Power calculations are optimistic, especially for Aim 2.** The power analysis uses adult effect sizes (Cohen's d = 0.7 for between-group comparisons, d = 0.58 for within-stuttering comparisons) [1], estimating 15 per group as sufficient. Three considerations suggest caution. First, adult effect sizes from practiced speakers with decades of stuttering experience are unlikely to transfer directly to children whose neural speech systems are still developing; the Ozernov-Palchik et al. (2026) precision fMRI study confirmed that while left-hemispheric lateralization is adult-like by age 4, response magnitude and functional connectivity strength show protracted development [8]. Second, the Chow et al. (2023) study, using a closely related paradigm with 22 pCWS and 18 controls, acknowledged that “the sample size of the current study could be relatively small for detecting subtler effects” and that “studies with small samples sizes are more susceptible to sampling variation and error, leading to false positives and negatives” [3]. Third, the proposal itself notes that recovery-related effects may be smaller than persistence-related effects, motivating the increase from 15 to 20 per group [1], yet even 20 per group leaves little margin. A recent meta-analysis by Demirel et al. (2025) pooling fMRI data from four studies to achieve 56 people who stutter versus 53 controls found that group differences in speech-motor regions “only emerged at an uncorrected voxel-wise threshold” [9]. For Aim 2, where rCWS is compared with both pCWS and CWNS, 20 per group is very modest and the team's own structural study cautioned that “the results related to rCWS need to be interpreted with caution because the sample size for rCWS was relatively small” [2].

**Aim 1b faces a distinct evidential challenge.** The team's own 2023 study found that most participants produced fewer than 10 stuttering events in the scanner and consequently treated stuttering events as an effect of no interest [3]. The proposal's arithmetic (≈300 syllables × 4.4% stuttering × 0.75 scanner reduction ≈ 9.9 events) already hovers at the 10-event inclusion threshold [1], and published evidence from Garnett et al. (2022) using the same scanner environment shows that scanner noise can reduce stuttering frequency to below 2% in adults [10]. If noise-induced suppression exceeds the estimated 25%, the number of children meeting the Aim 1b inclusion criterion could be substantially smaller than projected. The proposal's contingency—excluding children with fewer than 10 events from Aim 1b only, not from all comparisons [1]—is a sensible mitigation that preserves Aims 1a and 2. The study would nonetheless be strengthened by pre-specifying the minimum analyzable sample for Aim 1b and the interpretive limits if that sample is not achieved.

**Summary.** The cross-sectional design, treatment history confound, and optimistic power together constrain the inferences the study can support to group-level correlates rather than developmental markers or causal mechanisms. The design's within-subject component (Aim 1b) and several methodological refinements represent genuine strengths. The project remains capable of producing the first functional characterization of recovered children during continuous speech, which is a worthwhile contribution, but the framing and interpretation of findings should reflect these limitations.

## Feasibility

**The core continuous-speech fMRI protocol has been demonstrated by the same team in peer-reviewed work with 40 children aged 5–12, and the required equipment, cohort access, and expertise are all documented. The principal feasibility concerns—lower-than-estimated stuttering event yields for Aim 1b and differential attrition at younger ages—are partially mitigated by contingency plans, and the study can deliver Aims 1a and 2 even if Aim 1b is reduced in scope.**

The most direct feasibility evidence comes from Chow et al. (2023), where the same PI and core team successfully acquired analyzable continuous-speech fMRI data from 22 pCWS and 18 controls aged 5–12 using nearly identical sICA denoising and a similar picture-description paradigm [3]. The study's participant flow reveals the scale of attrition: from 74 enrolled children, 26 were excluded before or during MRI, with a notable asymmetry—9 CWS versus 1 control failed to complete MRI scanning [3]. Of 48 completing MRI, 8 were additionally excluded for excessive motion or incidental findings, yielding a 54% retention rate [3]. This published benchmark provides a realistic expectation: recruiting 20 per group to achieve approximately 15 analyzable datasets is a defensible target based on the team's own experience, though the asymmetric scanning completion rate signals that children who stutter may find the protocol more difficult, potentially biasing the pCWS sample toward milder or more compliant cases.

The team's direct access to an existing longitudinal cohort with well-characterized persistence and recovery status \[证据25, 证据26\] is a substantial operational advantage. The familiarity procedures—mock scanning, pre-training, rescheduling with extra training [1]—are aligned with established pediatric MRI practices. The proposal's note that participants have prior scanning experience from the longitudinal study [1] should improve compliance relative to de novo recruitment. The provision for parental presence during scanning [1] is supported by Iwayama et al. (2025), who found that in children aged 3–6, completion rates were 59% with parent present versus 18% without [11]. The Theys et al. (2014) submarine protocol achieved 99% DTI completion in 5–6-year-olds, though that study involved typically developing children performing passive scans rather than a clinical population producing overt speech [12].

Three specific risks require attention. **Scanner noise suppression of stuttering.** Garnett et al. (2022), from the same team, found that stuttering frequency in the scanner was below 2% in adults—well below the 3% clinical threshold—and noted that “the effect of the scanner noise during speech is likely to have had a strong influence on induced fluency” [10]. The broader altered auditory feedback literature confirms that masking noise can reduce stuttering by up to 90% [13], and Fiorin et al. (2019) demonstrated that masked auditory feedback significantly reduced stuttering-like disfluencies in children aged 8–17 [14]. If suppression exceeds 50–70% rather than 25%, the number of children meeting the 10-event threshold for Aim 1b could be substantially smaller than projected. The contingency of excluding children with fewer than 10 events from Aim 1b only [1] is sensible and preserves Aims 1a and 2.

**Task compliance at the youngest ages.** Chow et al. (2023) reported that “especially the younger children, often started to produce automatic speech as soon as they saw the cue instead of waiting for the onset of the pictures” [3], forcing a design compromise. The proposal's single case of an 8-year-old completing the paradigm [1] provides limited reassurance about 5-year-olds. Simhal et al. (2021) found that 78% of children below age 9 passed at least the first two scans (structural + functional), dropping to 3% for a full multiscan session, with age as the strongest predictor of success [15]. The proposal's single fMRI run lasting less than 15 minutes [1] keeps the session shorter than many published protocols, which is advantageous.

**sICA denoising and event-locked movement.** Volumes with abrupt movements above 9 mm will be additionally scrubbed [1], and the published study used a more conservative FD \>2 mm threshold [3]. The sICA pipeline has been validated against PET for continuous speech generally [16], but has not been specifically validated for stimulus-correlated movement during stuttering events. This issue is unlikely to prevent the primary between-group analyses for Aims 1a and 2, but adds uncertainty for Aim 1b.

| Risk | Evidence | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scanner noise suppresses stuttering \>25% | Garnett 2022: %SS \<2% in scanner [10]; Bradshaw 2021: masking reduces up to 90% [13] | Exclude from Aim 1b only; Aims 1a and 2 use fluent-speech contrasts |
| Younger children (age 5–6) fail task compliance | Chow 2023: younger children spoke prematurely [3]; Simhal 2021: 78% of \<9-year-olds passed first two scans [15] | Familiarization, rescheduling, parental presence \[证据38, 证据43\] |
| Differential attrition: CWS fail scanning more than controls | Chow 2023: 9 CWS vs. 1 control failed MRI [3] | Recruit 20/group for 15 analyzable; bias toward milder CWS acknowledged |

On balance, the protocol is feasible within the R21 exploratory framework. The team has published direct evidence of successful implementation in the target population using the core methods. The identified risks are manageable through contingency plans, and the study's most valuable contributions—the rCWS comparisons in Aim 2 and the fluent-speech contrasts in Aim 1a—are resilient to the most likely shortfalls in Aim 1b event counts and attrition at younger ages.

## Ethics and safety

**The proposal describes a well-conceived and proportionate ethics framework that appropriately addresses the specific risks of pediatric overt-speech fMRI.** Informed consent from parents or guardians and verbal or written assent from children are explicitly planned [1], consistent with established standards for research involving minors. MRI safety screening, hearing protection, familiarization, breaks, and the option to stop without penalty are all specified as measures to manage discomfort, fatigue, and anxiety [1]. The provision that parents may remain nearby or with a distressed child [1] is a particularly important safeguard given the young age range (5–11 years) and the demands of the overt-speech task. This parental-presence policy is supported by randomized trial evidence from Iwayama et al. (2025), who found that in children aged 3–6, MRI completion rates were 59% with a parent present versus only 18% without [11]. The proposal's explicit statement that children may withdraw without penalty further reinforces the voluntary nature of participation.

The data management plan addresses the principal information risks in a pediatric neuroimaging study with audio and video recordings. Audio, video, and imaging data will use coded identifiers rather than directly identifying information, with locked or password-protected storage and controlled collaborator access [1]. Data sharing after analysis and publication is limited to de-identified relevant data under a formal data-use agreement, with release anticipated within one year after study closeout [1]. This approach balances the scientific value of data sharing against the re-identification risks inherent in combining voice recordings, facial video, and structural brain images from a small clinical sample. The use of coded identifiers rather than fully anonymized data is appropriate for a longitudinal cohort where follow-up assessments may be needed, and the controlled-access sharing model is proportionate given the sensitivity of the combined data types.

No unmanaged physical, psychological, or data risks are apparent from the protocol as described. The MRI environment at 3T with standard exclusion criteria and hearing protection poses no greater than minimal incremental risk beyond routine clinical scanning. The overt-speech task itself—storytelling from the wordless picture book *Frog, Where Are You?* and reciting overlearned sequences [1]—is age-appropriate and non-distressing; the team's published experience with 40 children aged 5–12 confirms that children can complete closely related continuous-speech paradigms [3]. The protocol does not involve deception, invasive procedures, or experimental interventions that would alter the standard risk profile of fMRI research with children.

The psychological impact of stuttering assessment and group assignment is a consideration that the proposal addresses implicitly rather than through a dedicated risk analysis. Children classified as having persistent stuttering and their families may experience concern upon learning that the child's stuttering has not resolved, particularly since the study draws from a longitudinal cohort where recovery status has been tracked over time [1]. The proposal's procedure for sharing diagnostic information with families is not described in detail, but the involvement of experienced speech-language pathologists in assessment [1] and the study's embedding within an established longitudinal research program with ongoing clinical contact suggest that appropriate communication pathways exist. This is a manageable consideration rather than a material ethical deficiency.

Overall, the ethics and data management provisions are proportionate, well-specified for the population and activities, and aligned with standard safeguards for pediatric neuroimaging research involving audio-video recording.

## Management

**The management plan assembles the requisite complementary expertise, draws on an existing longitudinal cohort and documented institutional resources, and follows a plausible schedule, providing reasonable confidence that the project can be delivered as described.**

The proposal combines three distinct areas of expertise that collectively cover the project's critical requirements. Ho Ming Chow leads the fMRI methods component, having developed the sICA denoising pipeline validated against PET [16] and served as first author on the team's 2023 childhood continuous-speech fMRI study [3]. Soo-Eun Chang directs the longitudinal childhood stuttering cohort from which participants will be drawn, with published structural MRI findings on persistence and recovery that directly motivate the proposed functional hypotheses \[证据152, 证据153\]. Nan Bernstein Ratner contributes the speech-language pathology and fluency analysis expertise, with the proposal specifying that two independent speech-language pathologists will support reliable diagnosis and stuttering-event identification using acoustic and visible articulatory markers [1]. This team has a demonstrated track record of productive collaboration, having co-authored the key preliminary publications on which the proposal builds \[证据14, 证据17, 证据59\].

The required equipment and facilities are documented rather than aspirational. The GE MR750 3T scanner with eight-channel head coil, optical noise-cancellation microphone, MR-compatible camera, and E-Prime synchronization system are specified as available \[证据29, 证据40\]. The speech analysis infrastructure—CLAN/CHAT transcription, acoustic event location, and linguistic measures including TTR, VocD, DSS and IPSYN [1]—is similarly established in the team's published workflow [3]. The existing longitudinal cohort with serial SSI-4 assessments, clinician and parent reports, and prior scanning experience \[证据25, 证据26, 证据38\] eliminates the need for de novo recruitment and characterization, which would otherwise constitute a substantial logistical burden for a two-year R21 project.

The 36-month schedule is realistic for the proposed scope. Recruitment and imaging occupy months 1–18, with preparation of analyses conducted in parallel [1]. Speech coding, fMRI analysis, and preliminary presentations proceed during months 12–24, with manuscript preparation, submission, and dissemination continuing through months 24–36 [1]. This sequencing acknowledges that speech transcription and coding—particularly the identification of stuttering event onsets and offsets from audio-video recordings—is labor-intensive and can proceed concurrently with the later stages of data collection. The parallelization of recruitment and analysis preparation is a sensible use of the two-year active data-collection window. Future directions, including functional connectivity analyses and longitudinal tracking from onset through recovery, are appropriately deferred to subsequent work [1].

The proposal does not include a detailed budget or itemized personnel allocation, but at the level of an R21 scientific plan, the described distribution of responsibilities and resources is sufficient to establish that the necessary elements are in place. No critical gaps in expertise, equipment, or access are apparent. The team's publication record demonstrates that they have successfully executed a closely related protocol in the same population, which is the strongest available evidence of management capacity.

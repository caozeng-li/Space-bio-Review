# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project aims to optimize EMPOWER—Enhancing Management of Psychological Outcomes With Emotion Regulation—a web-based positive emotion regulation intervention for young adult cancer survivors after active treatment. Using a 2^5 full factorial experiment within the Multiphase Optimization Strategy (MOST) framework, the study will test five components (positive events/gratitude, mindfulness, positive reappraisal, personal strengths/goals, and acts of kindness), each present or absent, to determine their individual and combined effects on psychological well-being and distress [1]. The most valuable expected knowledge includes component-specific efficacy estimates, interaction patterns that reveal synergistic or redundant combinations, and exploratory evidence about mediating pathways through coping self-efficacy and social support, all of which will inform assembly of a streamlined intervention for confirmatory evaluation [1].

The proposal is supported by a strong scientific rationale grounded in positive emotion theory—Broaden-and-Build Theory and Revised Stress and Coping Theory—and well-documented unmet psychosocial needs among young adult cancer survivors [1]. Pilot feasibility data across two sites demonstrated acceptability and promising within-group improvements; the multidisciplinary team brings complementary expertise in AYA oncology, positive emotion interventions, and MOST methodology [1]. The factorial design efficiently leverages 352 participants to estimate main effects and interactions that package trials cannot separate [1].

The main limitations that materially affect the judgement are the risk that the proposed control content (factual journaling and psychoeducational reading) may itself improve well-being, thereby attenuating component effect estimates [1]; the inability to detect moderate-sized interactions (d = 0.37–0.62) that could influence component selection [1]; and recruitment projections that are more optimistic than the team's own pilot data, creating a material feasibility risk [1]. The absence of a multiplicity plan for the four co-primary outcomes and the descriptive rather than causal nature of the mediation analyses further temper the strength of the inferences [1].

Weighed together, the proposal describes a worthwhile direction and a methodologically appropriate optimization design. The knowledge about which components should be retained will be valuable whether or not a subsequent confirmatory trial is eventually powered to detect all effects. The limitations in control inertness, interaction detection, and recruitment assumptions are material but do not undermine the core contribution: the study can still produce reliable relative rankings of components and useful mechanistic patterns. The plan would be strengthened by (1) a satisfactory plan for measuring and analysing the actual effects of the control content to assess attenuation, and (2) a feasibility update demonstrating that recruitment is tracking to target with acceptable retention. The current proposal is sufficient for **conditional support**.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

The scientific value is good. The question—which positive emotion regulation skills are most beneficial for young adult cancer survivors—addresses a genuine gap in psychosocial supportive care, and the MOST full-factorial design can produce component-level knowledge that no prior package trial in this area has provided.

Young adult cancer survivors carry a disproportionate burden of psychological distress. Lang et al. showed that YAs had substantially higher odds of mood disorders (OR 2.00, 95% CI 1.44–2.77) and anxiety disorders (OR 2.20, 95% CI 1.70–2.86) compared with cancer-free peers, while the association was much weaker for older adult survivors (OR 1.10 and 1.15 respectively) [2]. Osmani et al.'s meta-analysis estimated that roughly one in three AYA cancer survivors experiences psychological distress or anxiety and one in four experiences depression [3]. These needs persist after active treatment ends, during years when survivors are pursuing education, career formation, relationships and family planning [1]. Despite this, few interventions have been designed specifically for this group, and those that exist—such as PRISM, which showed sustained improvements in quality of life and hope [4]—are multicomponent packages whose active ingredients remain unknown [5]. The present proposal therefore tackles a clinically important question with direct relevance to survivorship care planning.

**Component-level knowledge unattainable from package trials.** The contribution of the proposed work is not merely to test whether EMPOWER works but to identify which of its five components produce meaningful benefit, alone or in combination. The team's earlier trials—IRISS in HIV-positive individuals, DAHLIA in type 2 diabetes, LEAF in metastatic breast cancer caregivers, and LILAC in dementia caregivers [1]—all compared full multicomponent packages against a control, yielding package-level effect estimates only. The EMPOWER pilot produced promising within-group effect sizes (d = 0.45–0.63 for positive affect and life satisfaction at 12 weeks) but was a single-arm design that could not attribute changes to specific skills [6].

The proposed 2^5 factorial design changes the knowledge product fundamentally. Each component is assigned to 176 participants and withheld from 176, balanced across all other components [1], allowing independent estimation of each skill's main effect as well as two-way interactions. This directly answers which skills contribute beyond the others and whether certain combinations are synergistic or redundant—questions a package trial cannot address even in principle.

**Direct support for evidence-based intervention assembly.** The knowledge produced will enable the team to select components based on estimated effects against a d ≈ 0.50 retention threshold, with interaction patterns informing final decisions [1]. The study will also generate exploratory evidence on whether components operate through hypothesized mediators (coping self-efficacy, social support) and whether effects differ by age or gender [1]. These products—component-specific effect estimates, interaction patterns, and descriptive pathway evidence—provide exactly the information needed to assemble a streamlined, potentially more potent intervention package while avoiding unnecessary participant burden.

The value is tempered by two considerations. First, the control content (factual journaling, reading about nutrition and stress responses) may itself produce well-being improvements, which the proposal acknowledges would attenuate estimated EMPOWER effects [1]. This means the study will estimate each component's *incremental* contribution above an already-active control, rather than its absolute effect. The relative ranking of components—which skills add the most—remains valid under this attenuation, and that ranking is the primary optimization goal. The confirmatory trial that follows will provide the definitive test of the assembled package's absolute efficacy [1]. Second, the study can detect main effects of d = 0.31 with 80% power (α = 0.10), but interactions require d ≥ 0.62 for detection, meaning moderate synergy or antagonism could be missed [1]. Nevertheless, the knowledge that components can be compared against each other within a randomized framework represents a clear advance beyond the current evidence base.

## Innovation

The innovation is good. Applying a full-factorial MOST optimization design to a positive emotion regulation intervention and doing so specifically for young adult cancer survivors represents a genuine methodological advance beyond the team's prior work and the broader intervention literature, though the individual intervention techniques are drawn from established positive psychology.

**Systematic methodology, not new components.** The originality lies primarily in the systematic method rather than in inventing new intervention techniques. The five skills—noticing positive events/capitalization/gratitude, mindfulness, positive reappraisal, personal strengths/attainable goals, and acts of kindness [1]—are drawn from well-established positive psychology interventions and from the team's own prior multicomponent packages (IRISS, DAHLIA, LEAF, LILAC) [1]. The proposal does not claim to have created these techniques. What is new is the question the design can answer.

All of the team's prior trials tested multicomponent interventions as whole packages against a control condition. IRISS compared a five-session positive affect skills intervention against an attention-matched control in people newly diagnosed with HIV [7]. DAHLIA, LEAF, and LILAC followed the same two-arm package-trial structure, yielding overall effect estimates (Table 1) but no evidence about which specific skills drove any observed benefits [1]. The LEAF 2.0 trial currently underway compares facilitated versus self-guided delivery of the same package [8], but still examines the intervention as an indivisible unit. The EMPOWER pilot, conducted at the two proposed sites, demonstrated feasibility and promising within-group improvements, yet its single-arm design could not separate component effects from each other or from natural history [6]. None of this prior work can answer whether mindfulness adds anything beyond gratitude, whether positive reappraisal and strengths work synergistically, or whether acts of kindness are superfluous for a given outcome.

**A different knowledge product.** The proposed 2^5 = 32-condition factorial design changes the question from "does this package work?" to "which components work, alone or in combination, for whom, and through what pathways?" Each component is received by 176 participants and withheld from 176, balanced across all other components [1], enabling independent estimation of each skill's main effect. Interactions can reveal whether, for example, mindfulness and positive reappraisal together produce effects larger than the sum of their individual contributions. This is knowledge a package trial cannot generate even in principle.

**Distinctive application of MOST.** Prior MOST applications in behavioral health have typically targeted health behaviors: Huffman et al. used MOST to optimize a positive psychology intervention for physical activity after acute coronary syndrome, and Bennett et al. used a full factorial to optimize a texting-based obesity intervention [9]. The present proposal targets psychological well-being—positive affect, life satisfaction, depression, and anxiety—as the primary outcome itself [1], applying optimization methodology to what is essentially a mental health intervention. This represents an extension of MOST into a domain where component-level evidence is scarce.

The population focus adds a further novel dimension. YA cancer survivors have documented elevated psychosocial needs that differ from both older adult survivors and cancer-free peers [2] [3], and few interventions have been designed specifically for this group [5]. The team's prior trials enrolled HIV-positive adults, type 2 diabetes patients, metastatic breast cancer caregivers, and dementia caregivers [1]—populations with meaningfully different developmental contexts from YAs navigating education, career formation, relationships, and family planning while coping with cancer's aftermath [1]. The proposed study will identify which positive emotion regulation skills are most effective specifically for this under-studied group.

The decision to collapse what was originally eight skills into five components—combining positive events, capitalizing/savoring, and gratitude into one factor [1]—is a pragmatic consolidation reflecting power constraints. This limits the granularity of the component-level knowledge but is a reasonable trade-off given the 32-condition design.

## Rigor

Rigor is moderate. The factorial design is well-configured for its screening purpose and the analytic approach is statistically efficient, but three interrelated limitations constrain the strength of the inferences that can be drawn. The MOST framework and the planned confirmatory trial provide partial safeguards.

**Factorial design and power.** The 2^5 full factorial allocates 352 participants to 32 conditions, with each component present for 176 participants and absent for 176, balanced across all other components [1]. The primary analysis regresses each well-being outcome at one-month follow-up on baseline scores and five component indicators, with longitudinal mixed-effects models incorporating all time-points through six months [1]. This approach efficiently pools information across the entire sample rather than treating the experiment as 32 separate small comparisons.

At 80% power with α = 0.10, the design can detect a main effect of d = 0.31 (95% interval 0.06–0.56), which is substantially smaller than the d ≈ 0.50 retention threshold [1]. A component with a true effect at or above the retention threshold is therefore well above the detectable floor, providing strong protection against Type II error for effects that matter. The use of α = 0.10 is consistent with the MOST screening philosophy articulated by Collins et al., who note that a relaxed Type I error rate is acceptable when the relative cost of overlooking an effective component exceeds the cost of retaining an ineffective one, because a subsequent confirmatory trial will provide formal protection [10]. This represents a principled trade-off for an optimization study.

**Control content inertness.** The control material—factual journaling and reading about nutrition or stress responses [1]—raises a non-trivial concern about attenuation of estimated component effects. The CoPPS Statement recommends that control interventions be "inert for the studied mechanism(s)" [11]. The proposal acknowledges that if this content itself improves well-being, "estimated EMPOWER effects would be smaller" [1].

This is not merely a hypothetical risk. Gerger et al.'s network meta-analysis of writing treatments found that neutral writing (factual journaling about daily activities) was statistically significantly superior to waiting-list control at the longest follow-up, with a standardized mean difference of −0.37 (95% CI −0.61 to −0.14) for PTSD symptoms [12]. Kilimnik and Meston reported that a daily-events writing condition produced significantly greater perceived change in psychological well-being than an assessment-only condition [13]. Reading about stress responses overlaps with psychoeducation, which has been deployed as an active intervention component in its own right [14]. The control content may therefore not be adequately inert for estimating absolute component effects.

This limitation is mitigated in two ways. First, the factorial design still supports the *relative* comparison of components—which skills add the most to this platform—and that relative ranking is precisely the optimization goal of MOST. Interaction estimates are also less directly affected by uniform attenuation, provided control content effects are additive. Second, the confirmatory trial that follows will compare the assembled package against a proper control, providing the unbiased estimate of absolute efficacy that this screening phase cannot guarantee [1]. The proposal's explicit acknowledgement of the attenuation risk is scientifically appropriate.

**Interaction detection and multiplicity.** The design cannot detect moderate-sized interactions reliably. At 80% power, only interactions of d ≥ 0.62 are detectable, with the 95% confidence interval spanning 0.37–0.87 [1]. A true antagonistic interaction of d = 0.50, which could meaningfully reduce a component's net benefit, would fall entirely within the non-detectable range. The Collins et al. decision-making framework addresses this by recommending that interactions be used to "reconsider… tentative decisions" made on main effects, with the modification that interactions are examined only when at least one involved factor has a sufficiently large main effect [10]. The proposal's analytic plan follows this approach [1].

The four co-primary outcomes—positive affect, life satisfaction, depression, and anxiety [1]—are each analyzed separately with no multiplicity adjustment described. Under α = 0.10 per outcome, the familywise error rate across four outcomes could approach 0.34. The MOST framework permits a more flexible decision-making approach than strict hypothesis testing [10], and the confirmatory trial will provide formal protection. Nevertheless, the absence of a pre-specified decision rule for integrating evidence across outcomes—such as requiring effects on at least two of four outcomes before retaining a component—increases the risk that the optimized package will include components selected on the basis of chance findings in a single outcome.

**Mediation analysis is descriptive, not causal.** The Aim 2 mediation analysis can identify statistical associations consistent with hypothesized pathways through coping self-efficacy and social support, but the study design does not establish the temporal ordering needed for causal mediation inference. Mediators and outcomes are assessed at the same post-intervention time points [1]. For causal mediation, treatment must precede the mediator, and the mediator must precede the outcome. When mediator and outcome are measured simultaneously, the direction of influence cannot be disentangled. As Goldsmith, MacKinnon and colleagues note, "cross-sectional analyses do not respect the implied temporal ordering that mediation suggests" [15].

The mediation analysis has value as a descriptive and theory-building exercise. The factorial design provides randomized evidence on whether each component affects the putative mediators—this step (component → mediator) is causally identified, which is itself informative. If a component shows a main effect on a mediator and also on an outcome, and the mediator-outcome association is consistent with theory, the pattern is consistent with (though does not prove) mediation. Conversely, if a component affects outcomes but shows no association with the theorized mediator, that would suggest alternative mechanisms. The proposal's own language—"identify plausible pathways" and "exploratory mediator findings" [1]—appropriately signals this descriptive orientation.

Taken together, these limitations mean that the study is well-positioned to rank components and identify the most promising candidates for the optimized intervention, but less able to quantify absolute component effects, detect moderate interactions that could alter selection decisions, ensure that multi-outcome findings are robust to multiplicity, and establish causal mediation pathways. The confirmatory trial is therefore an essential component of the overall research strategy, not merely a downstream step.

## Feasibility

Feasibility is moderate. The recruitment projections are more optimistic than the team's own pilot data and published experience in this population, and the factorial delivery platform requires adaptation within a short startup window, though the team's direct pilot experience and the community-partnership contingency provide partial mitigation.

**Recruitment projections versus pilot evidence.** The proposal projects approximately 2,850 potentially eligible survivors over three years across Wake Forest (≈550/year) and Northwestern (≈400/year), and assumes a 13% recruitment rate to reach the target of 352 participants within 36 months [1]. An alternative figure of 16.5% "eligible-and-interested" is also cited from pilot experience. These projections must be weighed against what the pilots actually achieved.

At Northwestern, 18 of 185 potentially eligible survivors enrolled, a rate of 9.7%—below both the 13% and the 16.5% figures [1]. At Wake Forest, 23 of 99 *contacted* survivors enrolled (23.2%), but "contacted" is a narrower denominator than "potentially eligible"; the total pool screened at Wake Forest is not reported [1]. The Wake Forest figure therefore overstates the recruitment yield relative to the denominator the proposal uses for planning. Moreover, the Wake Forest pilot used "multiple recruitment channels and dedicated staff" that "improved accrual relative to the first wave" [1], suggesting that the 9.7% Northwestern figure is the more representative baseline, not the 23% that already reflects enhanced effort.

**The eligible pool is likely overestimated.** The estimate of 950 post-treatment AYA survivors annually across two sites has not been adjusted downward for the five concurrent eligibility filters: current age 18–39, within five years of completing active treatment, no recurrence or multiple primary cancers, English literacy, and access to a computer or smartphone with reliable internet [1]. Page et al. estimate that among 2.1 million AYA cancer survivors in the US, only approximately 357,000 (17%) are within five years of diagnosis, and only 19.4% are currently aged 30–39—with the proportion currently aged 18–39 being a subset of that [16]. Many AYA survivors diagnosed at 15–19 are now over 40. Whether two centers can identify 950 survivors annually who satisfy all five criteria simultaneously is not demonstrated.

At 13% of 2,850, the proposal reaches 370—only slightly above the 352 target with thin margin. If the actual eligible pool is 20% smaller or recruitment is 10% rather than 13%, enrollment could fall to 285–296, reducing the analyzable sample at the primary endpoint below the 256 assumed in power calculations [1]. The pilots were single-arm feasibility studies with no randomization and lower burden; the proposed factorial trial, with 32 conditions including conditions that withhold all active components, is a materially different ask of potential participants. Pilot recruitment rates therefore represent an upper bound.

**Published recruitment benchmarks.** The IMPACT trial recruited post-treatment YA cancer survivors nationally for an mHealth physical activity intervention, mailing 3,458 letters over 12 months and ultimately randomizing 280 participants at a rate of 20 per month—an 8% yield from letters to enrollment, consistent with the proposal's own 9.7% Northwestern experience [17]. The SURVAYA study achieved a 36% response rate to a one-time survey [18], but a survey imposes negligible burden compared with a five-week daily-practice intervention. The proposal's community-partnership route through Cancer Services is a reasonable contingency but is described without specific projections, and the IMPACT trial found that community organizations yielded under 4% of participants [17]. Relying on this channel to make up a deficit of 50–100 participants would require substantial unplanned effort.

**Retention concerns.** The retention assumption of 72% at the primary one-month endpoint draws on the Northwestern pilot (72%) but exceeds the Wake Forest pilot (66.7% retention and 58.3% adherence) [1]. In a factorial trial where some participants receive conditions with no active skills, retention may be lower still. At 60% retention, the analyzable sample would be approximately 211, increasing the detectable main effect modestly to d ≈ 0.34—still below the d = 0.50 retention criterion—but further degrading power for interactions already limited to d ≥ 0.62.

**Platform and timeline dependencies.** The pilot delivered EMPOWER via a customized Moodle website at Northwestern [6]; the proposal shifts hosting to the Wake Forest Research Information Systems Unit [1]. More importantly, the pilot was single-arm—all participants received the same sequence. The factorial trial requires the platform to dynamically assign participants to one of 32 conditions, delivering only assigned components and substituting control content for absent components. This re-engineering must occur within a six-month startup window (Y1Q1–Q2) [1] alongside two-site IRB approval and staff hiring. A comparable MOST factorial trial that built a new platform reported 11 months from funding to first enrollment [9]; the proposed six-month window is lean by comparison. While the existing codebase and content library reduce risk relative to building from scratch, no explicit contingency for platform delays is described.

These recruitment, platform, and retention risks are material but not disqualifying. The team has direct pilot experience at the proposed sites, has demonstrated improved accrual with enhanced strategies, and the community-partnership contingency provides a partial backstop.

## Ethics and safety

Ethics and safety are good. The proposal incorporates appropriate consent, data protection, and risk-sensitive eligibility criteria for a low-burden, remotely delivered supportive-care study, and the factorial assignment does not raise unusual ethical concerns.

**Participant welfare and burden.** The study recruits a population—young adult cancer survivors—that carries elevated psychological distress [2] [3] yet has limited access to psychosocial support. EMPOWER is designed as a supportive-care adjunct that does not replace or interfere with ongoing clinical care; the online format allows participants to engage asynchronously from home using common devices, with sessions requiring about 20–30 minutes daily over five weeks [1]. This imposes a modest time burden and avoids travel, clinic visits, or disruption of medical management. Participants are compensated $20 per assessment, up to $100 total [1], which is proportional to the time involved and not coercive.

The eligibility criteria manage psychological and medical risk proportionately. The study excludes individuals with significant psychiatric history and those receiving palliative or hospice care [1]. These exclusions are appropriate: the self-guided, web-based format is not designed to manage acute psychiatric crises or end-of-life distress, and including such participants could both expose them to an unsupported intervention and confound outcome assessment. Maintenance hormonal therapy and maintenance chemotherapy are permitted [1], correctly distinguishing stable adjuvant treatment from active intensive therapy. Requiring English literacy and internet access is a practical necessity for a text-based, web-delivered intervention, though it limits generalizability.

**Consent and data governance.** Potential participants complete a REDCap eligibility screener and online consent before accessing any study materials; unique study IDs and PINs control questionnaire access, and the REDCap platform supports encrypted survey and database collection [1]. The intervention is hosted by the Wake Forest Research Information Systems Unit [1], building on the HIPAA-compliant Moodle platform used in the EMPOWER pilot at Northwestern [6]. Two-site IRB oversight is implied within the six-month startup phase, consistent with standard R01 practice.

**Ethical considerations of the factorial design.** All participants receive a five-week program of equivalent duration and structure; what varies is which weeks contain active emotion-regulation skills versus control material (factual journaling, reading about nutrition or stress responses [1]). No participant receives fewer than zero weeks of potentially beneficial content, and the control material is designed to be credible, engaging, and non-harmful. The CoPPS Statement recommends that control interventions "elicit a similar expectation of benefit as the test intervention" to reduce the risk of differential attrition and unblinding [11]; the proposed control content plausibly meets this standard. While the control content may produce some well-being benefit—a concern discussed under Rigor—that does not make it ethically problematic; the control is not a pure no-treatment comparator, which is a design consideration rather than an ethical one. Participants are not deceived about the nature of the study, and the informed consent process should make clear that they will receive different combinations of skills and informational material.

A practical consideration is that participants assigned to conditions with few or no active EMPOWER components may perceive less benefit and be more likely to discontinue. The pilots showed that participants who left had poorer baseline well-being [1], raising the possibility of differential attrition concentrated among those with greater need. The study addresses this by allowing up to eight weeks to complete the five-week program to accommodate interruptions [1], and by providing compensation for assessment completion regardless of intervention adherence, but a monitoring plan for distress in the all-control condition (Condition 1) would be a prudent addition. Overall, the study manages the material risks appropriately for a Phase II optimization trial where confirmatory evaluation of the assembled package will follow.

## Management

Management is good. The team combines the necessary clinical, intervention-development, and optimization-methods expertise, and the four-year schedule is logically structured around overlapping phases of recruitment, analysis, and dissemination. Two specific risks—the web platform's adaptation for factorial delivery within a short startup window, and the compression of final analyses into the last two quarters—warrant attention but are partially mitigated by the team's direct pilot experience.

**Team expertise is a clear strength.** The co-PIs bring complementary and directly relevant expertise. Salsman contributes young-adult oncology, recruitment, and PROMIS/NIH Toolbox outcome-measurement expertise; Moskowitz contributes positive-emotion intervention development, having led the IRISS, DAHLIA, LEAF, and LILAC programs that provide the intervention's empirical foundation [1]. Wagner contributes MOST trial experience, and the collaborators cover psychosocial oncology, medical oncology, quantitative methods, survivorship, and eHealth [1]. Critically, the team has already conducted the EMPOWER pilot as a multi-site, web-based, single-arm feasibility trial in exactly this population and published the results with Salsman as first author and Moskowitz as senior author [6]. The pilot demonstrated that the team can recruit across two sites—using REDCap for screening, consent, and questionnaires [6]—and deliver the intervention via a customized Moodle platform with HIPAA-compliant hosting [6]. This pilot experience substantially reduces the uncertainty that a new collaboration or untested platform would introduce.

**Schedule structure and critical dependencies.** The four-year schedule [1] places study startup in Y1Q1–Q2, optimization (recruitment, intervention delivery, and follow-up) from Y1Q2 through Y4Q2, data analysis from Y3Q1 through Y4Q3, and dissemination and follow-on grant preparation from Y3Q3 through Y4Q4. The 36-month recruitment window is internally consistent with the projection of enrolling 352 participants at approximately 10–12 per month [1]. However, two dependencies concentrate risk.

The web platform adaptation is the most significant technical risk. The pilot delivered EMPOWER as a single-arm, fixed-sequence intervention at Northwestern [6]; the proposal shifts hosting to Wake Forest and requires the platform to dynamically assign each participant to one of 32 conditions, delivering only assigned components and substituting inert control content for absent components [1]. This re-engineering must occur within a six-month startup window that must also accommodate two-site IRB approval and staff hiring. The Bennett et al. MOST factorial trial, which built a new platform, required 11 months from funding to first enrollment [9]; the proposed six-month window is lean by comparison. No explicit contingency for platform delays is described, though the existing codebase and content library reduce risk relative to building from scratch.

Final analyses are compressed into the project's last two quarters. The intervention lasts five weeks with up to eight weeks allowed for interruptions, and follow-up extends to six months post-intervention [1]. The primary endpoint is the one-month follow-up, so primary outcome data for the last enrolled participants arrives by approximately Y4Q3, consistent with the analysis window. However, six-month follow-up data—needed for secondary outcomes and the longitudinal mixed-effects models that include all timepoints [1]—arrives at the very end of the project, leaving approximately two quarters for secondary analyses, interaction effects, mediation (Aim 2), and manuscript preparation. The compressed timeline provides minimal buffer for handling missing data, sensitivity analyses, or unexpected findings before the follow-up R01 submission planned for Y4Q2–Q4 [1].

The REDCap procedures for eligibility screening, consent, and questionnaire administration transfer directly from the pilot and represent a known quantity [1] [6]. The recruitment plan's multi-channel approach (registries, letters, calls, organizations, community partnership) [1] is well-specified and includes a contingency route through Cancer Services, though its projected contribution is not quantified. Overall, the team is well-qualified, the pilot experience provides a credible operational basis, and the schedule is logically organized; the platform and late-project compression risks are manageable with attentive monitoring.

# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project investigates how the inflammatory cytokine IL6 disrupts regulatory T cell (Treg) function in inflammatory bowel disease through post-translational modification of the epigenetic enzyme EZH2. The work is organized around three aims: mapping the FOXP3-EZH2 interaction at domain resolution and testing its functional contribution through interaction-defective mutants; systematically identifying IL6/JAK2-dependent phosphorylation sites in the EZH2 SET domain and connecting them to altered enzymatic activity and gene repression; and testing whether genetically insulating Tregs from IL6 signaling—through IL6Rα deletion or phosphorylation-resistant EZH2—improves their capacity to treat established intestinal inflammation. The most valuable expected knowledge is an integrated, residue-level account of an inflammatory-kinase-to-epigenetic-enzyme signaling axis in Tregs, together with functional tools (interaction-defective and phospho-mutant EZH2) and in vivo evidence on whether interrupting this pathway sustains Treg suppressive function in a therapeutically relevant setting.

The main supporting evidence includes strong genetic data from the conditional EZH2-deficient mouse (severe spontaneous inflammation, impaired suppression, altered cytokine profiles); published literature establishing FOXP3-EZH2 interaction (Arvey 2014 [1], Bamidele 2018 [2]) and the requirement for EZH2 in Tregs (DuPage 2015 [3]); and preliminary data showing that JAK2 inhibition blocks IL6-dependent reduction in EZH2-associated H3K27 methyltransferase activity. The experimental design has several strengths: orthogonal JAK2 manipulation, systematic six-site mutagenesis screening protecting against single-site failure, and a clinically meaningful treatment model. Three limitations materially affect the judgement. First, the proposal does not clearly distinguish whether IL6 reduces EZH2 methyltransferase activity through direct catalytic impairment or through JAK2/β-TrCP-mediated EZH2 degradation—a published mechanism (Sahasrabuddhe 2014 [4]) not discussed in the proposal. Second, the Y641F mutation has well-characterized gain-of-function properties in lymphoma (altered substrate preference toward dimethylated H3K27), making it an imperfect tool for testing phosphorylation resistance; a positive therapeutic result could reflect altered chromatin activity rather than escape from IL6 signaling. Third, the Aim 3 dose-response design is under-specified, and the power calculation relies on a large effect-size assumption whose realism in a treatment model is uncertain.

**This is a well-conceived project that extends published findings—FOXP3-EZH2 association, EZH2 dependence of Tregs, and IL6 disruption of their interaction—to a deeper, more integrated mechanistic understanding.** The main limitations (the degradation-versus-catalytic-impairment ambiguity, the Y641F interpretive burden, and the under-specified Aim 3 design) do not invalidate the project but reduce the certainty with which positive outcomes can be attributed to the specific inferred mechanism. The project's value is substantially protected by experimental breadth: the six-site screen in Aim 2 and the explicit alternatives in each aim ensure that informative knowledge is generated even if central hypotheses require revision. The proposed treatment experiment fills a genuine gap in a clinically meaningful setting. On balance, the scientific strengths and the durable knowledge contribution support proceeding with the current plan, while the identified limitations should be addressed in the experimental execution.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

The proposal addresses a timely and important question: how inflammatory signals disrupt the epigenetic machinery that maintains regulatory T cell function in intestinal inflammation. The integrated dissection of an IL6→JAK2→EZH2 phosphorylation→Treg dysfunction axis, with functional validation in an established-colitis treatment model, would provide the first connected account of cytokine-to-epigenetic-enzyme signaling in Tregs. The value is protected through experimental breadth: the systematic six-site screen means the contribution shifts from 'Y641 is the key' to 'first systematic mapping of inflammatory-kinase-sensitive SET-domain residues in T cells' if the leading site proves not to be dominant. The established-colitis treatment experiment addresses a clinically meaningful treatment-versus-prevention distinction relevant to IBD cell therapy. The project contributes mechanistic depth and possible entry points for therapeutic Treg engineering, going beyond the descriptive associations currently established in the literature.

## Innovation

The individual elements—FOXP3-EZH2 association (Arvey 2014 [1]), EZH2 requirement for Tregs (DuPage 2015 [3]), and IL6 disruption of FOXP3-EZH2 interaction (Bamidele 2018 [2])—are well-established in the literature. The innovation lies in the integration: a systematic, residue-level dissection of how an inflammatory kinase (JAK2) post-translationally modifies EZH2 to disrupt Treg function, coupled with functional separation of FOXP3-dependent from FOXP3-independent EZH2 activity through interaction-defective mutants. The domain-mapping and six-site screen go substantially beyond the published C232 point-mutant analysis, and the established-colitis treatment experiment tests a therapeutic concept absent from the prior literature. This work would add mechanistic depth—a cytokine-to-epigenetic-enzyme signaling axis in Tregs—that has not previously been established in a single connected experimental system.

## Rigor

The experimental design has considerable scientific strengths: orthogonal confirmation of JAK2-Y641 connection (inhibitor/siRNA/dominant-negative/constitutively active JAK2 plus receptor-specific KO), systematic six-site mutagenesis protecting against single-hypothesis failure, genomic integrated reporters avoiding episomal artifacts, and well-structured alternatives (indirect PRC2 interaction, EZH1 compensation, effector-cell resistance). However, three limitations materially affect the strength of inference. First, the proposal does not distinguish between reduced EZH2 catalytic activity and reduced EZH2 protein abundance (via JAK2/β-TrCP-mediated degradation, as established by Sahasrabuddhe 2014 [4]), yet this distinction is critical for interpreting the mechanism. Second, Y641F has well-characterized gain-of-function properties in lymphoma (Yap et al., Blood 2011 [5]), making it an imperfect tool for testing phosphorylation resistance; a positive result could equally reflect altered substrate preference rather than escape from IL6 signaling. Third, the Aim 3 dose-response design is under-specified (titration structure within vs. across replicates not stated), and the effect-size assumption underpinning the power calculation may be optimistic in a treatment model. These issues reduce the confidence with which a positive outcome can be attributed to the specific inferred mechanism.

## Feasibility

The proposed experiments are built on well-characterized reagents and assays: the conditional KO mouse (FOXP3ΔEZH2), established cell lines (Jurkat, HAP-1), and protocols (co-IP, PLA, ChIP, adenoviral reconstitution, transfer colitis). The lab has demonstrated efficiency for nucleofection (\~20%) and adenoviral transduction (\>75%). A systematic six-site mutagenesis screen protects against failure of the leading Y641 hypothesis, and the stated alternatives (PRC2-bridged interaction for Aim 1, EZH1 compensation, effector-cell resistance) are addressed in the experimental plans. The main feasibility risk is the under-specified dose-response design in Aim 3, where a single large effect-size assumption (1.25 SD) determines the power calculation; if the true treatment effect is smaller, the study may be underpowered. The TALEN editing of IL6Rα in primary Tregs is a stated new development that depends on the institutional genetics core—a moderate dependency, but one that falls within the existing collaboration.

## Ethics and safety

Animal work is subject to Mayo Clinic IACUC review with veterinary oversight. The proposal specifies daily weight and clinical monitoring with predefined humane endpoints (≥20% weight loss, moribund appearance), analgesic support after adoptive transfer, and approved euthanasia procedures. The proposal acknowledges that the Y641F mutation has been associated with lymphoproliferative conditions and indicates plans to investigate long-term function and toxicity. Human Crohn's disease tissue data form part of the preliminary scientific basis; the proposed treatment studies are in mice and do not involve human subjects. Resource sharing follows NIH guidelines. No ethical or safety problems were identified that would compromise support.

## Management

The five-year project combines the PI's established expertise in intestinal immunology and Treg biology with institutional core facilities (flow cytometry, imaging, sequencing, proteomics, mouse genetics). The sequential structure—domain mapping and mechanistic assays in Aims 1-2 feeding reagents into the therapeutic tests in Aim 3—provides logical connected workflow. Animal numbers, replicate structure, and power calculations are specified for the main experiments. Resource-sharing plans are included. The main management gap is the incomplete analysis specification for the Aim 3 treatment experiment: whether replicates are pooled or modeled as random effects, and the absence of explicit molecular readouts (H3K27me3, target gene expression) in recovered Tregs to close the mechanistic loop.

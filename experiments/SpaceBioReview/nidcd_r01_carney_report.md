# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project, led by Dr. Laurel H. Carney, aims to develop and test a mechanistic model of masked detection and spectral coding based on neural fluctuations in the auditory system, rather than on stimulus energy. The work integrates computational modeling with human psychophysics and neurophysiological recordings from the inferior colliculus of awake rabbits. The research will investigate how listeners detect tones in notched noise, how this process is affected by sensorineural hearing loss, and how these neural codes represent spectral slopes in fricative consonants and pinna cues for sound localization. A central and highly ambitious objective is to build models tailored to individual listeners, predicting their performance across multiple tasks from a single characterization, rather than assuming all listeners with a similar audiogram use the same perceptual cues. The most valuable expected knowledge is a rigorous, multi-level account of how peripheral hearing damage changes the neural code available for perception, potentially explaining why amplification often fails to fully restore perceptual abilities.

The proposal is supported by a strong and coherent theoretical framework from the Carney laboratory, demonstrating that a single model based on neural-fluctuation cues can explain behavioral results in both fixed- and roving-level masking and profile analysis, tasks that energy-based models fail to unify. Extensive preliminary data, including model simulations and awake-rabbit IC recordings, show that neural responses are consistent with the fluctuation hypothesis. The three-aim structure logically extends this work from basic cue characterization to complex real-world stimuli. However, the current plan is limited by several factors that materially affect the judgement of its feasibility and rigor. The most critical is a severe and pervasive gap between the project's enormous scope and its documented implementation plan. The proposal lacks a year-by-year schedule, a personnel plan, and a quantitative mapping of resources to tasks. The projected yield of \~200 neurons per condition far exceeds the lab's demonstrated throughput, and the human psychophysics time budget is insufficient for the individual-cue classification that is central to the project's architecture. This creates a high-risk dependency chain where a delay in physiology or subject characterization will cascade across all aims. Furthermore, the forward-masking aim has not adequately resolved the conceptual tension between the fluctuation account and the lab's own efferent-gain-control model, and the plan for model individualization remains under-specified regarding critical choices about DPOAE integration and central model parameters.

This is a proposal of remarkable scientific vision that seeks to fundamentally reframe how we understand hearing and hearing loss. Its strengths—the innovative fluctuation-based framework, the deep integration of modeling with physiology and behavior, and the ambitious goal of individualized prediction—set a high bar for what this research program could achieve. However, these strengths are critically undermined by a chronic discrepancy between the project's ambitious scope and its implementation planning. The feasibility and management risks are not peripheral issues that can be resolved with minor adjustments; they are structural: an unworkable psychophysics time budget, a physiology target beyond demonstrated capacity, and a complete absence of the personnel and scheduling detail necessary to coordinate three interdependent research arms. The forward-masking aim, while scientifically intriguing, introduces additional theoretical complexity by failing to resolve the tension between the fluctuation account and the lab's own efferent-based model. This proposal, therefore, identifies a set of transformative scientific questions and provides a coherent theoretical foundation to explore them, but it does not present a workable plan to deliver on its stated five-year goals.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

The scientific question at the core of this project is of high importance for auditory neuroscience and for understanding the perceptual consequences of sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL). The attainable knowledge is substantial for the central claims—a validated mechanistic account linking peripheral fluctuation coding to behavioral detection—but is more uncertain for the forward-masking extension, the fine spectral discriminations in Aim 3, and the practical translatability of individualized models.

The proposal addresses a fundamental limitation of conventional power-spectrum and temporal-window models of hearing: their inability to explain why listeners can detect tones in notched noise even when overall stimulus level is randomly roved, a manipulation that disrupts energy cues [1] [2] [3]. The alternative proposed here—that low-frequency fluctuations in auditory-nerve (AN) responses, shaped by peripheral filtering, saturation, and adaptation, are converted by modulation-tuned inferior colliculus (IC) neurons into a rate-place code for detection and spectral shape—is grounded in a coherent theoretical framework and has received substantial empirical support from the Carney laboratory. The Maxwell et al. (2020) study demonstrated that a single IC model using neural-fluctuation cues explains human thresholds in fixed-level notched-noise masking, roving-level masking, and profile analysis, tasks that energy-based and envelope-power-spectrum models cannot unify [2]. This alone constitutes a significant conceptual advance.

**Central contribution: individualized cue characterization and cross-task prediction**

The project's most valuable expected knowledge contribution is the demonstration—or falsification—that individual listeners can be characterized by their cue reliance (envelope-fluctuation vs. energy) and that this characterization, combined with individualized peripheral models, predicts performance across multiple tasks. The preliminary data showing that four of five listeners displayed frequency-dependent cue use, with envelope cues more useful in frequency regions with better hearing, while one listener with a flat audiogram relied on energy cues [1], illustrate the kind of individual variability that this framework could explain. If successful, this would reframe SNHL not simply as a loss of audibility but as a change in the neural code available for perception, explaining why amplification often fails to restore normal function [1].

**Qualified value: forward masking**

The attainable value is more qualified for Aim 2b, which seeks to test whether forward-masking results can be understood within the same fluctuation-based framework [1]. The proposal does not resolve the tension between this claim and the Carney laboratory's own published work demonstrating that MOC efferent gain control—a distinct mechanism—explains key forward-masking phenomena, including growth of masking, recovery over delay, and robustness to roving masker level [4] [5]. Without a clear account of how fluctuations per se would produce forward masking when masker and probe are temporally separated, the knowledge attainable from Aim 2b is more likely to be a correlation between modulation tuning and forward-masked thresholds than a mechanistic demonstration that fluctuation coding underlies both simultaneous and forward masking.

**Qualified value: spectral slopes in natural stimuli**

Aim 3 extends the fluctuation framework to spectral slopes in fricative consonants and pinna cues [1]. The Hamza et al. (2023) study showed that IC model rate profiles based on within-channel fluctuation amounts achieve classifier accuracy approaching behavioral performance for fricative identification [6]. However, the proposal explicitly defers cross-channel envelope phase relationships [1], and the Alves-Pinto et al. (2014) study demonstrated that fine high-frequency spectral notch discrimination requires temporal sampling in 4–9 ms bins, not merely gross fluctuation amounts [7]. The attainable knowledge from Aim 3 is therefore likely to delineate where the within-channel fluctuation code succeeds and where it breaks down, rather than to provide a complete account of spectral shape coding. This is scientifically valuable but represents a descriptive boundary condition rather than a fully integrative framework.

The project does not include a direct path to clinical application within its scope. Individualized models are developed and tested against psychophysical tasks, but the proposal does not evaluate whether model-based cue diagnoses predict speech perception outcomes or hearing-aid benefit. This is a reasonable scope limitation for a basic-science R01, but it means the useful capability dimension is prospective rather than demonstrated.

## Innovation

The proposal offers a genuinely novel mechanistic framework by placing neural fluctuations, rather than stimulus energy, at the center of masked detection and spectral coding. It extends this account in three directions that collectively distinguish it from prior work: individualized models that predict cross-task performance, a fluctuation-based interpretation of forward masking, and an explanation of spectral-slope coding in fricatives and pinna cues. Each extension represents a substantial conceptual departure from conventional approaches, though the forward-masking component introduces unresolved theoretical tension.

**A neural-fluctuation framework for masking**

The core innovation is the neural-fluctuation hypothesis itself. Conventional power-spectrum models assume listeners detect a tone in notched noise by monitoring energy at the output of an auditory filter. The roving-level paradigm, in which overall stimulus level is randomly varied across intervals, disrupts this energy cue, yet human thresholds remain largely stable [1] [3]. The proposal's alternative—that peripheral filtering produces narrowband fluctuations in auditory-nerve responses, that nonlinear cochlear processing changes these fluctuations, and that modulation-tuned IC neurons convert fluctuation contrast across frequency channels into a rate-place code [1]—is supported by the Maxwell et al. (2020) demonstration that a single IC model accounts for fixed-level, roving-level, and profile-analysis results that energy-based models cannot unify [2]. While the fluctuation mechanism itself builds on established concepts of envelope coding and modulation tuning, its application to simultaneous notched-noise masking and its integration with a fully specified peripheral model represent a distinctive theoretical synthesis.

**From group averages to individualized models**

The most significant conceptual innovation is the shift from group-level audiogram-based fitting to individualized models that characterize each listener's cue reliance. The preliminary finding that four of five listeners showed frequency-dependent cue use, with envelope cues more useful where hearing was better, while one listener with a flat audiogram relied on energy cues [1], challenges the assumption underlying most clinical fitting that all listeners with similar audiograms use the same cues. The architecture proposed—constraining individualized peripheral models from Aim 1 characterization and using those models to predict Aim 2 and Aim 3 results without independent fitting [1]—is methodologically ambitious. It transforms the proposal from a model-fitting exercise into a test of whether individual differences in cue use, once characterized, generalize across tasks. This approach to individual differences is innovative in auditory psychophysics, where individual variability is more often treated as noise than as a phenomenon to be predicted.

**Forward masking: an unresolved theoretical tension**

The extension to forward masking is conceptually bold but incompletely resolved. The proposal frames Aim 2b as testing "whether forward-masking results are better explained by this \[neural-fluctuation\] mechanism than by current temporal-processing accounts" [1]. Interpreting forward masking through fluctuations—where the masker alters the fluctuation statistics available to the probe—would be a significant theoretical departure from the dominant temporal-window framework. However, the Carney laboratory's own published work has advanced an alternative mechanism: MOC efferent gain control, which explains growth of masking, recovery over delay, and robustness to roving masker level through feedback-driven reduction of cochlear gain rather than through fluctuation coding [4] [5]. The proposal does not explicitly distinguish these two accounts or specify manipulations that would cleanly dissociate them. This unresolved tension weakens the innovative contribution of Aim 2b, because a failure to support the fluctuation account could be interpreted as support for the efferent account—a conclusion the laboratory has already published—rather than as a discriminative test between mechanisms.

**Spectral slopes in ecologically relevant stimuli**

The third extension, to spectral slopes in fricatives and pinna cues, is innovative in applying the fluctuation framework to natural stimuli with ecological relevance. The Hamza et al. (2023) demonstration that IC model rate profiles based on within-channel fluctuation amounts approach behavioral accuracy for fricative identification [6], and the Wang and Carney (2019) finding that IC model responses are more sensitive to steep spectral slopes than to spectral peaks [8], suggest the framework generalizes beyond laboratory masking paradigms. The innovation lies in proposing a single mechanism—modulation-tuned IC neurons reading out fluctuation profiles created by peripheral interactions between spectral shape and cochlear processing—that spans detection, spectral discrimination, and speech-relevant coding, rather than postulating separate mechanisms for each task.

## Rigor

The core methodological framework is well-established and appropriately rigorous for the project's central goals: the fixed-versus-roving level diagnostic, signal-detection-theory linking of model, neural, and behavioral thresholds, and ROC-based neural threshold estimation all follow validated precedents from the Carney laboratory and the broader literature. However, the rigor of the proposal is weakened by two specific issues: the forward-masking aim does not adequately disentangle the fluctuation account from the laboratory's own efferent-gain-control alternative, and the model individualization procedure remains under-specified regarding DPOAE integration and how central model parameters will be fixed to maintain the integrity of cross-task predictions.

**Strengths of the core experimental design**

The proposal's strongest methodological feature is the design for Aim 1 and Aim 2a. The fixed-versus-roving level paradigm is a principled diagnostic: similar thresholds under fixed and roving levels support envelope-cue use, while roving-level elevation supports energy-cue reliance [1]. The Maxwell et al. (2020) model demonstrates that a fluctuation-based IC population predicts average roving-level threshold elevations of approximately 3–4 dB, whereas an energy-based AN model fails to reach threshold under roving [2]. The use of signal-detection theory to translate model sensitivity into predicted thresholds—via response derivatives normalized by variability, combined across populations with specified variance models [1]—provides a rigorous quantitative bridge between model and behavior. The ROC-based neural threshold estimation at 70.7% correct, matched to the two-down/one-up tracking procedure [9], is a well-validated method from the Fan et al. (2021) study that permits direct comparison of single-neuron neurometrics with behavioral thresholds on the same percent-correct scale.

**Unresolved tension in Aim 2b: fluctuation coding versus efferent gain control**

The forward-masking aim (Aim 2b) has a structural rigor problem: it frames the investigation as testing whether forward-masking results are better explained by fluctuations than by temporal-processing accounts [1], yet does not confront the fact that the proposing laboratory has already advanced and published an alternative mechanism—MOC efferent gain control—that is distinct from neural-fluctuation coding. The Maxwell et al. (2024) model demonstrates that efferent-driven reduction of cochlear gain explains growth of masking, recovery over delay, and robustness to roving masker level [4]. The Agarwalla, Farhadi, and Carney (2025) IC recording study showed that forward masking by Gaussian noise was not attributable to persistence of masker responses and was better explained by efferent gain control [5].

Critically, this efferent mechanism is not a fluctuation-based account: it operates through feedback-driven changes in cochlear gain that suppress probe responses, independently of the envelope-fluctuation interactions between masker and probe that drive the simultaneous-masking model [1]. The proposal's plan to relate forward-masked IC responses to modulation tuning [1] can demonstrate a correlation between modulation sensitivity and forward masking, but cannot establish that the fluctuation mechanism causes forward masking without manipulations that dissociate efferent from fluctuation contributions. A neuron could show both modulation tuning and efferent-mediated forward masking because the same IC subpopulation through which efferent effects are expressed also happens to be modulation-tuned. The proposal lacks the critical experimental manipulations that would cleanly dissociate these mechanisms, such as forward-masking conditions where the MOC reflex is selectively engaged or bypassed.

**Under-specified model individualization**

The model individualization procedure also has a rigor limitation: the proposal states that DPOAEs will "jointly constrain" each listener's model [1], but provides no method for translating DPOAE measurements into the model's COHC and CIHC parameters. The Zilany et al. (2014) model's fitaudiogram2 function uses only the audiogram to set parameters, allocating two-thirds of threshold elevation to OHC and one-third to IHC damage as a default heuristic [10] [11]. The model does not natively simulate DPOAEs, and the Carney laboratory's own published individualization work (Guest et al., 2023) used only audiometric thresholds [11]. The external literature indicates that DPOAE-to-parameter mapping for individualized cochlear models is unreliable at the individual level: a 2026 preprint noted that "OHC individualization of the mouse model based on DPOAEs failed to faithfully reproduce individual measurements" [12]. The proposal therefore claims a capability—DPOAE-constrained individualization—that current published methods do not support, without specifying how this gap will be bridged.

A related concern is that the model's central processing choices—which MTF types are included, how responses are combined across the population, what variance model is used [1]—represent additional degrees of freedom that could absorb prediction errors from the peripheral parameterization. The proposal does not specify these choices a priori or explain how they will remain fixed across aims to maintain the integrity of cross-task prediction [1]. Without this specification, a successful cross-task prediction could reflect post-hoc tuning of central parameters rather than genuine generalization from the individualized peripheral model.

These limitations do not undermine the project's core scientific approach, which remains well-grounded in prior work and uses appropriately rigorous methods for the central claims. However, they represent specific gaps in the chain of inference for two project components—forward masking and individualized cross-task prediction—that would need to be addressed for the proposal to fully support its stated conclusions.

## Feasibility

The proposal integrates modeling, physiology, and human psychophysics across three interdependent aims, but the implementation planning is insufficiently detailed to establish feasibility. Critical gaps include an absent personnel plan, no year-by-year schedule, and a physiology target that significantly exceeds the laboratory's demonstrated throughput. The human psychophysics time budget for Aim 1 is infeasible for the individual cue classification that anchors the project's architecture, and the tight dependency chain among aims lacks explicit risk mitigation. These are structural concerns that cumulatively threaten completion within the proposed five-year scope.

**Absent personnel and scheduling detail**

The implementation section is strikingly thin. The entire resource description consists of a single passage: "The laboratory has sound-attenuated booths, auditory equipment, local computational facilities and access to university high-performance computing. Consultants contribute expertise in audiology, hearing-loss psychophysics, phonetics and theoretical psychoacoustics" [1]. No personnel are specified beyond the PI—no graduate students, postdocs, technicians, or research staff are allocated to any aim. No year-by-year schedule or milestones are provided. For a program that must concurrently run awake-rabbit IC recordings (daily two-hour sessions [1]), human psychophysical testing (up to 45 participants at 12–15 hours each [1]), and computational modeling with individualized parameter sweeps, the absence of a personnel plan is not a minor omission but a fundamental gap.

**Physiology target exceeds demonstrated throughput**

The physiology target is ambitious relative to demonstrated throughput. The proposal anticipates obtaining "approximately 200 neurons for each stimulus condition" using four rabbits recorded for 12–24 months each [1]. However, the laboratory's published record shows a substantially lower yield: the Fan et al. (2022) study, using four awake Dutch-belted rabbits studied for 13–55 months, yielded 136 isolated single units total across the entire study, with 68 tested in all conditions [13]. The Agarwalla and Carney (2026) study used two rabbits and recorded 150 neurons total [14]. The proposal's target of approximately 200 per condition—with multiple conditions implied by the combined notch-width, level, frequency, forward-masking, pinna-cue, and fricative manipulations across Aims 2a, 2b, and 3—is approximately an order of magnitude above demonstrated capacity. Even interpreting "per condition" loosely as the total across all conditions, the logistics of holding well-isolated neurons across daily sessions to complete the full stimulus battery per neuron [1] are not addressed.

**Aim 1 time budget cannot support individual cue classification**

The human psychophysics faces a bottleneck at the project's entry point. The proposed three-hour session for characterizing five frequencies with fixed- and roving-level tracks at two sensation levels is infeasible for individual cue classification. The Mao et al. (2015) study required approximately 3–4 hours per condition for a single test frequency, with an average of 35 blocks per condition [15]. The effect size separating envelope from energy cue users is modest, approximately 5 dB [15], and the within-listener precision achievable with four repeats per track is unlikely to support confident individual classification at the boundary: Leek et al. (1992) demonstrate that adaptive tracks of 100 trials can produce unbiased threshold estimates, but the standard error of a single-track threshold is on the order of 1.5–2.5 dB, yielding a standard error for the fixed-minus-roving difference of roughly 1–1.8 dB—meaning the \~5 dB effect is approximately 3–5 standard errors, which is statistically distinguishable at the group level but provides limited confidence for classifying a single listener whose true difference lies near the boundary [16].

This bottleneck is structural: "All human participants will complete Aim 1 characterization before Aim 2 and/or Aim 3" [1], meaning that every participant must pass through a characterization session whose time budget is inadequate before any downstream testing can begin. If Aim 1 testing requires 6–9 hours rather than 3, the total per-participant commitment rises from the proposed 12–15 hours to perhaps 18–25 hours, and the 6–8 week completion window per listener [1] becomes unrealistic for older participants with SNHL.

**Unmanaged dependency risk across arms**

The dependency chain creates a systemic risk. Individual behavioral data from Aim 1 must constrain individualized models, which must then predict Aim 2 and Aim 3 results [1]. Physiology must test model predictions at the neural population level, and model results must guide which stimulus conditions are tested physiologically and psychophysically [1]. This creates a bidirectional dependency with no documented contingency: if IC recording yield is low, model validation is delayed; if Aim 1 characterization takes longer than planned, all downstream psychophysics stalls; if individualized models fail to predict Aim 2 thresholds, substantial model revision may be needed mid-program. The alternatives section mentions scientific fallbacks [1] but no schedule risk mitigations.

The computational requirements are stated but not quantified. The proposal mentions "access to university high-performance computing" [1], but the Guest et al. (2023) study reduced model fidelity (61 CFs instead of 91, fewer trials) to manage computational cost for 21 listeners [11]. Scaling to 45 listeners with individualized parameters, multiple stimulus conditions, and the feedback loop between modeling and experiment requires explicit resource quantification that is absent. The feasibility of the proposed work therefore cannot be assessed with confidence from the documented implementation plan.

## Ethics and safety

The proposal describes appropriate ethical and safety measures for both human participants and animal subjects, with procedures that align with standard institutional review and animal care requirements for the described activities.

Human participant procedures are outlined in the ethics section. Written informed consent will be obtained using institutional review procedures, sound-delivery safeguards will limit loudness exposure, participants will receive frequent breaks, and reports will use arbitrary identifiers to protect confidentiality [1]. The testing protocol, which involves standard audiometry, DPOAE measurement, speech-in-noise screening, and masked-detection psychophysics [1], is non-invasive and falls within the routine scope of auditory research. The sound levels are specified with a ceiling: no presentation above 100 dB SPL [1], which is a reasonable safeguard given the participant population. The inclusion of age-matched controls and listeners with SNHL aged 18–80 [1] is appropriate for the research question, and the screening procedures—including audiometry, bone conduction, tympanograms, and DPOAEs [1]—are standard clinical measures that pose minimal risk.

Animal work is described with appropriate detail for ethical assessment. The proposal uses awake Dutch-belted rabbits for IC tetrode recordings, with veterinary supervision, anesthesia for surgery and any procedures causing discomfort, postoperative analgesia, gradual habituation to the recording chair, and ongoing monitoring of health and behavior [1]. The physiological task is passive listening [1], meaning animals are not required to perform behavioral tasks during recording, which reduces potential stress. Daily sessions are limited to approximately two hours [1], a duration consistent with the laboratory's established practice [14]. The animal justification anticipates using four rabbits, each for approximately 12–24 months of recording [1], which is a substantial duration. The published record from this laboratory shows animals can be maintained in good health over extended periods: Fan et al. (2022) studied two rabbits for 17–55 months and two for 13–23 months, with DPOAEs used to monitor hearing over the time course [13]. This precedent supports the feasibility of maintaining animal welfare over the proposed recording period. Electrode locations may be verified histologically at the end of the study [1], consistent with standard practice for terminal procedures.

The data sharing plan is appropriate and proportionate. Models and collected data will be shared through the laboratory website, with software provided as MATLAB or C components including compiler instructions, readme files, and executable examples [1]. Associated publications will describe parameter determination and validation, supporting reproducibility.

No environmental or operational safety concerns are identified from the described activities. The work involves standard laboratory equipment (sound-attenuated booths, auditory equipment, computational facilities [1]) and does not propose hazardous materials, field operations, or interventions that would pose risks beyond those routinely managed in auditory research laboratories. The ethical and safety measures are therefore proportionate to the activities proposed and do not raise concerns that would affect the overall judgement of the project.

## Management

The management plan is insufficient to support the ambitious scope. The proposal lacks a personnel plan specifying postdocs, graduate students, or technicians responsible for concurrent psychophysics, physiology, and modeling. Consultants are mentioned but without defined roles, time commitments, or direct task responsibility. No year-by-year schedule, milestones, or quantitative resource allocation reconciles the three interdependent arms, and no mechanism is provided for managing the substantial schedule risk if one arm falls behind. These are structural planning deficiencies that affect the core of the integrated project, not peripheral administrative details.

The proposal describes a program that must concurrently run awake-rabbit IC physiology (daily two-hour sessions across four animals maintained for 12–24 months [1]), human psychophysical testing (up to 45 participants at 12–15 hours each, with a rigid dependency requiring Aim 1 characterization before any downstream testing [1]), and iterative computational modeling that feeds into experiment design and cross-task prediction [1]. The entire resource description consists of a single passage listing existing equipment and unnamed consultants, with no personnel specified beyond the PI [1]. This is insufficient for a five-year integrated program.

No postdoctoral researchers, graduate students, or technicians are allocated to any aim. The proposal does not specify who will conduct the daily IC recording sessions, who will recruit and test the 45 human participants across the 6–8 week per-listener testing windows, or who will implement and run the individualized model simulations that must be completed between physiological and psychophysical data collection. Consultants in audiology, phonetics, and theoretical psychoacoustics are mentioned [1], but their roles, time commitments, specific aims they support, and mechanisms of coordination are unspecified. Consultants cannot substitute for the dedicated research personnel needed to execute concurrent experimental arms.

The proposal provides no year-by-year schedule, Gantt chart, or milestones. This is particularly critical given the tight dependency chain among the three arms. Individual behavioral data from Aim 1 must constrain individualized models before those models predict Aim 2 and Aim 3 results [1]. Physiology must test model predictions at the neural population level, and model results must guide which stimulus conditions are tested physiologically and psychophysically [1]. All three strands must progress in coordination, yet no mechanism is described for managing the situation where one arm falls behind. The alternatives section mentions scientific fallbacks—dorsal-cochlear-nucleus contributions can be modeled if needed, envelope phase relationships are deferred—but these are conceptual contingency plans, not schedule risk mitigations [1].

The coordinator role that the PI must play across modeling, physiology, and psychophysics is substantial, yet the proposal provides no indication of how this coordination will be achieved with the documented resources. Without a personnel plan specifying who executes each activity and a schedule reconciling the concurrent work streams, the management plan does not demonstrate that the laboratory can deliver the proposed integrated program within the stated five years.

# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project evaluates breast cancer screening performance when synthesized two-dimensional mammography replaces a conventional digital mammogram during digital breast tomosynthesis (s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT). Using a natural experiment across seven radiology facilities in Vermont and Pennsylvania that uniformly transitioned from DM/DBT to s2D/DBT, the study will pool approximately 185,700 existing screening examinations with 12-month cancer registry follow-up to compare recall, cancer detection, sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rates. It will also conduct lesion-specific analyses by histologic and exploratory molecular subtypes, and evaluate whether s2D alters clinical BI-RADS breast density assignments—all outcomes that have implications for radiation dose, overdiagnosis, and risk-based supplemental screening.

The proposal’s main strength is its efficient use of high-quality, existing PROSPR registry data with complete cancer ascertainment, common equipment (Hologic/Hologic C-View), and analysis by an experienced multicenter team. This enables the first multicenter sensitivity and specificity estimation for this comparison with 12-month follow-up, addressing a recognized gap in the meta-analytic literature. The principal limitation is the inherent observational before/after design, which is fully confounded by calendar time and cannot separate the effect of s2D from documented secular declines in recall rates, radiologist learning, and evolving practice patterns. The resulting evidence will be associational rather than causal, and the study’s detectable sensitivity difference of 5.6 percentage points is too coarse to rule out clinically consequential changes.

Weighing the evidence, this is a well-executed plan that asks a practical question and uses an efficient design, but the fundamental confounding of modality with time means the study cannot attribute observed differences to s2D itself. For an R03 mechanism, the descriptive characterization of what happened when these seven diverse facilities made the switch has real value for implementation decisions, particularly the 12-month sensitivity, lesion-specific, and density assessments that remain underexplored. The proposal would be stronger with an explicit analytical plan for secular trends, but as a rapid, low-cost evaluation leveraging irreplaceable before/after data, its contribution to screening practice justifies support at this stage.

**Rating: B (good). Confidence: A (very high).**

## Scientific value

**The question is clinically and programmatically important for contemporary breast cancer screening, and the attainable knowledge constitutes a useful multicenter characterisation of what happened when seven diverse US facilities replaced conventional digital mammography with synthesised 2D images during tomosynthesis screening.**

The replacement of a separate digital mammogram (DM) with a synthesized 2D image reconstructed from the DBT acquisition (s2D/DBT) approximately halves the radiation dose delivered to screened women, a real benefit if screening performance is maintained [1]. The proposal therefore asks a straightforward and practical question: does s2D/DBT, compared with the current standard DM/DBT, preserve screening outcomes while delivering that dose reduction? This matters to patients choosing screening modalities, to providers implementing equipment transitions, to payers evaluating cost-effectiveness under dose-reduction mandates, and to guideline groups who need practice-based evidence alongside controlled trials.

The study pools existing, prospectively collected screening records from seven facilities—three academic-centre and three community-hospital sites in Vermont, and one large urban academic medical centre in Pennsylvania—that uniformly switched from DM/DBT to s2D/DBT [1]. With approximately 185 700 examinations interpreted by 22 radiologists and complete 12-month cancer-registry follow-up, the project can deliver the first **multicentre sensitivity and specificity estimates** for this comparison using the rigorous BI-RADS fifth-edition performance framework [1]. The Hamad 2024 meta-analysis explicitly noted that “meta-analysis was not performed for sensitivity due to the low number of studies reporting on it” [2], identifying a recognised gap that the proposal directly addresses. Sensitivity and specificity, rather than recall and detection rates alone, are the measures that capture the balance of benefit and harm in screening—they distinguish false-negative examinations from true negatives using registry-ascertained interval cancers, yielding information that recall rates cannot provide [1].

Beyond the primary performance measures, the study addresses two additional outcomes that remain underexplored in the s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT comparison. **Lesion-specific analyses** (Aim 2) will distinguish invasive cancers, DCIS, and benign lesion subtypes—including exploratory molecular subtyping by ER/PR/HER2 status—informing whether s2D alters the histologic spectrum of detected disease and potentially the invasive-to-DCIS ratio, which is relevant to overdiagnosis concerns [1]. The **density-reclassification analysis** (Aim 3) examines whether s2D systematically shifts BI-RADS density assignments, a practical clinical question with direct implications for density-notification laws and supplemental-screening recommendations that affect thousands of women [1]. Both questions are clinically meaningful and insufficiently characterised by existing s2D/DBT evaluations.

**The knowledge produced is inherently associational rather than causal.** The before/after design is fully confounded by calendar time: the DM/DBT examinations were performed earlier (approximately 2011–2014/2015) and the s2D/DBT examinations later (approximately 2015–2017), with no facility maintaining concurrent DM/DBT after adopting s2D/DBT [1]. The documented secular decline in US screening recall rates of approximately 1.2 % per year [3], radiologists’ accumulating DBT-interpretation experience [4], and evolving practice guidelines during the 2011–2017 period all threaten the internal validity of the comparison. The study can provide a well-measured description of how performance changed when these facilities made the switch, but it cannot attribute those changes to s2D itself. The rigor section examines this limitation in detail.

**The value increment over the existing literature is real but modest.** Multiple meta-analyses—Zeng 2021, Hamad 2024, Houssami 2018—and large multicentre studies, including work co-authored by the PI, have already established equivalent cancer detection rates, modestly lower or equivalent recall, and slightly higher specificity for s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT [5] [6] [7] [2]. The proposal’s distinctive contribution resides chiefly in the formal sensitivity estimation with registry follow-up, the lesion-specific and density analyses, and the confirmatory multicentre evidence from diverse US practice settings that includes both rural and urban, predominantly white and predominantly African-American populations [1].

For the R03 mechanism—designed to support small, efficient research projects—this pragmatic, rapid, low-cost characterisation of screening outcomes associated with an important equipment transition represents a proportionate and useful scientific contribution.

**Rating: B (good). Confidence: A (very high).**

## Innovation

**The core comparison—s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT—is already well characterised by a mature meta-analytic and multicentre evidence base, and the proposal’s innovation lies in incremental but genuine additions that address specific residual gaps: formal sensitivity and specificity with 12-month cancer-registry follow-up, exploratory molecular-subtype analyses, and confirmatory multicentre density-reclassification evidence.**

The evidence that synthesised 2D mammography combined with tomosynthesis maintains cancer detection rates equivalent to conventional DM/DBT while reducing radiation dose is now robust. The Zeng 2021 meta-analysis of 13 studies with 1,370,670 participants found no difference in cancer detection rate between SM/DBT and DM/DBT (risk difference −0.1 per 1000 screens; 95 % CI −0.4 to 0.2; I² = 0 %) and lower recall (risk difference −0.56 %; p = 0.022) [6]. The Hamad 2024 meta-analysis confirmed comparable detection rates (difference −0.15 per 1000; 95 % CI −1.17 to 0.86) and higher specificity for DBT + S2D versus DBT + FFDM (absolute difference 1.08 %; 95 % CI 0.59–1.56) [2]. The Houssami 2018 review concluded that “comparative population screening studies have provided consistent evidence that cancer detection rates do not differ between integrated 2D/3D and s2D/3D” [5].

Large single-centre studies have contributed consistent findings. Zuckerman 2016, from one of the same centers participating in this proposal, reported on 15,571 DM/DBT and 5,366 s2D/DBT examinations and found recall of 7.1% versus 8.8% (P \< 0.001) with no significant difference in cancer detection (5.03 versus 5.45 per 1000; P = 0.72) and a 39% lower average glandular dose [8]. The proposers’ own Zuckerman 2020 multicenter study pooled 151,363 examinations at Penn and Vermont using the same data sources and found no significant differences in cancer detection rate, sensitivity, or false-negative rate after multivariable adjustment, concluding that “breast cancer screening performance is maintained within benchmarks when synthetic mammography replaces digital mammography” [7]. The Durand 2018 review summarised the field: “audit parameters for cancer detection have been preserved in the face of false positive reductions and reduced radiation dose” [9].

**The most novel element is the provision of sensitivity and specificity with complete 12-month cancer-registry follow-up in a multicentre setting.** The Hamad 2024 review explicitly noted that “meta-analysis was not performed for sensitivity due to the low number of studies reporting on it” [2], and among the four studies that did compare sensitivity for DM/DBT versus s2D/DBT, most used retrospective re-reading designs rather than prospective ascertainment with registry linkage for interval cancers [2]. No prior s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT study has combined prospective multicentre data collection with formal sensitivity and false-negative rate estimation using the BI-RADS fifth-edition framework that requires tracking cancer diagnoses for 12 months after a negative screen [1]. This combination—distinguishing true negatives from false negatives through registry-ascertained interval cancers—provides a more complete performance characterisation than recall and detection rates alone.

**The exploratory molecular-subtype analysis of invasive cancers (ER/PR/HER2) is genuinely novel in the s2D/DBT context.** The proposal hypothesises that s2D image reconstruction may alter the conspicuity of architectural distortion and calcifications, potentially shifting the histologic spectrum of detected cancers [1]. While the Malmö trial reported molecular subtypes for DBT + DM versus DM (finding similar distributions) [10] and the MAITA trials reported histological subtypes for DBT + DM [11], neither addressed s2D/DBT specifically. The concern is physiologically plausible and has not been tested empirically.

**The density-reclassification analysis is confirmatory rather than groundbreaking.** Gastounioti 2019, from the same Penn group, already reported that s2D/DBT was associated with substantially lower likelihood of high-density categorisation compared with DM/DBT (OR 0.62; 95 % CI 0.56–0.69), with significant interactions by ethnicity and BMI [12]. The Haider 2018 reader study found no statistically significant density shift between s2D and FFDM (only 4 % of patients had a clinically significant transition) [13], and the Khanani 2022 study found no difference between two Hologic s2D reconstruction versions [14]. The proposal’s preliminary data already suggest the same direction (28.6 % versus 32.5 % dense classifications) [1]. The multicentre replication across Vermont’s different patient population would add generalisability, and the density-notification implications make this clinically relevant, but the finding is more confirmatory than exploratory.

The basic recall, cancer-detection, and biopsy-rate comparisons in Aim 1 are confirmatory at this stage, although the inclusion of both academic and community settings with diverse populations—rural, predominantly white Vermont alongside urban, predominantly African-American Pennsylvania [1]—adds practice-based evidence useful for generalisability assessments that single-institution studies cannot provide. Overall, the proposal’s strongest novelty is concentrated in its formal sensitivity-specificity estimation with registry follow-up and its molecular-subtype exploration; these are incremental but they address gaps that the meta-analytic literature has explicitly recognised as open questions.

**Rating: C (moderate). Confidence: A (very high).**

## Rigor

**The observational before/after design is well-executed for its intended descriptive purpose, but it cannot support causal attribution of outcome differences to s2D substitution because calendar time is completely confounded with the modality change. The analytical methods are appropriate for the data structure, but the absence of any strategy to address secular trends is a material limitation that constrains the inferences the study can support.**

### Design strengths

The design exploits a natural experiment in which seven radiology facilities—three academic-centre and three community-hospital sites in Vermont plus one large urban academic centre in Pennsylvania—uniformly transitioned from DM/DBT to s2D/DBT, with 22 interpreting radiologists reading examinations in both periods [1]. The within-facility, within-radiologist structure eliminates between-facility confounding and avoids the selective assignment of patient subgroups to one modality that can distort unpaired comparisons: all eligible women screened after the transition received s2D/DBT, and the study excludes transitional examinations that mixed conventional and synthesized images [1]. These are genuine design strengths that distinguish the study from cross-sectional comparisons of unrelated sites.

Outcome ascertainment is a further strength that supports stronger inference than has been typical of prior s2D/DBT studies. Cancer outcomes are defined as diagnoses within 12 months after the screening examination, identified through linkage to pathology records and to the Vermont and Pennsylvania state cancer registries [1]. This permits formal estimation of sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rates—measures that require complete ascertainment of interval cancers and that the Hamad 2024 meta-analysis explicitly noted were unavailable for pooling because too few studies reported them [2].

The analytical models use generalized estimating equations with the Miglioretti–Heagerty approach to account for non-nested clustering within women, radiologists, and facilities, which is appropriate for the multilevel data structure [1]. Covariate adjustment for age, race/ethnicity, breast density, family history, and screening interval will address measured imbalances between the two periods [1].

### Fundamental confounding with calendar time

The fundamental limitation is that **modality is perfectly confounded with calendar time**. The DM/DBT examinations were performed earlier (approximately 2011–2014/2015) and the s2D/DBT examinations later (approximately 2015–2017), with no facility maintaining concurrent DM/DBT after adopting s2D/DBT [1]. Any observed difference between the two periods reflects the joint effect of the modality change plus all temporal trends occurring between the earlier and later intervals. Several such trends are particularly consequential.

**Secular decline in recall rates.** The PI’s own prior work documented a multivariable-adjusted secular decline in recall rate of 1.2% per year (95% CI 0.9%–1.5%) across US screening facilities from 2009–2017, independent of DBT adoption, estimated using a concurrent control group of 72 radiologists who never interpreted DBT [3]. Applied over the approximately three to four years separating the DM/DBT and s2D/DBT periods, this secular trend alone would produce a 3.6–4.8% absolute reduction in recall rate—an effect that overlaps substantially with the expected s2D/DBT effect.

**Radiologist learning effects.** The Malmö Breast Tomosynthesis Screening Trial demonstrated that DBT false-positive recall rates declined from 2.6% in year 1 to a stable 1.5% in years 2–5, with the proportion of stellate distortion findings dropping from 50% to 35% [4]. In the proposed study, each radiologist gains additional years of tomosynthesis interpretation experience between the DM/DBT and s2D/DBT periods. Improved performance in the later period may reflect accumulated DBT-reading expertise rather than any property of the s2D image.

**Practice-pattern changes.** The 2011–2017 period saw dissemination of updated BI-RADS benchmarks, the 2015 American Cancer Society guideline revision, and growing awareness of overdiagnosis, all of which may have influenced recall thresholds and biopsy practices downward independent of imaging technology [3].

The proposal acknowledges the observational nature of the design and describes its protections—common facilities and radiologists, uniform adoption, statistical adjustment—but it does not propose any analytical strategy to separate the modality change from these temporal effects [1]. It does not include a concurrent control group of facilities or radiologists who continued DM/DBT throughout the study period, nor does it model time-since-DBT-adoption as a covariate, nor does it incorporate the documented secular trend into its estimation approach.

### Power limitations for sensitivity

The power calculations, while appropriate for detecting the stated effect sizes under an assumption of no confounding [1], highlight an important limitation for the sensitivity endpoint. The detectable sensitivity difference of 5.6 percentage points is wide enough that a true sensitivity decline of 3–4 percentage points—a clinically concerning magnitude, representing a decline from the baseline sensitivity of 90.9% to approximately 87%—would pass undetected [1]. This limits the reassurance the study can provide about the most consequential harm of screening: missing cancers that would have been detected under the previous protocol.

### Implications for the knowledge contribution

The attainable knowledge is therefore **descriptive and associational**: the study can measure what changed after s2D adoption at these facilities, but it cannot establish that s2D caused those changes. This limitation is consistent with an observational study using existing registry data at the R03 scale, and it does not negate the study’s descriptive value. It does mean that the findings must be reported transparently as outcomes associated with the transition rather than as causal effects of s2D, and that the discussion should explicitly model the magnitude of difference that could be explained by the documented secular trend.

The comparison with the PI’s earlier single-site Pennsylvania data—which already showed similar direction and magnitude for recall (7.1% vs 8.8%), cancer detection (5.0 vs 5.5/1,000), and dense classification (28.6% vs 32.5%) [1]—suggests the multicenter study may largely confirm patterns already observed, albeit with greater precision, lesion-specific detail, and the added rigour of 12-month outcome ascertainment.

**Rating: C (moderate). Confidence: A (very high).**

## Feasibility

**The project is highly feasible: it uses existing, prospectively collected registry data that are already in hand, requires no prospective recruitment or additional imaging, and is led by an experienced team with direct prior success pooling these exact data sources.**

The proposed work consists of assembling eligible examination records from two established PROSPR research centers, pooling common data elements, linking to existing pathology and cancer registry records to establish 12-month outcomes, and performing three prespecified statistical analyses. No new mammograms are acquired, no participants are recruited, and no research radiation exposure is introduced—the study uses clinical imaging already obtained in routine care [1]. The entire dataset already exists: the screening examinations were performed between 2011 and 2017 at facilities that have already completed their uniform transition from DM/DBT to s2D/DBT [1].

Several features underpin the feasibility assessment.

**The data infrastructure is mature and proven.** The Vermont and Pennsylvania PROSPR centers have collected standardized breast screening and outcomes information since 2011 and have already collaborated on pooled analyses of screening performance and density assessment [1]. The two centers’ data align with BI-RADS, College of American Pathologists, and central cancer registry standards, and the proposal describes specific mechanisms for pathology data acquisition—standardized abstraction forms in Vermont, electronic extraction through Penn Medicine in Pennsylvania [1]. The Zuckerman 2020 multicenter study (co-authored by the PI) already pooled 151,363 examinations from these same Penn and Vermont sites using identical linkage procedures, demonstrating that the proposed data integration is not hypothetical but has been successfully executed [7].

**The analysis methods are well within the team’s demonstrated capabilities.** The study requires application of standard BI-RADS fifth-edition performance definitions—recall rate, cancer detection rate, false-negative rate, sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value [1]—and generalized estimating equations with the Miglioretti–Heagherty approach for multilevel, non-nested clustering [1]. The PI has co-authored prior work applying exactly these methods to these data, including the multicenter DBT performance studies whose estimates serve as the power calculations’ baseline parameters [1] [7]. The lesion-specific analyses use descriptive statistics and logistic regression analogous to Aim 1 [1]; the density comparisons use chi-square tests and logistic regression with adjustment for age, race/ethnicity, and body mass index [1]. None of the planned analyses requires methods development or validation that exceeds standard epidemiological practice for screening performance studies.

**The sample is already enumerated and sufficient.** The proposal specifies 98,850 DM/DBT and 86,850 s2D/DBT screening examinations interpreted by 22 radiologists, with expected cancer outcomes of approximately 1,114 cancers and 174,546 non-cancer examinations [1]. These numbers are derived from actual facility volumes during the study period and are not aspirational projections. The power calculations demonstrate adequate precision for the clinically meaningful effect sizes discussed in the scientific value and rigor sections [1].

The critical dependency—successful pooling of the Penn and Vermont datasets with consistent variable definitions—is mitigated by the prior collaborative history and the described common data elements [1]. The proposal describes a secure data transfer from Pennsylvania to Vermont for pooled analysis [1]. No insurmountable technical, regulatory, or logistical barriers are evident for a project at this scale.

**Rating: A (excellent). Confidence: A (very high).**

## Ethics and safety

**The study is minimal-risk, uses only existing clinical data obtained during routine care, and is supported by appropriate ethical oversight arrangements in both participating jurisdictions. No material risks to participants, communities, the environment, or research operations are identifiable.**

The proposed research involves no new imaging, no additional radiation exposure, and no direct participant contact. All mammography examinations were obtained as part of routine clinical screening at the seven participating facilities between 2011 and 2017. The study assembles these existing records, links them to pathology and cancer registry data, and performs statistical analyses on de-identified examination-level data [1].

The ethical oversight arrangements differ between the two centers, reflecting their respective jurisdictional requirements, but both are proportionate to the minimal-risk nature of the work. Vermont’s PROSPR center operates under passive consent, administered through an imaging questionnaire distributed to all screened women with an opt-out checkbox; the proposal reports approximately 95% participation among eligible patients [1]. Pennsylvania secured a waiver of informed consent for inclusion of women screened in its network, a mechanism appropriate for retrospective analysis of existing clinical data where obtaining individual consent from a large screened population would be impracticable and the research poses no more than minimal risk [1]. Both approaches are consistent with standard practice in large-scale screening outcomes research using registry data.

Data privacy protections are addressed. Examination-level data from Pennsylvania will be transferred securely to Vermont for pooled analysis, using established procedures within the PROSPR consortium’s collaborative infrastructure [1]. The proposal does not describe a specific data security protocol or data use agreement, which is a minor documentation gap for a study handling linked cancer registry data. However, given that the two centers have already executed exactly this data pooling in the Zuckerman 2020 multicenter study with no reported privacy incidents [7], and that they operate within the standardized PROSPR data infrastructure [1], this gap does not indicate a material ethical vulnerability.

No environmental risks are associated with the project. The work is computational and analytical—data assembly, statistical modeling, and manuscript preparation—and does not involve laboratory procedures, hazardous materials, or field operations. No animal subjects are used.

The inclusion of diverse populations—rural, predominantly white Vermont alongside urban, predominantly African-American Pennsylvania [1]—carries an associated ethical benefit: the study’s findings will be informed by screening outcomes in populations that differ substantially in race, geography, and practice setting, reducing the risk that conclusions about s2D/DBT performance generalize only to the populations most commonly represented in screening research.

**Rating: A (excellent). Confidence: B (high).**

*The confidence is lowered from very high because the proposal narrative provides only brief documentation of institutional approvals and does not specify data security protocols. These are minor documentation gaps that do not suggest any ethical deficiency, but they limit full verification from the text alone.*

## Management

**The project is well-scoped for an R03 mechanism, leverages an established collaborative infrastructure with demonstrated prior success, and is led by an investigator team with direct expertise in the data sources, analytical methods, and clinical context.**

The management structure is straightforward and proportionate to the scale of the work. The study uses existing screening registries and outcome linkage infrastructure at the Vermont and Pennsylvania PROSPR research centers [1]. The two centers have collaborated on pooled analyses of screening performance and breast density assessment since the PROSPR consortium’s inception, and they have established common data elements and linkage procedures that the current proposal directly inherits [1]. The Zuckerman 2020 multicenter study, co-authored by the PI Brian Sprague, already pooled 151,363 examinations from these same Penn and Vermont sites using identical data integration workflows, demonstrating that the proposed cross-site data assembly has been successfully executed by this team [7].

The work plan is well-defined and limited to activities appropriate for an R03 mechanism: assembling eligible examination records from both centers, pooling common data elements, establishing 12-month outcomes through linkage to pathology and cancer registry records, and completing three prespecified, coordinated analyses—performance metrics (Aim 1), lesion-specific detection (Aim 2), and density reclassification (Aim 3) [1]. No prospective data collection, equipment procurement, or participant recruitment is required. The proposal describes specific data-acquisition procedures for both sites: Vermont pathology facilities provide standardized abstraction forms and reports, while Pennsylvania obtains pathology electronically through Penn Medicine; both sites link to their respective state cancer registries for validated cancer diagnoses including cancers diagnosed outside the screening facility [1]. The Pennsylvania exam-level dataset will be securely transferred to Vermont for pooling [1].

The PI is directly experienced with these data and methods. He has co-authored prior multicenter DBT performance studies whose estimates serve as the power calculations’ baseline parameters [1], applied the proposed GEE modeling with Miglioretti–Heagerty clustering to these data [1], and contributed to the Zuckerman 2020 multicenter study that forms the most direct precedent for the current proposal [7]. The proposal describes a team combining radiology, pathology, and epidemiology expertise with experience in multicenter PROSPR collaborations, which aligns well with the project’s requirements for clinical performance metric computation, histologic classification of lesions, and multilevel statistical modeling [1].

The proposal narrative provides sufficient detail on roles and data integration to support confidence in delivery. It does not include individual-level curriculum vitae for key personnel beyond the PI, nor does it provide an itemized budget or timeline with milestones. For an R03 mechanism—where projects are expected to be small, efficient, and time-limited—the absence of these administrative details from the scientific narrative is a documentation gap rather than evidence of incapacity. The prior publication record of the investigative team, the use of existing data and infrastructure, and the well-defined analytical plan collectively provide adequate confidence that the work can be completed with the stated resources. The clear division of responsibilities—Vermont serving as the pooling center, both sites contributing their respective registry data—is logical and follows a tested collaborative pattern. No critical coordination risks beyond those already managed in prior pooled analyses are evident from the proposal.

**Rating: B (good). Confidence: A (very high).**

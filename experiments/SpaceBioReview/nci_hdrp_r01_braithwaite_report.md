# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project addresses whether the benefits of low-dose CT lung cancer screening—established in the National Lung Screening Trial for a relatively healthy population—persist among the older and sicker patients who undergo screening in routine clinical care. The research combines an observational cohort of approximately 34,000 screened adults from three diverse U.S. health systems (Kaiser Permanente Southern California, Medical University of South Carolina, VA Portland) with the UM-LCS microsimulation model from the CISNET consortium. The work has three sequential aims: characterizing the prevalence and distribution of multimorbidity, functional limitations, and impaired pulmonary function among screened patients; quantifying how these factors modify screening-related outcomes including false-positive findings, procedural complications, and cancer detection; and refining the simulation model to project long-term net benefit across comorbidity-defined subgroups. The most valuable expected contribution is a set of empirically grounded, subgroup-specific estimates of screening benefits and harms that can support individualized clinical decisions—an evidence base that current guidelines lack.

The proposal has several convincing strengths. The clinical question targets a widely recognized gap: trial participants were substantially healthier than community screening populations, and the ATS research statement explicitly called for comorbidity-stratified evidence. The team has relevant preliminary work, including validated NLP algorithms for nodule detection and prior latent-class comorbidity analyses. The three-site design captures geographic, socioeconomic, and racial/ethnic diversity beyond what trials provide. The five-year timeline is realistic, with clear phasing from data assembly through analysis to modeling. However, material limitations affect the strength of the study's conclusions. The observational cohort includes only screened patients, making it impossible to estimate screening effectiveness directly—healthy screenee bias and confounding by indication cannot be fully resolved. The proposal relies on Aim 3's microsimulation to supply the unscreened counterfactual, but the specification for translating cohort estimates into model parameters is incomplete, and the risk of circularity when the same cohort supplies both input parameters and comparison subgroups is not adequately addressed. The FRI score, while validated for mortality, has not been validated for predicting screening complications in this population, and its equal weighting of heterogeneous indicators may limit discrimination.

The central trade-off is between the strength and timeliness of the clinical question and the observational limitations of the design. The proposal identifies an urgent knowledge gap: whether real-world patients with multimorbidity benefit from lung cancer screening to the same degree as the healthier NLST participants. The three-aim architecture—characterizing comorbidity, estimating how multimorbidity modifies screening harms, and using microsimulation to project net benefit—is conceptually well-structured and builds on established infrastructure (validated NLP, CISNET models, Watch the Spot data model). The published Aim 1 results already confirm that the PLuS cohort has substantially higher comorbidity than NLST, validating the project's premise. However, the screened-only design cannot directly estimate screening effectiveness, and the microsimulation's credibility depends on assumptions about comorbidity-conditioned competing mortality and treatment effectiveness that are not fully specified. These limitations are partially mitigated by the division of labor across aims, but they prevent the project from being a definitive resolution of the question. On balance, the project will produce a valuable empirical resource and advance the evidence base meaningfully, even if its simulation-based net benefit estimates require cautious interpretation. This is worthwhile work that addresses a real clinical need with proportionate methods.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**The question is both clinically important and methodologically timely, addressing a widely recognized evidence gap with a design capable of producing practically consequential knowledge.**

The central problem is well-established. Lung cancer screening with low-dose CT reduces lung cancer mortality—the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) reported a 20% reduction and approximately three fewer lung cancer deaths per 1,000 people screened [1]. But NLST participants were substantially healthier than the community populations now being screened. Estimated life expectancy was 21.2 years for NLST participants versus 14 years for a Medicare screening-eligible subgroup; rates of diabetes (9.7% vs. 24.1%), heart disease (12.7% vs. 33.2%), and stroke (2.8% vs. 11.6%) were markedly lower among trial participants [1]. The conceptual framework that net benefit need not rise monotonically with baseline lung cancer risk—it may peak at intermediate risk and decline when competing mortality and treatment-related harms dominate—is both intuitive and supported by emerging evidence [1]. COPD illustrates this tension: it is associated with roughly two- to threefold greater lung cancer risk, but advanced disease also increases procedural complications, mortality after lung resection, and competing respiratory mortality [1]. In the NLST, false-positive findings affected almost 40% of participants over the screening rounds, and some individuals with benign nodules underwent needle biopsy, bronchoscopy, or surgery [1]. In older and sicker populations, the balance may shift further.

**The proposal's three-aim architecture is designed to generate precisely the kind of evidence that current screening guidelines lack.** Aim 1 will characterize the prevalence and distribution of multimorbidity, functional limitations (measured by the FRI score), and impaired pulmonary function (spirometry-based GOLD categories) among approximately 34,039 adults undergoing screening across three geographically and demographically distinct health systems: Kaiser Permanente Southern California (KPSC, integrated care, ethnically diverse), the Medical University of South Carolina (MUSC, safety-net hospital), and VA Portland Health Care System [1]. The published results from this grant's Aim 1, reported in Braithwaite et al. (2025), confirm that the PLuS cohort (n=31,795) has substantially higher comorbidity than NLST—COPD 32.7% vs. 17.5%, diabetes 24.6% vs. 9.7%—validating the project's foundational premise that real-world screening populations differ meaningfully from trial participants. These findings demonstrate the empirical foundation of the project's premise and confirm that the cohort can deliver the promised characterization.

Aim 2 will quantify how these multimorbidity measures modify screening-related harms: false-positive findings, procedure-related complications (pneumothorax, pulmonary hemorrhage, acute respiratory failure, myocardial infarction within 7 and 30 days of diagnostic biopsies), and cancer detection rates [1]. Aim 3 will then incorporate these cohort-derived estimates into the CISNET-validated UM-LCS microsimulation model to project long-term net benefit across comorbidity-defined subgroups under alternative screening strategies, varying start and stop ages, frequency, pack-years, quit years, COPD status, and comorbidity eligibility [1].

**The attainable knowledge is both descriptive and practically consequential.** The project will produce the first empirically grounded, subgroup-specific estimates of how comorbidity, functional limitation, and pulmonary impairment jointly modify LCS benefits and harms in routine clinical care. Its expected products—a harmonized multi-site clinical cohort, quantitative estimates of screening harms stratified by multimorbidity level, and a refined simulation model capable of comparing alternative screening eligibility strategies—directly address the knowledge gaps identified by the American Thoracic Society research statement on incorporating coexisting chronic illness into LCS patient selection. The Almatrafi et al. (2022) systematic review confirmed that comorbidities are highly prevalent in LCS populations but that research on their impact on screening outcomes is lacking, identifying this as "a priority in LCS research" [2]. The PLuS study is positioned to fill this gap.

Even the study's acknowledged limitation—that the screened-only observational design cannot directly estimate screening effectiveness—is substantially mitigated by the simulation component. The Kale et al. (2024) study demonstrated that a similarly structured CISNET model can credibly project how comorbidities reduce life-years gained from screening by 19–29%, with competing mortality rather than complications driving the reduction [3]. The current proposal extends this approach by adding functional and pulmonary measures absent from prior work and by drawing inputs from a purpose-built real-world screening cohort rather than secondary trial and survey data. The resulting evidence can directly inform the individualized screening discussions that the Caverly et al. (2018) preference-sensitivity framework identified as clinically necessary, where moderate differences in preferences about the downsides of LDCT screening influenced whether screening was appropriate for eligible persons with annual lung cancer risk less than 0.3% or life expectancy less than 10.5 years [4].

## Innovation

**The project's contribution lies in empirical integration rather than in novel methods, representing a meaningful but incremental advance over prior work.** It is the first study to combine granular, multi-site real-world EHR data—including functional limitation and pulmonary function measures beyond comorbidity counts—with a validated CISNET microsimulation model to estimate comorbidity-stratified lung cancer screening outcomes. This synthesis advances the field beyond what existing studies have achieved individually, though the constituent methods are adapted rather than newly developed.

**What the project adds beyond prior comorbidity-stratified LCS research can be understood by comparing it with three key precedents.** The Caverly et al. (2018) microsimulation study identified life-expectancy and lung-cancer-risk thresholds below which screening becomes preference-sensitive, finding that for patients with annual lung cancer risk below 0.3% or life expectancy under 10.5 years, moderate differences in preferences could alter whether screening was appropriate. That work was a modeling study using trial-derived inputs and a simulated US-representative population; it did not use real-world observational data on actual screened patients, nor did it empirically estimate how specific comorbidity profiles affect downstream outcomes [4].

The Kale et al. (2024) study extended a CISNET microsimulation model to incorporate comorbidity-specific parameters for COPD, diabetes, heart disease, and stroke, demonstrating that comorbidities reduce life-years gained from screening by 19–29% and that competing mortality, rather than complications, drives the reduction [3]. However, that study used secondary data (NHIS for mortality, PLCO for complications) and did not include functional limitation or pulmonary function measures [3]. The current proposal advances beyond both studies by adding the FRI score (13 claims-based indicators of functional vulnerability) and structured spirometry (FEV1/FVC, GOLD categories A–D) alongside Charlson/Elixhauser comorbidity scores, and by drawing these inputs from a purpose-built screening cohort across three diverse health systems rather than secondary analyses of trial and survey data [1].

The Rendle et al. (2024) study of 9,266 screened patients across five US health systems reported downstream procedure and complication rates from real-world screening, finding complication rates substantially higher than in NLST (30.6% vs. 17.7% for any complication) [5]. The current proposal's advance over that work is the systematic stratification of these outcomes by multimorbidity, functional status, and pulmonary function, coupled with microsimulation for net benefit projection.

**The empirical foundation, not the methods, constitutes the novel contribution.** The microsimulation methods (UM-LCS discrete-state model, multistage carcinogenesis framework), the statistical approaches (discrete-time survival models, generalized propensity scores, multiple imputation), and the FRI score (adapted from Segal et al. 2017) are well-established rather than newly developed. The project's innovative contribution is their combined application: generating real-world, comorbidity-conditioned estimates of screening process parameters (false-positive rates, Lung-RADS adherence, biopsy rates, complication rates, competing mortality) and feeding these into a validated simulation to project subgroup-specific net benefit. The PLuS protocol states that "to our knowledge, this is the first study to combine observational data and simulation models to estimate the long-term impact of LCS with LDCT" [6], and the available literature supports this claim. The published Aim 1 results confirm what this added resolution yields: 18.0% of the PLuS cohort had a frailty index above 0.20 and 16.9% had FEV1 below 50% predicted—dimensions entirely absent from prior microsimulation work [7]. The Braithwaite et al. (2025) results paper appropriately describes the incorporation of these measures as "a major innovation and step toward precision LCS" [7].

**The contribution is best characterized as a well-designed synthesis filling a clearly defined evidence gap.** The Myeong et al. (2026) Korean gastric cancer screening study, which used propensity score matching of screened to unscreened individuals (n=555,904), found that comorbidity (CCI ≥3) reversed the mortality benefit of screening—a design that could directly estimate how comorbidity modifies screening effectiveness because it included unscreened controls [8]. The proposed study cannot replicate this directly but represents a complementary strategy: using empirical harm estimation plus simulation to approximate what a randomized trial stratified by comorbidity would require. This is a pragmatic response to the absence of randomized data on screening effectiveness by comorbidity status in lung cancer, and it extends the research agenda that the ATS statement called for.

## Rigor

**The three-aim design is logically structured and the statistical methods are appropriate for the descriptive and harm-estimation goals, but the observational architecture cannot directly estimate screening effectiveness, and the microsimulation's capacity to fill this gap depends on specification details that the proposal does not fully resolve.**

### Strengths of the design

The design's architecture is its strongest feature. Aim 1 characterizes the prevalence of multimorbidity, functional limitations (FRI score, 0–13 scale), and impaired pulmonary function (FEV1/FVC below 70%, GOLD categories A–D) among approximately 34,039 screened adults across three health systems [1]. This is straightforward descriptive epidemiology, well-supported by the harmonized data infrastructure (Watch the Spot common data model, validated NLP algorithms for nodule identification with 96% sensitivity and 92% specificity) [1].

Aim 2 uses discrete-time survival models, indexed by screening examination number, to estimate how these multimorbidity measures modify screening-related harms—false-positive findings, procedure-related complications within 7 and 30 days, and cancer detection rates—across repeated screening rounds [1]. The time-varying exposure specification (comorbidity, FRI, and pulmonary function updated between observations) is a strength, as is the plan for generalized propensity scores and multiple imputation to address confounding and missing data [1]. Power calculations project 34,039 screened people with 902 incident cancers, yielding 80% power to detect a 5% absolute difference in procedure-related complications and 1.3–1.9% differences in false-positive risk across comorbidity levels [1]. These methods are appropriate for the question Aim 2 actually asks: whether patients with more comorbidity experience higher rates of screening-related harms *among those who undergo screening*. The Rendle et al. (2024) study of 9,266 screened patients provides a direct precedent for this approach, and the proposal advances beyond that precedent by explicitly modeling how multimorbidity modifies these rates using time-varying exposures across repeated screening rounds [5].

### The screened-only limitation and the role of Aim 3

The fundamental limitation is that the cohort includes only screened patients, making it impossible to estimate screening effectiveness directly. Three distinct sources of bias apply. First, healthy screenee bias—the systematic difference between people who undergo screening and those who do not—cannot be addressed without an unscreened comparison group. NLST participants had an estimated life expectancy of 21.2 years versus 14 years for Medicare screening-eligible individuals [1], and the proposal's own Table 1 documents substantial differences in comorbidity prevalence between trial and community populations [1]. Second, confounding by indication operates at the level of screening uptake (healthier individuals may be more likely to be referred for and accept screening) and at the level of multimorbidity-outcome relationships (patients with multimorbidity may have differential healthcare utilization, generating more encounter-based diagnosis codes). Third, differential healthcare utilization may increase the probability of incidental nodule detection, diagnostic coding, and procedure recording independently of screening effects. The propensity scores proposed in Aim 2 address confounding *across multimorbidity levels among screened patients*, not selection into screening itself.

The proposal mitigates this through Aim 3's microsimulation, which supplies the unscreened counterfactual from independently sourced parameters. The UM-LCS model draws cancer natural history inputs from SEER, NLST, and PLCO, and will incorporate cohort-derived estimates for screening process parameters (complication rates, Lung-RADS adherence, false-positive rates) and competing mortality, all conditioned on comorbidity, functional limitation, and pulmonary function [1]. This division of labor—empirical harm estimation plus model-based net benefit projection—is conceptually sound, and the Kale et al. (2024) study demonstrated the feasibility of this general approach [3].

### Specification gaps in the simulation

Three specification gaps affect confidence in the simulation results. First, the proposal does not specify the functional form for translating cohort-derived competing mortality estimates into model parameters—whether hazard ratios will replace, blend with, or calibrate against existing CISNET mortality tables. Second, it does not describe how comorbidity, FRI, and pulmonary function inputs will be jointly conditioned, particularly whether competing mortality will be modeled as additive or multiplicative across these dimensions. The proposal identifies three cohort-derived inputs requiring conditioning on multimorbidity—competing mortality, complication rates, and Lung-RADS adherence [1] [6]—but does not specify how separate stratifications by Charlson/Elixhauser score (0, 1, ≥2), FRI score (0, 1, ≥2), and pulmonary function category will be combined into a joint parameterization.

Third, and most consequential, the proposal does not address circularity: when the same cohort supplies both input parameters (e.g., competing mortality for the Charlson ≥2 subgroup) and the subgroup definitions used for strategy comparison, the simulation's predictions for that subgroup are partly determined by inputs estimated from it. The proposal partially mitigates this by drawing many model inputs from external sources (SEER, NLST, PLCO, CISNET) [1], but the complication and adherence parameters are cohort-derived. A data-splitting or cross-validation strategy would strengthen the independence of model evaluation but is not described. The PLuS protocol does note that the refined model results will be compared with the original trial-based model [1], which provides one form of validation, but this does not address the issue of whether cohort-estimated inputs pre-determine subgroup comparisons.

### Measurement limitations of the FRI score

The FRI score introduces additional measurement uncertainty. While Segal et al. (2017) validated the underlying Claims-based Frailty Indicator against the Fried frailty phenotype (AUC 0.75), the score has not been validated for predicting procedural complications from lung biopsy or the competing-mortality calculus specific to lung cancer screening. The 13 indicators include items directly relevant to procedural risk (respiratory failure, oxygen use) alongside items less clearly connected (depression, urinary catheter use), yet the unweighted count (0, 1, ≥2) treats all as equally informative [1]. This loses the differential predictive information that some indicators carry—for instance, respiratory failure and oxygen use are likely far more predictive of complications from transthoracic needle biopsy than depression or urinary catheter use, but the count treats them identically. The proposal mitigates this partly by including spirometry-based pulmonary function measures alongside FRI, but an analysis plan that treats FRI, comorbidity, and pulmonary function as separate predictors, with backward selection based on Akaike's Information Criterion, may not adequately explore the interactions where clinically meaningful risk stratification likely resides. A patient with well-controlled COPD who uses supplemental oxygen would have an FRI of 1, yet might tolerate bronchoscopy or needle biopsy well; another patient with advanced emphysema and borderline respiratory function who does not trigger any FRI claim might nonetheless be at high risk for pneumothorax or respiratory failure after biopsy.

**Overall, the design supports its intended descriptive and harm-estimation contributions well.** The screened-only limitation is inherent to the design but is addressed through the simulation component, even though specification gaps in that component temper confidence in the net benefit projections. The published Aim 1 results confirm the cohort's capacity to deliver the promised characterization; Aims 2 and 3 remain prospective but build on demonstrated infrastructure and methods.

## Feasibility

**The project has a strong operational foundation, with demonstrated data infrastructure, validated tools, and an experienced team, making the empirical aims highly feasible; the main uncertainty lies in the microsimulation refinement for Aim 3, where specification gaps introduce development risk rather than evidence of infeasibility.**

### Established data infrastructure and validated tools

The coordinating center at KPSC will reuse the Watch the Spot (WTS) common data model, which already includes 13 groups of standardized elements covering eligibility, demographics, smoking, nodules, utilization, comorbidity, and vital status [1]. Each site will implement a common data dictionary using structured EHR exports, NLP, and text mining from administrative records, ICD-9/10 and CPT/HCPCS codes, radiology and pathology reports, tumor registries, and mortality data [1]. The NLP algorithm for pulmonary nodule identification is already developed and validated—96% sensitivity and 92% specificity in the original work, with the expanded version capturing laterality, lobe, size, attenuation, edge, and calcification at reported sensitivity and specificity above 95% [1]. These are not tools under development; they are operational instruments that reduce technical risk for Aims 1 and 2 substantially.

### Adequate cohort and statistical power

The proposal projects approximately 34,039 screened adults and 902 incident cancers, based on an estimated 2% cancer detection rate [1]. Power calculations show 80% power at two-sided alpha 0.05 to detect a 5% absolute difference in procedure-related complications and 1.3–1.9% differences in false-positive risk across comorbidity levels, assuming at least 70% have a consequential comorbidity and approximately 20% have COPD [1]. The published Aim 1 results from the same grant, covering 31,795 patients, confirm the cohort's capacity to deliver the promised characterization—COPD prevalence of 32.7%, diabetes 24.6%, frailty index above 0.20 in 18.0%, and FEV1 below 50% predicted in 16.9% demonstrate both adequate sample size and sufficient variation in the exposures of interest [7]. The retrospective data cover 2016–2019 and prospective data extend through 2022, providing temporal depth for the time-varying exposure specifications in Aim 2 [1].

### Operational diversity across sites

The three health systems provide operational diversity that supports generalizability without threatening execution. KPSC provides integrated care with established screening order sets distinguishing screening, diagnostic, and nodule-follow-up examinations. MUSC uses EHR notifications and referral to its LCS program. VA Portland employs an EHR eligibility algorithm, clinical reminders, and a dedicated screening nurse coordinator [1]. Each site has existing screening programs with defined workflows, meaning the study is capturing patients already undergoing care rather than implementing new clinical protocols—a design that simplifies data collection compared with prospective intervention studies. Census linkage will supply area-level socioeconomic measures, and Medicare linkage will help identify diagnoses or treatment outside the participating systems, providing additional data completeness [1].

### Microsimulation refinement risk

The UM-LCS model is a CISNET-validated platform with documented inputs and performance characteristics [1]. The Kale et al. (2024) study, which shares several co-authors and extended a related CISNET model with comorbidity-specific parameters—competing mortality from NHIS data, complication probabilities from PLCO, and quality-of-life utilities from the literature—demonstrates the general feasibility of the approach [3]. The current proposal extends this framework by adding functional limitation and pulmonary function as conditioning dimensions, and by drawing inputs from the PLuS cohort rather than secondary data.

However, the proposal does not fully specify how cohort-derived estimates will be translated into model parameters, nor how joint conditioning across comorbidity, FRI, and pulmonary function dimensions will be achieved. These specification gaps mean the difficulty of the Aim 3 development work cannot be fully assessed from the current description. The five-year timeline allocates Years 4–5 to model refinement and Aim 3 analysis, and the strategy of beginning simulation work early (Year 1, second half) while cohort data accumulate is a prudent use of the timeline—but the unspecified translation steps mean that Aim 3 represents the phase where execution risk is highest. This does not make Aim 3 infeasible; it makes it the component whose difficulty is least well characterized in the current proposal.

## Ethics and safety

**The study presents minimal material risk to participants and applies proportionate data protection measures consistent with the observational design.** The research involves retrospective and prospective analysis of existing clinical and administrative records; it does not include experimental interventions, specimen collection, or direct participant contact. The ethical considerations center on data privacy, informed consent requirements for secondary data use, and the potential for group-level findings to influence screening recommendations for vulnerable populations.

**Data protection and regulatory arrangements are well-specified.** The proposal outlines a multi-layered approach to data security [1]. Each participating institution will maintain a secure patient database with names, medical record numbers, and dates of birth linked to unique study IDs and stored separately from study data. The coordinating center at KPSC will use a HIPAA-compliant common data structure, consistent with the mature Watch the Spot infrastructure already in operation [1]. Data transfer from MUSC and VA Portland to KPSC will use REDCap with encrypted transmission and authenticated, authorized access [1]. These measures reflect standard practice for multi-site EHR-based research and are proportionate to the sensitivity of the data—which, while individually identifiable at source, will be de-identified for analysis. The PLuS protocol states that safety considerations include protection of patient confidentiality [6], and the published IRB approvals from KPSC and VA Portland confirm that regulatory review has occurred [6].

Regulatory approvals will be sought at all study sites [1]. As a secondary analysis of data collected during routine clinical care, the study likely qualifies for waiver of individual informed consent—a standard determination for retrospective observational research using de-identified or limited datasets. The proposal describes this process briefly rather than exhaustively, but the mention of regulatory approvals at all sites and the published confirmation of IRB approval provide adequate assurance for a study of this type.

**Responsible use of findings for vulnerable subgroups is anticipated.** A less formal but ethically significant consideration arises from the study's goal of identifying subgroups for whom screening may not be beneficial [1]. Findings that certain patients with advanced comorbidity, functional limitation, or severe COPD derive little or no net benefit from screening could influence clinical practice, insurance coverage, or guideline development. The proposal anticipates this by including patient advocates in the interpretation and dissemination process to inform patient-centered decision support [1]. This is an appropriate safeguard: the goal is to inform individualized discussions rather than to produce categorical exclusion criteria. The Caverly et al. (2018) framework that the project builds on explicitly frames screening decisions as preference-sensitive for certain patient groups, which preserves patient autonomy rather than imposing algorithmic restrictions [4].

**The ethics documentation is adequate for the study type, though some dimensions receive limited attention.** The ethics section focuses on data security rather than on broader ethical dimensions. It does not describe community engagement processes, procedures for returning incidental findings (if any), or data governance arrangements beyond regulatory compliance. These are minor documentary gaps rather than ethical deficiencies; they do not affect the judgment that the study is ethically appropriate for its design and aims. The published PLuS protocol and IRB approvals fill some of these gaps [6], and the inclusion of patient advocates provides a mechanism for addressing the distributive and communication implications of the findings.

## Management

**The management arrangements are well-organized, demonstrate appropriate expertise across all required domains, and follow a realistic schedule supported by established infrastructure—providing strong confidence that the stated aims can be delivered as planned.**

**The team covers all necessary domains.** The multidisciplinary group includes epidemiology (Braithwaite), thoracic oncology and health services research (Gould), interventional pulmonology (Silvestri), CISNET microsimulation (Meza), and statistics (Shen, Fan) [1]. Braithwaite has prior experience with comorbidity characterization in lung cancer populations, including the latent-class analysis at KPSC that identified five comorbidity profiles associated with treatment selection and survival [1]. Gould leads the data coordinating center with established expertise in pulmonary nodule management and the Watch the Spot infrastructure [1]. Meza provides the UM-LCS model, already validated in comparative screening analyses [1]. Patient advocates are included to inform interpretation and dissemination for patient-centered decision support [1]. The team has demonstrable prior collaboration: the published PLuS protocol and the Braithwaite et al. (2025) results paper confirm that this group has already worked together to assemble the cohort and complete Aim 1 analyses [6] [7].

**Coordination and data governance rely on established infrastructure.** The coordinating center at KPSC provides centralized oversight for capture, mapping, transfer, management, and Aims 1–2 analyses, with clear leadership assignments (Gould and Shen) [1]. The reuse of the Watch the Spot common data model reduces the coordination burden by providing a pre-specified vocabulary and infrastructure rather than requiring de novo development [1]. Each site will implement a common data dictionary using its available systems, with Lung-RADS standardization for LDCT findings and a HIPAA-compliant structure at the coordinating center [1]. Regular coordination through monthly calls and annual meetings provides adequate oversight for a five-year project [1].

**The schedule is appropriately phased.** Regulatory approvals, subcontracts, and IRB review occupy the first half of Year 1. Cohort development—covering both retrospective data (2016–2019) and prospective enrollment (2020–2022)—begins in the second quarter of Year 1 and continues through Year 3, overlapping with the Aims 1 and 2 analyses that run from Year 1 through Year 4. Simulation refinement for Aim 3 begins in the second half of Year 1 and extends through Year 5, allowing the cohort-derived input estimates from Aims 1 and 2 to mature before the intensive modeling work. Manuscript preparation occupies Years 3–5, consistent with phased publication of descriptive results, outcome analyses, and simulation findings [1]. This phasing is realistic: the data assembly and empirical analyses precede the simulation work that depends on them, and the published Aim 1 results already demonstrate that the cohort can deliver the planned characterization within this timeframe [7]. The schedule assumes a January 2020 start [1], and the strategy of beginning simulation literature review and refinement early (Year 1, second half) while cohort data accumulate is a prudent use of the timeline.

**Resource adequacy is well-documented.** The proposal documents the data sources required for model inputs across all three aims, with appropriate mapping to external sources (SEER, NLST, PLCO, CISNET) for parameters not derived from the cohort [1]. No critical resource gaps are evident. The main dependency that is external to the team's direct control—successful data harmonization across three health systems with different EHR platforms—is addressed by the reuse of established infrastructure and the centralized coordinating center. The published results confirm that this integration has been successfully achieved for Aim 1 [7].

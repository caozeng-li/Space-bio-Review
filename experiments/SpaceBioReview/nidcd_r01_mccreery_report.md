# Project review

## Overall assessment

This R01 proposal from Ryan McCreery investigates why children with mild-to-severe hearing loss who use hearing aids continue to have difficulty understanding speech in noise even when amplification improves access to speech. The project addresses a persistent clinical problem—school-age children learn and socialise in noisy settings, yet current hearing-aid fitting approaches cannot explain or remediate their remaining deficits. The planned work will examine three interlinked processes across 80 children with hearing loss and 80 children with normal hearing, aged 6–12, with 24-month longitudinal follow-up: (1) the sensory and cognitive contributions to spectral resolution measured through improved ripple-discrimination tasks with manipulated memory demands, (2) individual differences in the frequency bands children rely on for word recognition and how those weighting strategies develop, and (3) how selective attention, working memory, and language affect recognition of speech masked by steady-state noise versus competing talkers. A mature adult comparison group anchors the weighting measures.

**The most valuable expected knowledge is an integrated developmental account of how sensory, cognitive, and linguistic factors combine to produce the speech-in-noise difficulties that amplification alone cannot resolve, providing the necessary groundwork for future individualised hearing-aid fitting based on a child's perceptual strategy rather than average audibility targets alone.**

The proposal's main supporting evidence comes from the team's established research programme and documented infrastructure. The investigators have published extensively on the same population, including longitudinal data from 290 children showing persistent speech-in-noise deficits with parallel growth trajectories [1], cross-sectional evidence that vocabulary and working memory predict masked speech recognition [2], and pilot data demonstrating feasibility of the spectral-ripple and band-importance procedures. Their recent Bosen et al. (2025) study found developmental shifts in frequency-importance weights in 42 normal-hearing children [3], providing the immediate foundation that this proposal extends to children with hearing loss. Recruitment through Boys Town and the University of Iowa builds on the successful OCHL study that enrolled 317 children with hearing loss under comparable criteria [4]. The two-site design, shared measures across aims, preregistration, and data-sharing plans further support quality.

**Three limitations materially affect the judgement.** First, the path and mediation models from observational data cannot confirm the causal structure the three-route framework implies, though the team appropriately treats the framework as generating testable association predictions. Second, the band-importance estimation captures main-effect weights that conflate independent band contributions with cross-frequency interactions—Bosen et al. (2026) show only moderate correlation between main-effect and interaction-inclusive weights [5]—and individual-weight reliability has not been directly quantified. Third, the longitudinal arms for spectral resolution and perceptual weights lack established effect-size benchmarks and pilot test-retest reliability, making their capacity to detect developmental change uncertain, unlike the well-supported speech-in-noise trajectories. A further consideration is that the visual-domain attention tasks chosen (Flanker, Switcher) have not been validated against the auditory attention measures that the team's own work showed did not predict performance in children with hearing loss [6]—the proposal does not explain why visual tasks should overcome this limitation.

**Weighing the contribution against the limitations, this proposal deserves support.** The persistent deficit in speech-in-noise perception among children with hearing loss is a clinically and educationally important problem that current amplification approaches do not solve. The proposed work would deliver the first systematic characterisation of how spectral resolution, individual perceptual weighting strategies, and informational masking susceptibility develop in this population—knowledge that is acknowledged as a critical gap in the field and necessary groundwork for individualised fitting. The main design limitations constrain the strength of mechanistic conclusions but do not undermine the descriptive and developmental value of the data. The team's explicit consideration of alternative hypotheses and their track record with this population further support confidence that the study will produce informative results under a range of possible outcomes. The longitudinal arms for spectral resolution (Aim 1c) and perceptual weights (Aim 2c) should be treated as exploratory extensions whose primary value is generating preliminary developmental estimates and test-retest benchmarks, rather than confirmatory tests of the bidirectional mediation hypotheses, unless pilot reliability data become available that justify the planned growth models.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**This project addresses a persistent, clinically important problem that current amplification cannot solve, and it would deliver descriptive and developmental knowledge that is acknowledged as a critical gap in the field.** Children with mild-to-severe hearing loss who use hearing aids continue to struggle with speech in noise even when amplification improves access to speech, and this deficit persists without evidence of convergence with normal-hearing peers through at least age 11 [7] [1]. Because children learn and socialise in noisy settings, routine school and social participation depends on understanding speech in these conditions. The research community has explicitly identified the need for spectral-resolution and frequency-weighting data in children who use hearing aids as an imperative priority. The Jahn et al. (2022) review concluded that "very little research has been conducted to characterize frequency or spectral resolution development in children who use hearing aids, and it is imperative that future work focuses on that population" [8].

### Knowledge the project would produce

The proposal would produce three knowledge contributions that the field currently lacks.

First, it would provide the first systematic dissociation of sensory from cognitive contributions to spectral resolution in children with hearing loss, using improved narrowband, level-roved ripple-discrimination tasks with manipulated interstimulus intervals to vary memory demands [7]. Prior work from the same team has already shown that spectrotemporal ripple thresholds correlate with nonverbal intelligence and working memory in children who are hard of hearing, and the proposal directly confronts the resulting interpretative ambiguity—that poor thresholds may reflect sensory resolution, task demands, or both—rather than ignoring it [7] [9].

Second, it would deliver the first measurement of individual frequency-band importance weights in children with hearing loss and their 24-month longitudinal change. The recent Bosen et al. (2025) study established that weights undergo developmental shifts in normal-hearing children aged 6–13, with increasing importance at 2 and 4 kHz with age, but found no cognitive predictors after controlling for age and did not include children with hearing loss [3]. The proposal extends this foundation to the population where it matters clinically.

Third, it would provide a direct developmental comparison of energetic versus informational masking that tracks how audibility, language, and attention interact over time, building on the team's prior findings that vocabulary and audibility—rather than attention—predicted two-talker speech recognition in children with hearing loss [6].

### Character of the attainable knowledge

The attainable knowledge is primarily descriptive and developmental rather than causal. The three-route framework—separating attention to spectral features, inhibition of less useful frequency information, and segregation of target from masker speech, with working memory and language as modulating factors—organises the investigation and generates testable association predictions [7]. The longitudinal design adds within-child developmental covariation patterns to the cross-sectional associations.

However, the path and mediation models from observational data cannot confirm the causal structure the framework implies. Mediation analysis from cross-sectional data has well-known limitations for causal inference: it assumes correct temporal ordering, no unmeasured confounding of the mediator-outcome relationship, and no measurement error in the mediator—assumptions that are rarely met in practice [10]. The proposal acknowledges this through its explicit treatment of alternative outcomes and bidirectional relationships: "failure to find the predicted mediation would still characterize the sensory-cognitive relation" [7], and stable weights would be "a meaningful alternative that would support the current assumption of temporal stability" [7]. The knowledge produced—normative developmental trajectories of spectral resolution, individual perceptual weights, and masking susceptibility in children with hearing loss, together with their cognitive and linguistic correlates—would be necessary groundwork for any future effort to individualise hearing-aid fitting based on a child's perceptual strategy rather than average adult frequency-importance functions alone [7].

### Constraints on the contribution

The knowledge contribution is constrained in two further respects. First, the band-importance estimation captures main-effect weights that conflate independent band contributions with cross-frequency interactions. Bosen et al. (2026) have shown that pairwise interactions between bands contribute substantially to speech recognition, and that including interaction terms reduces individual band importance estimates, with the largest decreases above 1 kHz [5]. The correlation between main-effect-only and interaction-inclusive band weights is only approximately *r* = 0.57 [5]. This means group or developmental differences in main-effect weights could partially reflect differences in how bands are combined synergistically rather than genuine differences in frequency-specific cue use. The proposal acknowledges that weights represent "the contribution of each band averaged across its combinations with other bands" [7] but does not address how interaction differences between age groups or hearing-status groups could confound the interpretation of weight differences.

Second, the exclusion criteria—bilateral hearing loss, no cochlear implants, nonverbal cognition within 1.5 SD, no secondary disabilities, hearing parents using spoken English, at least 25% word recognition—create a relatively selected sample [7]. This is scientifically appropriate for isolating the mechanisms of interest, but it means the findings will not generalise to the substantial proportion of children with hearing loss who have additional disabilities, use cochlear implants, or come from non-English-speaking homes. Each exclusion is justified for internal validity, but the cumulative effect narrows the scope of generalisability. These constraints do not negate the substantial descriptive and developmental value the study would provide for its target population; the selected sample is arguably the most relevant reference group for the clinical goal of developing individualised amplification strategies for hearing-aid-using children with bilateral mild-to-severe loss.

**Rating: B (Confidence: B).** The clinical and educational importance of persistent speech-in-noise deficits, combined with the field's explicit recognition of the knowledge gap, supports a strong judgement of scientific value. The design limitations—the observational nature of the causal framework and the conflation of band-importance weights with cross-frequency interactions—constrain the theoretical scope and the strength of mechanistic conclusions, without undermining the substantial descriptive and developmental knowledge the study would produce.

## Innovation

**This proposal makes three advances that no single study or programme has yet delivered, each extending prior work into a critical gap that the field has explicitly identified as an imperative future focus.** The combined cross-sectional and 24-month longitudinal design across three linked aims, each addressing a distinct component of the proposed three-route framework, would produce an integrated developmental account that the current literature cannot provide.

### Dissociation of sensory from cognitive contributions to spectral resolution

Prior work established that spectrotemporal ripple thresholds correlate with working memory and nonverbal intelligence in children who are hard of hearing [9], but the confound between sensory resolution and task demands remained unaddressed. The present proposal explicitly confronts this ambiguity through an interstimulus-interval manipulation—varying retention demands across 0.4, 1.2, and 3.0 seconds—that tests whether cognition–ripple associations strengthen with increasing memory requirements [7].

This experimental gradient of task demand represents a genuine methodological innovation over the field's prior practice of treating ripple thresholds as pure sensory measures. The manipulation is grounded in known properties of auditory sensory memory: at short intervals (approximately 600 ms), discrimination relies substantially on the echoic sensory trace, whereas at longer intervals beyond roughly 2.5 seconds, long-term memory representations and active maintenance become necessary [11]. The improved narrowband, level-roved carriers at two centre frequencies (500 and 2000 Hz) further refine measurement specificity compared with the broadband spectrotemporal tasks used in earlier work [7].

### Individual band-importance weights in children with hearing loss

The team's recent Bosen et al. (2025) study demonstrated developmental shifts in band-importance weights—increasing weighting at 2 and 4 kHz with age—in 42 normal-hearing children aged 6–13, using the same filtered-word recognition procedure proposed here [3]. That study, however, found no cognitive predictors of weights after controlling for age and explicitly concluded that "additional work is needed to determine whether the developmental trajectory of frequency importance weights is affected by hearing loss in children who are hard of hearing" [3].

The current proposal directly addresses this gap by extending band-importance estimation to children with hearing loss (Aim 2b), using a hearing-aid simulator under headphones to allow precise control of band sensation levels and to examine whether poorly audible or poorly resolved bands contribute little to recognition [7]. Both ears are initially tested, and asymmetry between ears with symmetric audiograms can help distinguish shared cognitive influences from ear-specific audibility or resolution effects. The longitudinal arm (Aim 2c) follows 50 CNH and 80 CHL over 24 months to test whether growth in selective attention shifts weighting from higher to middle frequencies after controlling for language, and whether increased aided sensation level increases the contribution of affected bands [7]. No prior study has measured how aided audibility, spectral resolution, and cognitive development jointly shape which frequency regions a child with hearing loss relies on for word recognition.

### Developmental comparison of energetic versus informational masking

The team's own Lalonde et al. (2023) study found that selective attention predicted two-talker speech recognition in normal-hearing children but not in children with hearing loss, where vocabulary and aided audibility were the primary predictors instead [6]. That cross-sectional finding was identified as requiring larger samples and longitudinal data to characterise developmental trajectories. The proposal's Aim 3 directly responds to this call, with 80 children per group tested in both speech-shaped noise and two-talker speech maskers, and followed over 24 months [7].

### The three-route framework as a conceptual advance

The three-route framework itself represents a conceptual innovation over existing models. The Ease of Language Understanding model makes the general prediction that cognition supports degraded speech perception [2], but the proposal's framework—separating attention to spectral features, inhibition of less useful frequency information, and segregation of target from masker speech—is more specific and makes distinct, falsifiable predictions about which cognitive constructs should matter for which listening tasks under which masking conditions [7]. The framework specifies that working memory can affect all tasks requiring storage and processing, while language matters particularly when target or masker content is speech, with audibility acting immediately on signal access and cumulatively through language development.

**Rating: A (Confidence: B).** The three advances each target gaps that the field has explicitly identified as priorities. The interstimulus-interval manipulation for dissociating sensory from cognitive variance in ripple tasks is a methodological advance. The extension of band-importance measurement to children with hearing loss and longitudinal follow-up addresses a question with direct clinical relevance. The developmental masking comparison responds to a specific finding—that hearing loss alters the cognitive predictors of speech-in-speech perception—from the team's own recent work. The three-route framework generates more specific, falsifiable predictions than existing general models. The moderate confidence reflects the dependence of the innovation on measurement reliability that has not yet been fully established for the novel perceptual-weighting measures in the CHL population.

## Rigor

**The design has meaningful strengths—improved measurement methods, theoretically motivated experimental manipulations, systematic comparison of masking conditions, and longitudinal follow-up with reproducibility commitments—but three limitations constrain the strength of mechanistic inference relative to the stated aims.** The study is well-positioned to produce high-quality descriptive and developmental data; its ability to confirm the causal routes posited by the three-route framework is more limited.

### Strengths in measurement and design

The proposal directly addresses a known confound in spectral-ripple measurement: that poor thresholds may reflect sensory resolution, task demands, or both [7]. The interstimulus-interval manipulation (0.4, 1.2, and 3.0 seconds) in Experiment 1b provides a theoretically grounded gradient of memory demand [7]. The narrowband carriers with level roving at two centre frequencies improve frequency specificity compared with the broadband spectrotemporal carrier used in the team's earlier work. Separate measurement of visual and nonverbal working memory (AWMA Block Recall, Odd One Out) reduces the circularity of using auditory memory to predict auditory task performance [7]. These are real methodological refinements.

The design also includes appropriate contingency plans. If children cannot tolerate the standard 20-dB sensation level, testing proceeds at a lower level and absolute presentation level is included in interpretation [7]. If SNR50 tracks show poor repeatability, the procedure can target 71% correct to improve reliability [7]. If attention measures become less informative near adolescence, other age-appropriate tasks may be substituted [7]. The planned preregistration, blinded scoring of speech responses, and release of analysis code and de-identified data through the Open Science Framework and GitHub further support reproducibility [7].

### Limitations in causal inference

The most important limitation concerns causal inference. The three-route framework posits distinct causal pathways—attention to spectral features, inhibition of less useful frequency information, and segregation of target from masker speech—and the path and mediation models are presented as tests of whether effects are "direct, mediated, or both" [7]. However, mediation analysis from observational data cannot confirm causal structure [10]. The longitudinal component (three waves over 24 months) addresses temporal ordering to some degree, but with only three time points at 12-month intervals, the design cannot resolve the timescale on which sensory and cognitive processes interact—likely seconds to minutes rather than months.

The proposal appropriately treats alternative outcomes as informative—"failure to find the predicted mediation would still characterize the sensory-cognitive relation" [7], and stable weights would be a meaningful alternative supporting the clinical assumption of temporal stability [7]—but the framework figure implies stronger causal structure than the design can support. What the analyses will yield is a well-characterised set of associations and developmental covariation patterns, not a causal decomposition of the three routes.

### The SSN–TTS comparison and informational masking

The speech-masking comparison (speech-shaped noise versus two-talker speech) is the standard paradigm for contrasting energetic and informational masking, and the team's prior work has established that masker type matters: vocabulary predicts performance across masker types, while attention predicts two-talker speech recognition in CNH but not CHL [6]. However, the SSN–TTS subtraction does not yield a pure measure of informational masking. Buss et al. (2021) demonstrated that variability in speech-in-speech recognition often attributed to informational masking can arise from stimulus-driven differences in energetic masking: "Although variability in speech-in-speech recognition is often thought to reflect differences in informational masking, the present results suggest that variability in energetic masking across stimuli can play an important role in performance" [12]. The two masker types differ not only in informational content but also in spectrotemporal structure—SSN is steady-state while TTS has fluctuating amplitude envelopes that create glimpsing opportunities. This does not invalidate the comparison, but it narrows the specificity of what is being measured.

### Band-importance estimation and attention measurement

Two measurement choices warrant attention. The band-importance estimation procedure uses logistic regression that captures only the main effect of each frequency band, averaged across its co-occurrence with other bands [7] [13]. Bosen et al. (2026) have shown that pairwise interactions between bands contribute substantially to speech recognition, that including interaction terms reduces individual band importance estimates, and that main-effect-only and interaction-inclusive band weights correlate only moderately (*r* = 0.57) [5]. While main-effect-only functions remain "a valid approximation" [5], group or developmental differences in cross-frequency interaction structure could confound the interpretation of weight differences. The individual-weight reliability of the proposed procedure has also not been directly quantified. The Shen and Kern (2018) qBIF procedure, which uses a different adaptive approach, showed that standard deviations of spectral weights across listeners were approximately 0.05–0.08 on the log-odds scale [14], which is appreciable relative to the between-subject variability against which group differences and individual-difference correlations are tested.

The attention measures—visual-domain Eriksen Flanker and Switcher tasks [7]—have not been validated against the auditory attention measures that showed ceiling effects and null associations with two-talker performance in children with hearing loss in the team's own recent work. Lalonde et al. (2023) found that CHL were clustered near the ceiling on the NEPSY-II auditory attention measure, with 41.5% scoring at least 63 out of 66, and that attention predicted TTS performance in CNH but not CHL [6]. The proposal hypothesises that stronger attention predicts reduced susceptibility to competing speech [7] but does not explain why switching from auditory to visual attention measures should overcome the prior null finding. The Hedge et al. (2018) meta-analysis further showed that RT costs and error costs from flanker-type tasks correlate only weakly (mean *r* = 0.17), because individual differences in response threshold confound subtraction-based cost measures [15], raising questions about what construct the visual-domain measures actually capture.

### Longitudinal power and detection of change

The longitudinal design is well-powered for speech-in-noise trajectories (Aim 3c), where prior work from the same team provides clear effect-size benchmarks. Walker et al. (2019) demonstrated with 290 children that SNR-50 decreases by approximately 2.2 dB from first through fourth grade, with parallel growth trajectories for CNH and CHH [1]. Over a 24-month window, expected improvement of roughly 1.0–1.5 dB is detectable with the proposed sample.

For spectral resolution (Aim 1c) and perceptual weights (Aim 2c), the evidence for meaningful developmental change over 24 months in school-age children is thinner. The Jahn et al. (2022) review concluded that spectral resolution matures gradually, with performance continuing to improve through the teenage years, though spectral ripple performance may mature between 8 and 11 years of age [8]. The proposal's pilot data for the longitudinal perceptual-weighting component consists of only five CNH—"older children place more weight at two kHz and less at four kHz than younger children" [7]—an observation consistent with the hypothesis but far from establishing a developmental trajectory. With only 50 CNH in the longitudinal arm for Aim 2c and three time points, detecting a developmental shift in weights or confirming the alternative of temporal stability faces substantial uncertainty.

**Rating: B (Confidence: B).** The improved measurement methods, systematic masking comparisons, contingency plans, and reproducibility commitments are strengths. The design limitations—the gap between the causal framework and the observational evidence, the conflation of band-importance weights with cross-frequency interactions, the unvalidated attention measures, and the uncertain power for detecting developmental change in novel perceptual measures—constrain the strength of mechanistic inference. These issues do not undermine the study's capacity to produce valuable descriptive and developmental data, but they mean the three-route framework will receive associative support rather than causal confirmation, and the longitudinal findings for spectral resolution and perceptual weights will be more exploratory than the proposal suggests.

## Feasibility

**The recruitment targets are ambitious but achievable given the team's documented infrastructure and prior track record with comparable populations.** The plan targets approximately 150 children with normal hearing, 200 children with hearing loss, and 25 normal-hearing adults across five years at two established sites—Boys Town National Research Hospital and the University of Iowa [7]. Both institutions maintain participant databases and have extensive experience with paediatric audiology, cognitive and language testing, and longitudinal analyses from the Outcomes of Children with Hearing Loss (OCHL) study, which provides the closest precedent.

### Evidence from prior recruitment

The OCHL study, led by the same investigator team, applied comparable criteria—bilateral better-ear pure-tone average of 25–75 dB HL, no cochlear implants, English as primary language, no significant cognitive or motor delays—and enrolled 317 children with hearing loss from 430 initial contacts, a 73.7% yield [4]. The present proposal extends the audiological range slightly (20–80 dB HL), adds explicit nonverbal cognition and word-recognition thresholds, and targets an older age range (6–12 years versus 6 months–7 years).

Several differences between the two studies cut in both directions. The explicit cognitive criterion (≥1.5 SD on WASI Matrix Reasoning, approximately the 7th percentile) will exclude children with significant intellectual disability but is unlikely to eliminate more than 10–20% of the audiologically eligible pool. The ≥25% word recognition threshold is permissive, screening out only children with essentially no usable speech perception. The OCHL study undersampled children with mild losses [4]; the proposal's broader audiological range may improve mild-loss representation. The older age range may reduce the pool of newly identified children but facilitates referral from school-based and clinical audiology services.

### Effect of the combined eligibility criteria

The requirement of hearing parents using spoken English and absence of secondary disabilities removes additional children. German register data indicate comorbidities in approximately 40–50% of children with bilateral hearing loss [16], though many of these—vision impairment, medical conditions—would not necessarily reduce nonverbal IQ below the 1.5 SD threshold. The exclusion of cochlear implant users removes children with severe-to-profound losses. Each exclusion is justified for the mechanistic questions asked, but the cumulative filter means the resulting sample represents children with bilateral mild-to-severe hearing loss who use hearing aids, have typical-range nonverbal cognition, possess at least minimal speech recognition, and come from English-speaking hearing families. For the clinical goal of developing individualised amplification strategies, this selected sample is the most relevant reference group, even though findings will not generalise to children with cochlear implants, unilateral loss, or significant comorbid disabilities.

### Throughput and retention

The five-year timeline with overlapping aims allows cumulative recruitment across approximately 30 new CNH and 40 CHL per year across two sites, a rate comparable to the OCHL study's throughput. The scheduling table confirms appropriate reuse of participants across experiments, reducing the total number of unique children required [7].

The planned 10% attrition buffer may be optimistic relative to the OCHL experience. Walker et al. (2019) reported that of 290 children, only 57 (20%) contributed all four annual visits, while 88 (30%) contributed only one visit [1]. The present study's shorter follow-up (24 versus 36-plus months) and older age range may improve retention. The 10% figure is acknowledged in the proposal as an estimate, and the plan to recruit beyond the per-experiment targets to account for attrition and children aging out of the age range provides a buffer [7]. Nonetheless, a more conservative attrition assumption grounded in the OCHL experience would strengthen confidence. Children enrolled near the upper age boundary contribute less longitudinal data before exceeding the target age range, effectively reducing the sample available for estimating developmental slopes. Because the age band spans 6 years, the sample is age-heterogeneous at each wave, and age heterogeneity within waves competes with developmental change between waves for statistical power.

**Rating: B (Confidence: B).** The recruitment infrastructure is strong and the team has successfully enrolled comparable populations. The ambitious 90% retention rate contrasts with the OCHL study's retention patterns, and the combination of eligibility criteria creates a selected sample with limited generalisability—though the mechanistic questions asked are appropriately served by this focused population. The overall recruitment targets are achievable, but the longitudinal retention assumptions warrant caution.

## Ethics and safety

**The proposal describes ethical and safety measures that are proportionate to the minimal-risk nature of the planned behavioural testing, with appropriate attention to consent, hearing protection, data governance, and clinically relevant feedback to families.**

The study involves no invasive procedures, no pharmacological interventions, and no experimental medical devices. The primary activities are psychophysical listening tasks, standardised cognitive and language assessments, audiometric measurement, and probe-microphone verification of hearing-aid output. These procedures are routine in paediatric audiology research and the characterisation as minimal risk is consistent with established practice.

The consent and assent framework is appropriately described. Children and caregivers will complete applicable consent and assent procedures, with opportunities to address questions during study visits [7]. The inclusion of child assent alongside caregiver consent respects the developing autonomy of participants aged 6–12. The proposal further describes a mechanism for sharing clinically relevant audiological, language, or cognitive findings with a child's care or education team, subject to caregiver permission [7]. This is a meaningful ethical provision: children in the study undergo comprehensive assessment that may identify previously undetected changes in hearing, language, or attention with real implications for clinical management and educational support.

Two specific physical risks are addressed with concrete mitigations. Auditory signals will be limited below potentially damaging levels through hardware and software safeguards, essential given that the study exposes children—including those with hearing loss—to amplified speech and noise stimuli for extended periods [7]. Probe-microphone measurements, which require insertion of a flexible tube into the ear canal, can cause discomfort, and the proposal specifies that discomfort will be addressed by removing and repositioning the tube [7]. Both measures are proportionate and specific to the procedures involved.

The two-site design will operate under a single institutional review board arrangement, which streamlines oversight and ensures consistent ethical standards across sites [7]. Data management provisions are comprehensive: research records and audio-video recordings will be protected by access controls and password-protected storage; published reports will not identify individual children; and de-identified data will be shared through the Open Science Framework under agreements that restrict reidentification and require secure use, with return or destruction of data after analysis [7]. These provisions align with current standards for human-subjects data sharing and appropriately balance reproducibility benefits against privacy interests in a potentially identifiable clinical population.

One area the proposal does not address in detail is management of child fatigue or distress during extended testing sessions. The burden table indicates individual visit durations of two to three hours [7], which is substantial for children aged 6–12. The proposal does not describe planned rest breaks, criteria for discontinuing a session, or procedures for responding to child distress. The research team's extensive experience with paediatric populations provides reasonable assurance that age-appropriate testing procedures will be followed, and the omission reduces thoroughness rather than raising concern about unmanaged risks.

**Rating: B (Confidence: B).** The consent, hearing-protection, data-governance, and clinical-feedback measures are well-specified and proportionate. The absence of detail on managing extended-session fatigue is a gap that warrants attention during implementation but does not indicate unmanaged risks to participants.

## Management

**The two-site design leverages established infrastructure and investigator expertise directly relevant to the proposed work. The team's track record with the OCHL study—enrolling 317 children with hearing loss and following them longitudinally under comparable multi-site conditions—provides the strongest evidence that the necessary coordination and operational capacity exist [4].**

### Site infrastructure and expertise

The proposal distributes recruitment and data collection across Boys Town National Research Hospital and the University of Iowa, with each site contributing approximately half the child sample, drawing on existing participant databases and clinical audiology services [7]. This dual-site arrangement provides geographic catchment diversity and reduces the risk that a single-site bottleneck could stall the entire project.

The investigators' combined expertise covers the necessary domains: paediatric audiology and hearing-aid fitting (McCreery, Walker, Lewis), psychoacoustics and speech perception (Buss, Leibold), longitudinal developmental methods (Walker, Oleson), and band-importance methodology (Bosen). The shared-measures strategy—standardised cognitive, language, and audiological assessments administered across aims, with participants reused when feasible—reduces duplication and the total number of unique children needed (approximately 150 CNH and 200 CHL across five years, plus 25 adults) [7].

### Schedule and resource demands

The five-year schedule is logically structured, with overlapping aims that stagger the heaviest data-collection demands. Aim 1 cross-sectional experiments (1a, 1b) run in years 1–2, with longitudinal follow-up (1c) extending through year 3. Aim 2 begins in year 2, with cross-sectional data collection through year 3 and longitudinal follow-up through year 4. Aim 3 launches in year 3, with cross-sectional work through year 4 and longitudinal follow-up through year 5. Manuscript preparation windows are allocated for each experiment [7].

The primary management concern is the convergence of multiple data-collection streams in years 3–4, when Aim 1 longitudinal visits, Aim 2 cross-sectional and longitudinal testing, and Aim 3 cross-sectional data collection all overlap temporally [7]. The burden table indicates a total of approximately 2,100 subject-visit hours across the project, with the heaviest concentration in the middle years [7]. Two-to-three-hour testing sessions with school-age children, some of whom have hearing loss, require careful scheduling and adequate staffing. The proposal does not provide detailed staffing allocations by year or describe contingency plans for managing concurrent demands. The documented OCHL experience provides reasonable assurance that the team can manage this complexity, but the compressed later-year schedule would benefit from more explicit resource planning.

### Reproducibility and analysis provisions

The proposal describes centralised analysis protocols using linear mixed models and path analysis, with model selection guided by AIC or BIC and empirical variable reduction addressing collinearity before hypothesis testing [7]. Experimental designs and analyses will be preregistered, a subset of speech responses will be independently scored by a blinded examiner, and experimental software, analysis code, and de-identified data will be released through the Boys Town GitHub repository and the Open Science Framework [7]. These provisions support analytic consistency across sites and reproducible research outputs.

**Rating: B (Confidence: B).** The two-site infrastructure, investigator expertise, shared-measures strategy, and reproducibility provisions are strong. The compressed later-year schedule and the absence of detailed staffing allocations by year are manageable concerns given the team's experience, but more explicit resource planning would strengthen confidence.

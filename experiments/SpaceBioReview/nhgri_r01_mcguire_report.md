# Project review

## Overall assessment

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) is transitioning from research into clinical diagnosis and management, yet the regulatory, intellectual property, and reimbursement frameworks that govern genetic testing were largely built for single-condition tests, not for a technology that generates findings of heterogeneous validity, clinical significance, and utility [1]. This proposal investigates how emerging commercial and clinical WGS models interact with those existing policy frameworks. The project combines a systematic landscape analysis of the WGS industry, semistructured interviews with industry leaders, a five-round modified Policy Delphi with approximately 40 diverse stakeholders, and formal legal and policy analysis across three domains: analytical validity and testing quality, patents and licensing, and reimbursement and payment [1].

The project’s most valuable expected contribution is an integrated package of knowledge: a contemporaneous, structured map of the WGS industry; a stakeholder-validated, prioritized list of the most important and tractable policy questions; and a set of concrete, thoroughly analyzed policy alternatives with documented areas of stakeholder agreement and disagreement [1]. The integration architecture — landscape → Delphi prioritization → legal and policy analysis → policy alternatives → meeting deliberation → dissemination with active policy engagement — is more than the sum of its parts, because each stage grounds the next in documented evidence rather than general impressions or investigator-selected questions [1]. The dissemination plan, including year-two and year-three Capitol Hill briefings and follow-up meetings with federal officials and congressional staff, is designed to place findings in front of decision-makers during the project rather than after its conclusion [1].

The proposal is strongly supported by a well-justified rationale that WGS’s distinctive characteristics — heterogeneous findings, separable technology and interpretation services — challenge a policy framework designed for narrow-purpose tests [1]. The explicit commitment that the Delphi process seeks to map stakeholder perspectives rather than force consensus is a methodological strength that distinguishes this study from conventional consensus-seeking Delphis and is well-suited to surfacing political and ethical trade-offs [1]. The investigative team has a demonstrated track record in the component methods and policy domains: the Genetics and Public Policy Center’s landscape and stakeholder engagement work, Cook-Deegan’s intellectual property expertise, Tunis’s payer and health technology assessment experience, Deverka’s reimbursement research, and Messner’s Delphi methodology [1] [2]\[證據91\].

The main limitations affecting the judgement are the incremental nature of the innovation and the absence of detailed contingency planning for several realistic execution risks. The individual methods and three policy domains are substantially continuous with the team’s own published work on next-generation sequencing (NGS) policy, including the Curnutte et al. (2014) landscape analysis and the Messner et al. (2016, 2017) modified Policy Delphi studies [3] [4] [5]. What this proposal adds — WGS specificity, a fifth Delphi survey, formal white papers, and enhanced dissemination — represents genuine but modest refinements rather than a novel departure. The research design is generally rigorous, though the handling of Delphi feedback by stakeholder group and the criteria for interpreting composite scores are insufficiently specified. The schedule is logically sequenced but compressed, with a heavy year-2 loading, and critical dependencies including panel recruitment, retention, and industry interview access are acknowledged but lack concrete contingency plans.

On balance, the value of a coherent, timely, and actionable policy package for a crucial technology at a formative stage outweighs the project’s limited novelty, and the execution risks are within the team’s capacity to manage.

### Recommendation: Support

The project as a whole represents a worthwhile scientific contribution with a sufficient current route to its stated goals. It will produce a valuable, integrated package of policy-relevant knowledge including a systematic industry landscape, validated priorities, and concrete policy alternatives for clinical WGS integration.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**The project addresses a timely and consequential policy problem with a coherent research architecture that can produce knowledge valuable for near-term clinical translation decisions.** The central question — how the emerging commercial and clinical models of whole genome sequencing interact with the fragmented legal, regulatory, intellectual property, and reimbursement frameworks governing genetic testing — is important precisely because it arises at a formative moment when technological capability is outpacing the policy infrastructure designed for single-condition tests [1]. The attainable knowledge falls into three integrated layers, each with distinct value.

**Industry landscape (Aim 1).** Aim 1 will produce a contemporaneous, structured map of the WGS industry: organizations developing sequencing instruments, providing sequencing services, and offering clinical annotation and interpretation, characterized by their technologies, business models, patents, health claims, CLIA certification, and third-party coverage arrangements [1]. This map serves both as a standalone public resource for researchers and policymakers tracking an industry in transition, and as the evidentiary foundation for the subsequent Delphi process [1]. The value of a WGS-specific landscape at this moment lies in its capacity to reveal precisely which activities — equipment manufacturing, sequencing, interpretation, or delivery to clinicians — are actually being performed, by whom, and under what regulatory assumptions, thereby connecting policy analysis to documented industry realities rather than to abstract possibilities [1].

**Prioritized policy questions and alternatives (Aim 2).** Aim 2 will produce a stakeholder-validated, prioritized list of policy questions and a set of concrete alternative policy approaches for three selected issues spanning analytical validity and testing quality, patenting and licensing, and reimbursement and payment [1]. The value of the prioritized list extends beyond the three issues the team selects for detailed analysis: questions falling outside the selected domains, including those the proposal identifies as consequential but beyond the team’s core expertise — privacy, data ownership, workforce, equitable access — will remain visible in the published priorities rather than being treated as answered [1]. The policy alternatives themselves are designed to capture a range of choices differing in timing, mechanisms, allocation of burdens, and federal and state involvement, with explicit documentation of where stakeholder positions converge and where they diverge [1]. This mapping of agreement and disagreement is a knowledge product in its own right, because it tells policymakers what the relevant constituencies can and cannot currently agree upon without negotiation or compromise.

**Integration and dissemination (Aim 3).** The integration architecture — landscape → Delphi prioritization → legal and policy analysis → policy alternatives → meeting deliberation → dissemination with active policy engagement [1] — produces a package whose collective value exceeds the sum of its parts. The landscape ensures that the Delphi panel deliberates on documented industry conditions rather than on general impressions; the Delphi process ensures that the legal and policy white papers address questions that stakeholders have actually ranked as important and tractable rather than questions chosen solely by the research team [1]. This sequential grounding from empirical description through structured stakeholder input to formal policy analysis is a more complete policy development pipeline than any single component method could deliver. The dissemination plan further amplifies practical value: year-two and year-three Capitol Hill briefings, follow-up meetings with federal officials and congressional staff, webcasts, issue briefs, and publicly accessible electronic materials [1] are designed to place findings directly in front of decision-makers during the project rather than after its conclusion.

**The knowledge contribution is primarily descriptive, synthetic, and policy-analytic** rather than mechanistic or hypothesis-driven, and this is appropriate to the project’s stated purpose. The proposal describes its contribution as “a systematic description of the WGS industry, a prioritized set of policy questions, and alternative approaches to three questions selected for both importance and tractability” [1]. This framing is realistic: the project will not resolve the policy questions it examines, nor is it designed to produce primary evidence of clinical utility, cost-effectiveness, or patient outcomes. Its contribution lies in organizing what is known and what is believed by the relevant actors into a form that supports policy deliberation.

## Innovation

**The project’s contribution is best characterized as an incremental extension of a well-established research program rather than a novel departure in either method or domain.** The individual methods — structured environmental scan of the genomics industry, modified Policy Delphi, and legal and policy analysis — are each well-precedented, including by the same investigator team. The primary value lies in the integration architecture and the specific application to WGS clinical integration with an active dissemination model, rather than in breaking new methodological or conceptual ground.

**Continuity with prior work.** The three policy domains organizing this project — analytical validity and testing quality, patents and licensing, and reimbursement and payment [1] — are substantially continuous with the team’s prior work. The Curnutte et al. (2014) Nature Biotechnology paper from the same team conducted a web-based analysis of 100 clinical NGS companies supplemented by 19 in-depth industry leader interviews, identifying 68 companies producing hardware, software, reagents, and services for the clinical NGS pipeline [5]. That analysis was organized around the pre-analytic, analytic, and post-analytic and informatics phases and explicitly addressed how the industry was responding to regulatory and reimbursement uncertainty [5].

More directly, the Messner et al. (2016, 2017) modified Policy Delphi studies, conducted by essentially the same team (Messner, Cook-Deegan, Majumder, Javitt, Deverka, Bollinger, Curnutte, McGuire), used a 48-expert multi-stakeholder panel across the identical three domains of intellectual property, coverage and reimbursement, and FDA regulation to first rank 29 policy challenges and then deliberate solutions for the four most important [3] [4]. The Majumder et al. (2021) modified Policy Delphi on a cancer gene variant commons, also involving Cook-Deegan and McGuire, used semi-structured interviews to generate issue statements, a survey to rank them by validity, importance, and feasibility, and a full-day virtual meeting to generate policy options across five domains [6].

Within the broader literature, landscape analyses of commercial genomics are documented by Hogarth, Javitt, and Melzer (2008), who surveyed the direct-to-consumer genetic testing market including over two dozen companies and characterized regulatory frameworks [7]; by Wiechers, Perin, and Cook-Deegan (2013), who tracked commercial genomics from 1990–2004 using firm databases and patent searches [8]; and by Agarwal et al. (2013), who assembled commercial landscape information on noninvasive prenatal testing from trade press, news, and business publications, again involving Cook-Deegan [9].

Policy Delphi exercises are similarly well-precedented beyond this team’s work: Federico et al. (2025) conducted a four-round Delphi with 34 European stakeholders to develop a value assessment framework for NGS in oncology [10], and the IC2PerMed project used three Delphi surveys to identify 65 priorities for a joint EU-China personalized medicine roadmap [11]. The Boulkedid et al. (2011) systematic review of 80 Delphi studies for healthcare quality indicator selection found that a modified Delphi with a physical meeting was used in 63% of studies, with a median panel size of 17, making the proposed approximately 40-person panel and two-day meeting conventional rather than innovative in design terms [12].

**What the current proposal adds.** The refinements are genuine but incremental. They include: a WGS-specific rather than NGS-generic focus, justified by WGS’s distinctive characteristics of producing heterogeneous findings across different validity and utility [1]; five Delphi surveys rather than the four used in the Messner studies, with the additional round devoted to open-ended generation of policy approaches before ranking [1]; formal legal and policy white papers prepared by three institutions — Genetics and Public Policy Center, Duke, and the Center for Medical Technology Policy — to ground the policy alternative development, where prior Delphi exercises relied primarily on panel expertise and team background research [1]; a two-day in-person meeting with anonymous real-time voting designed to explore complexity and disagreement rather than to force consensus [1]; an explicit methodological self-evaluation component assessing the perceived usefulness of the Delphi method itself [1]; and an embedded dissemination model including year-two and year-three Capitol Hill briefings and feedback mechanisms [1].

**Where the primary contribution lies.** The most distinctive programmatic contribution is the integration architecture: a systematic WGS industry landscape that feeds directly into the stakeholder panel’s identification and prioritization of policy questions, which in turn feeds into formal legal and policy analyses that ground the development of alternative policy approaches, with the entire process embedded in an active dissemination model that includes ongoing policy engagement [1]. This pipeline represents a more complete and logically connected policy development process than any single prior study, including the team’s own. The forced prioritization to exactly one issue per domain — using the Delphi results and advisory board input to select the most important and tractable issue in each of the three policy areas — is a meaningful advance over the Messner studies, which selected four top issues without guaranteeing domain coverage [1].

A reviewer who weights methodological novelty heavily will see limited innovation; a reviewer who values the systematic assembly and integration of evidence for near-term policy decision-making will see the architecture and dissemination plan as a meaningful step forward.

## Rigor

**The research design is generally fit for purpose, with the modified Policy Delphi architecture well-matched to the project’s goal of mapping stakeholder perspectives rather than forcing consensus.** However, two methodological choices — the handling of Delphi feedback and the absence of pre-specified interpretation criteria for the composite scores — modestly reduce the rigor with which the stated inferences can be supported, without undermining the design’s core capacity to deliver the promised outputs.

### Delphi survey design and its capacity to deliver

The five-survey, two-phase structure follows the logic of Turoff’s Policy Delphi framework. Surveys 1 and 2 iteratively generate, comment on, and re-rate policy questions on importance, validity, and technical and political feasibility, producing composite scores — Priority (importance + validity, 0–8), Tractability (technical + political feasibility, 0–8), and Overall (sum of all four, 0–16) — that support a sortable, annotated priority list [1]. Surveys 3–5 then move to the three selected issues, gathering open-ended suggestions, ranking resolutions for desirability and feasibility, and rating arguments for importance and validity, with iterative feedback between rounds [1]. This structure can credibly produce the two outputs the proposal promises: a prioritized list of policy questions and a set of alternative policy approaches with documented areas of agreement and disagreement [1].

The explicit commitment that consensus is not the intended endpoint, and that minority views will be documented, is a strength of the design that distinguishes it from consensus-seeking conventional Delphis [1]. The final meeting’s first purpose — to verify whether staff have accurately represented preferred options, apparent agreement, and reasons for disagreement [1] — reinforces this orientation.

### Landscape analysis methods

The documentary search strategy for Aim 1 triangulates across PubMed, Internet searches, NEXIS media database, company websites, the USPTO database, and LexisNexis, supplemented by direct company contact [1]. This multi-source approach is appropriate for an emerging industry without standardized indexing. The collected data fields — technologies, services, customers, patents, health claims, CLIA status, and third-party coverage — map directly onto the three policy domains [1].

The interview component, drawing up to 20 participants from at least 40 eligible organizations with purposive sampling to thematic saturation, is supported by empirical evidence. Young and Casey (2018) found significant code coverage at 6–9 interviews, partial theme representation at 4–6, and substantial theme completion at 7–10 across three distinct qualitative projects, while noting that these findings assumed participants met predetermined criteria and described similar experiences [13]. The WGS industry’s diversity — spanning instrument manufacturers, service laboratories, informatics companies, and academic centers — means the interview findings will map the range of models and pathways rather than achieving deep saturation within any single segment. This is appropriate for the landscape’s role in grounding the Delphi panel [1].

### Feedback handling and the influence of panel composition

Two specific methodological choices reduce the rigor with which the Delphi process can support the claimed inferences.

First, the Campbell et al. (1999) randomized trial, cited in the proposal, demonstrated that the type of feedback given between rounds significantly influences ratings: participants receiving collective (whole-panel) feedback were influenced by other professional groups’ ratings compared to those receiving group-only feedback [14]. A subsequent Campbell et al. (2004) study confirmed that different stakeholder groups “have diverse views of quality of care and these differences translate into how they rate quality indicators” [15]. The proposal states that feedback will include median values and composite scores but does not specify whether feedback will be disaggregated by constituency group [1]. Without such disaggregation, the iterative re-rating that is central to the Delphi method may unintentionally shift individual ratings toward the group mean, obscuring rather than preserving the differences in perspective that the policy Delphi is designed to surface. This is a meaningful gap given that the three broad constituencies — industry, healthcare, and regulatory, policy, and patient groups [1] — are selected precisely because they hold different knowledge and interests [1].

Second, the proposal does not specify what threshold will define a “high-priority” or “tractable” question, nor does it set a priori criteria for when agreement or disagreement on policy resolutions will be considered meaningful [1]. The Boulkedid et al. (2011) systematic review found that 77% of Delphi studies reported their consensus method, but there was no standardized definition across studies [12]. In a policy Delphi where the goal is mapping distributions rather than binary inclusion or exclusion, the absence of pre-specified thresholds is not fatal — medians, distributions, and the spread of ratings can serve as the primary outputs. The proposal should, however, state this explicitly rather than leaving the composite scores’ interpretation implicit.

### The final meeting and the transition to face-to-face interaction

The final meeting’s design mitigates some of these concerns: anonymous real-time voting and the explicit purpose of verifying whether staff have accurately represented agreement and disagreement [1] provide a check on the survey-round findings. The meeting does, however, introduce face-to-face interaction after five rounds of anonymous surveys, which, as Boulkedid et al. note, “contradicts one of the basic rules of the Delphi procedure, which is avoidance of situations that might allow one of the panel members to dominate the consensus process” [12]. The proposal’s use of an experienced facilitator (Tunis) and the meeting’s positioning as a validation rather than consensus-forcing exercise are appropriate mitigations.

**In summary,** the design supports the intended inferences — that the outputs will represent a credible, stakeholder-informed mapping of policy questions and alternatives — but the unspecified feedback handling and the absence of interpretation criteria introduce avoidable uncertainty about the precision and stability of the resulting rankings.

## Feasibility

**The project can be carried out with the stated methods and resources, but three critical dependencies — panel recruitment, panel retention, and industry interview access — could delay the work without substantive contingency planning.** The team’s track record, the advisory board’s role, and the proposal’s general monitoring provisions provide meaningful mitigation, leaving these as manageable rather than disqualifying risks.

### Year 1 activities and interview access

The year 1 activities — landscape documentary search, industry leader interviews, and initial dissemination — are well within the capacity of the Genetics and Public Policy Center (GPPC) team, which has conducted comparable landscape analyses of genetic testing markets and demonstrated proficiency in semistructured interviews with industry informants [1]. The Curnutte et al. (2014) study, involving a web-based analysis of 100 NGS companies and 19 in-depth interviews with industry leaders [5], provides a direct precedent for the methods and scale of work proposed under Aim 1. The 30–40 minute telephone interview format is practical and minimizes participant burden [1], and the thematic saturation stopping rule [1] provides a principled endpoint that does not depend on reaching exactly 20 interviews.

Access to senior industry leaders is not guaranteed. The purposive sampling design allows for substitution, and the thematic saturation criterion provides a principled stopping rule, but reaching 20 interviews may require approaching substantially more than 20 individuals if access proves difficult, particularly at organizations that may view the policy analysis as potentially adverse to their interests. The proposal does not specify whether the investigators have existing relationships with any of the 34 companies listed in the initial landscape table [1] or whether the advisory board will facilitate introductions. The team’s demonstrated ability to secure 19 industry interviews for the Curnutte et al. (2014) study [5] provides some reassurance, though the current proposal’s senior executive target (chief executives or senior scientific and business officers [1]) may present a higher access threshold than the prior study’s broader informant pool.

### Delphi panel recruitment and retention

The Delphi component poses the most significant feasibility challenges. Recruiting approximately 40 senior professionals across three broad constituencies for a commitment of five surveys and a two-day Washington meeting over approximately 20 months [1] represents a substantial ask. The recruitment strategy — identifying candidates through academic and trade literature, industry leader discussions, and advisory board recommendations, then ranking and approaching them sequentially [1] — is standard and well-described, but the proposal does not specify the minimum acceptable panel size, the fallback if certain stakeholder categories prove difficult to fill, or whether honoraria will be offered. The Messner et al. (2016) study, which offered $100 per completed round, achieved 48 participants from 93 invitations [3], demonstrating that the team can recruit panels at this scale.

Attrition across five surveys and a meeting over 20 months is a recognized risk. The Boulkedid et al. (2011) systematic review found a median Delphi panel size of 17 (Q1:11; Q3:31) across 80 studies [12], meaning the approximately 40-person target is at the upper end but within the established range. Comparable Delphi exercises have reported retention rates of 76–79%: the PROSPER e-Delphi study retained 76% of 207 participants across three rounds [16], and the Federico et al. (2025) European NGS Delphi retained 34 of 43 participants (79%) through all four rounds [10]. If this project experiences comparable attrition, a starting panel of 40 could yield approximately 30 completers, which remains workable for a policy Delphi where the goal is mapping perspectives rather than achieving statistical power [17]. The proposal would benefit from specifying target response rates per round and a plan for following up with non-responders.

### Schedule compression in year 2

The schedule’s feasibility rests on the team’s ability to manage a compressed year 2, which must accommodate panel recruitment and preparation, two Delphi surveys with intervening analysis, selection of three issues, initial policy analysis, and the first Capitol Hill briefing [1]. This loading is a direct consequence of the sequential logic the method requires — the landscape must precede the Delphi, and prioritization must precede policy analysis [1] — so schedule compression cannot be avoided by reordering tasks. The proposal’s monitoring provision stating that “the team will monitor developments and reassess the research strategy” [1] acknowledges the need for flexibility but does not constitute a detailed contingency plan with specific decision points, timeline buffers, or scope-reduction options. The advisory board’s five planned meetings [1] provide governance checkpoints that should help identify delays early, and the piloting of initial survey instruments in advance [1] reduces the risk of mid-process redesign.

### Dissemination capacity

The dissemination plan’s viability is reasonably supported by the engagement of Burness Communications for stakeholder identification, communication strategy, events, and archiving [1], which provides professional capacity that complements the academic team’s policy expertise.

## Ethics and safety

**The ethics and data safety plan is thorough, proportionate to the study’s minimal-risk activities, and appropriately addresses the specific confidentiality challenges created when research participants hold commercially or politically sensitive positions.**

### Risk profile and consent

The human participation in this project consists of two activities: semistructured telephone interviews with up to 20 industry leaders, and the five-survey Delphi process with an associated two-day meeting involving approximately 40 stakeholders [1]. Neither activity exposes participants to more than minimal risk, defined as the possibility of reputational or legal harm from inadvertent disclosure of professionally or commercially sensitive views. The proposal recognizes this risk explicitly, acknowledges that it is small but real, and describes a multi-layered set of protections that are appropriate to the nature and magnitude of the risk.

The consent process is well-structured for the professional populations involved. For interviews and the summit, verbal consent will be obtained after advance provision and discussion of study information, rather than signed written consent [1]. This is a pragmatic and ethically defensible choice when the primary risk to participants is confidentiality breach rather than physical harm: signed consent documents would themselves create a written record linking individuals to the study, thereby increasing rather than reducing the very risk the consent process is designed to address. The proposal states that the consent discussion will explain the small possibility that commercially or legally sensitive information could be disclosed, including information potentially subject to subpoena [1]. This disclosure is essential for informed consent in a study where industry executives may discuss business strategies and intellectual property positions, and where Delphi panelists may take positions on politically contentious regulatory questions. The investigators’ commitment to request a Certificate of Confidentiality and to inform participants about it and its purpose [1] provides an additional legal safeguard against compelled disclosure of identifiable research data, though the proposal appropriately acknowledges that such certificates have limits and does not promise absolute protection.

### Data handling and confidentiality

The data handling procedures are detailed and proportionate. For interviews, identifying information will be held securely and separately from numbered interview recordings and transcripts; two recording devices will be used as a redundancy measure; recordings will be transcribed and de-identified before analysis; and NVivo will be used for thematic analysis of the de-identified material [1]. For the Delphi surveys, electronic access will use individual links or passwords, responses will be encrypted and password-protected, and each panel member will receive a two-letter code for the surveys so that individual responses are linked to a code rather than to a name [1].

The initial group-building exercise in which participants share their names, affiliations, and expertise with each other [1] is a deliberate design choice that makes the panel’s composition transparent while preserving the anonymity of individual survey responses — a reasonable compromise between the policy Delphi’s need for participants to know the relevant expertise around the table and the equally important need for candid responses unconstrained by reputational concerns.

### The summit and face-to-face interaction

The two-day summit introduces face-to-face interaction after five rounds of anonymous surveys. The proposal addresses the confidentiality implications by specifying that the meeting will be closed to outside participants and the press, that transcripts will be de-identified before analysis, that comments will be reported in aggregate, and that the meeting recording will be destroyed after transcription [1]. The use of anonymous real-time voting during the meeting [1] further protects individual participants from having their positions attributed to them in the presence of other panelists.

### Recruitment equity and benefits

The recruitment approach explicitly will not exclude people on the basis of sex, race, or ethnicity [1]. The proposal acknowledges that the demographic composition of the professional populations from which participants will be drawn — senior industry executives, regulatory officials, healthcare administrators, and policy experts — is not sufficiently defined to specify demographic enrollment targets. This is an honest recognition of a constraint inherent in elite and expert sampling rather than a failure of ethical commitment; the purposive selection by professional role and expertise, combined with the non-exclusion principle, is the ethically appropriate approach for the study population.

There is no expected direct benefit to participants beyond ensuring that their perspectives inform policy development [1]. This is appropriate for a policy research study: the participants are contributing expertise to a process designed to produce public goods (the landscape analysis, priority list, and policy alternatives), and the dissemination plan’s commitment to sharing findings through multiple accessible formats [1] ensures that participants and their constituencies can use the resulting knowledge.

## Management

**The PI pairing, investigator team, and institutional collaborators bring genuine, domain-matched expertise to each aim, and the schedule’s sequential logic is appropriate to the method, but the compressed year-2 loading and several critical unspecified dependencies introduce manageable execution risk.**

### Team expertise and governance

The co-leadership arrangement between Kaufman and McGuire is well-constituted and creates a clean division of labor. Kaufman will oversee the Delphi process and dissemination; McGuire will primarily oversee the development of policy approaches; and both will share responsibility for the landscape [1]. The Genetics and Public Policy Center (GPPC) at Johns Hopkins has a demonstrated institutional track record in landscape analysis of genetic testing oversight, stakeholder engagement through interviews, surveys, focus groups, and town halls, and in convening diverse stakeholders who do not share a single institutional perspective [1].

The three institutional collaborators each bring expertise matched to a specific policy domain. Robert Cook-Deegan at Duke has published extensively on DNA sequencing patents, intellectual property, and gene patent eligibility, including work on the Human Genome Project’s data-sharing policies and the Mayo and Myriad Supreme Court decisions [18] [19]. Sean Tunis, as former CMS Chief Medical Officer and Director of the Center for Medical Technology Policy, brings direct payer and health technology assessment expertise to the reimbursement domain [2]. Patricia Deverka’s published work on pharmacogenomics evidence for payers and real-world evidence for NGS coverage decisions directly addresses the reimbursement and payment questions the project examines [20]\[證據91\]. Donna Messner’s experience in qualitative stakeholder research, technical panel organization, and Delphi implementation [1] provides the methodological anchor for the Delphi process itself, as demonstrated by her role in the Messner et al. (2016, 2017) studies [3] [4].

The eight-member advisory board adds governance capacity and domain coverage beyond the investigator team, encompassing clinical genetics, sequencing technology, regulation, economics, intellectual property, and consumer protection [1]. Its five planned video-conference meetings across three years [1] represent a modest burden and provide structured checkpoints at which the research strategy can be reviewed and adjusted — particularly valuable given the proposal’s acknowledgement that WGS technologies, business models, and regulations can change during the study [1]. The individual board members are not named in the available proposal, which is a minor gap: named members with demonstrated willingness to serve would strengthen confidence in recruitment feasibility. The engagement of Burness Communications for stakeholder identification, communication strategy, events, and archiving [1] provides professional dissemination capacity that complements the academic team’s policy expertise.

### Schedule realism and critical dependencies

The three-year schedule sequences phases in the logical order the method imposes: the landscape must precede panel preparation, question prioritization must precede issue selection, and legal analysis must precede policy alternative development [1]. This sequential dependency is not a weakness but a structural feature of the design.

The challenge is that year 2 is heavily loaded. It must accommodate panel recruitment and preparation, two Delphi surveys with intervening analysis, selection of three issues, initial policy analysis, and the first Capitol Hill briefing [1]. The Delphi process alone is estimated at approximately 20 months [1], and panel recruitment — involving identification, ranking, and sequential approach of candidates [1] — could consume several months before surveys begin. Year 3 must then accommodate three additional surveys with iterative analysis, a two-day Washington meeting with preparation and transcription, integration of feedback, process evaluation, and the second Capitol Hill briefing, leaving little slack for delays.

Three critical dependencies are acknowledged but undertreated. First, panel recruitment of approximately 40 senior professionals for a 20-month commitment is demanding; the proposal does not specify a minimum acceptable panel size, a fallback if certain stakeholder categories prove difficult to fill, or whether honoraria will be offered [1]. Second, panel retention across five surveys faces recognized attrition risk, and the proposal would benefit from specifying target response rates per round. Third, industry interview access to chief executives or senior scientific and business officers [1] is not guaranteed, and the proposal does not indicate whether the investigators have existing relationships with the organizations listed in the initial landscape table [1]. The monitoring and strategy-reassessment provision [1], the advisory board’s role in candidate identification [1], and the thematic saturation criterion for interviews [1] collectively provide some mitigation, but the proposal lacks concrete contingency planning with specific decision points, timeline buffers, or scope-reduction options if recruitment or retention fall short. The investigators’ demonstrated ability to recruit a 48-expert panel for the Messner et al. (2016) study and to secure 19 industry interviews for the Curnutte et al. (2014) study [5] [3] provides reassurance, though the current proposal’s duration and seniority requirements represent a somewhat higher bar.

# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project will evaluate image-guided cochlear implant programming (IGCIP) in 72 prelingually deafened children (aged 6–12) using a double-blind, randomized waitlist-control design. IGCIP uses CT-derived electrode position to deactivate specific channels, hypothesizing reduced channel interaction and improved spatial selectivity. The study’s most valuable expected knowledge extends beyond the primary efficacy question to rigorously test developmental cascades: whether auditory improvements, in specific spectral or temporal dimensions, predict individual gains in speech recognition, phonological awareness, language, and literacy over 24 months. The project capitalizes on the known dissociation—that prelingually deafened children often fail to utilize spectral resolution for speech perception as adults do—to investigate alternative or compensatory mechanisms, offering a significant advance in understanding the cognitive architecture of pediatric CI hearing.

The proposal is grounded in extensive preliminary work from an expert, multi-disciplinary team, and the double-blind, waitlist-control design is the strongest feasible test of the intervention. The comprehensive measurement battery is thoughtfully designed to capture multiple levels of auditory and developmental function. However, the project is critically constrained by the analytic plan for its core mechanistic aims. The path analyses are severely underpowered (N = 60) for detecting the hypothesized mediated pathways, and the sparse measurement of cognitive constructs (baseline, 12, and 24 months) cannot resolve the temporal ordering of developmental cascades within the 12‑month lags. Furthermore, the power analysis for the primary group comparison is optimistic, targeting an effect size larger than preliminary data and published literature suggest is plausible, while the “near‑zero waitlist slope” assumption is inconsistent with evidence of ongoing auditory maturation in this age group. These limitations, combined with the absence of published test-retest reliability for the psychoacoustic measures in this population, mean the study’s most scientifically ambitious and valuable claims are not well‑supported by the proposed design.

The proposal’s strength lies in its mechanistic aims rather than its primary efficacy comparison. The rigor of the developmental cascade analysis is materially limited by low power for mediation, sparse cognitive measurement, and the current inability of psychoacoustic tasks to predict speech recognition in this population. These limitations do not negate the study’s value—the design will still yield the most comprehensive dataset to date on this topic—but they reduce the certainty of the conclusions and relegate the path analyses to an exploratory, hypothesis‑generating role. Weighing the substantial potential for new knowledge against these methodological constraints, **the current plan has substantive problems affecting its main mechanistic goals and is insufficient for support as proposed**. The scientific value justifies revision and resubmission with a more realistic analytic scope and modest design refinements.

## Scientific value

**The proposal addresses a question of high clinical and scientific importance, and the most valuable attainable knowledge lies in its mechanistic investigation of developmental cascades rather than in a simple test of IGCIP efficacy.** The persistent gap in language and literacy outcomes for early‑implanted children with prelingual deafness, despite restored auditory access through cochlear implantation, is a well‑documented and clinically urgent problem. The proposal marshals evidence that mean language and vocabulary standard scores for pediatric implant users at entry to first grade remain below the age‑normative range, even for children implanted before 24 months [1]. This gap has resisted explanation by implantation age alone and motivates the search for modifiable factors in the quality of the auditory signal delivered by the implant. The project tackles this problem at its source—the electrode‑neuron interface—by using image guidance to optimize the programming of each child’s device and then tracing the consequences of any resulting auditory changes through speech recognition, phonological awareness, language, and reading over a two‑year developmental window.

The scientific value is structured across three aims [1], but the proposal’s intellectual center of gravity resides in Aims 2 and 3. These aims test whether individual differences in auditory change—in specific spectral, temporal, or spectrotemporal dimensions—predict growth in phonological awareness, speech production, receptive and expressive language, and ultimately reading comprehension. The proposal explicitly states that these analyses are “not contingent on a significant average intervention effect” [1], and also provides for alternative outcomes in which lack of an average auditory benefit does not prevent testing whether variable auditory, phonological‑awareness, or recognition changes predict language and production trajectories [1]. This framing is a genuine strength: even if IGCIP produces no detectable group‑level benefit—a plausible outcome given that only 16 % of children showed significant improvement in quiet and 28 % in noise in the preliminary data [1]—the resulting longitudinal dataset will be uniquely positioned to characterize the relationships among anatomically informed auditory function and higher‑order developmental outcomes.

This descriptive contribution would advance a literature in which neither spectral nor temporal resolution has been shown to correlate significantly with speech recognition in prelingually deafened children [2] [3], challenging adult‑centric models and redirecting inquiry toward the possibly distinct perceptual architecture of pediatric CI hearing. The planned path‑analytic framework, diagrammed in Figure 7 [1], compares auditory and speech‑recognition changes with later phonological‑awareness, receptive‑language, and reading‑comprehension outcomes while accounting for baseline abilities. The inclusion of a deferred‑treatment arm that provides 12 months of untreated growth followed by 12 months of treated growth [1] strengthens the within‑subject replication logic and partially addresses the confound between maturation and intervention. The rich covariate battery—including nonverbal cognition, working memory, maternal education, socioeconomic status, and daily device use [1]—further supports the investigation of individual differences. These features make the study, even in a worst‑case scenario for Aim 1, the most comprehensive longitudinal characterization yet attempted of how auditory processing relates to language and literacy development in children with cochlear implants.

**The eligibility criteria restrict the sample to a higher‑functioning subset, which limits generalizability but is defensible for the current study’s stage.** Restricting enrollment to children with nonverbal IQ ≥ 85, no autism, no neurological disorders, and no major temporal‑bone abnormalities [1] creates a sample representing roughly 30–40 % of the clinical pediatric CI population. The broader literature demonstrates that 30–40 % of children receiving CIs have a comorbid developmental or medical condition [4], and the mean nonverbal IQ of 95.4 (SD = 20.3) reported in a large sample of children with hearing loss implies that roughly 25–30 % score below 85 [5]. The trial will therefore test IGCIP effects under relatively favorable conditions, and the path analyses will be estimated in children whose developmental trajectories are less constrained by cognitive or neurological factors. This restriction is appropriate for an efficacy trial whose assessment battery demands sustained attention and compliance over seven study visits, but it means the results will not directly generalize to the substantial minority of CI users with comorbid conditions. The limitation is noted here as a matter of generalizability rather than feasibility, because the trial itself can be conducted with the specified population.

## Innovation

**The proposal’s primary innovation lies in its integrated approach: using an anatomically guided intervention as a controlled probe to test competing mechanistic hypotheses about how auditory improvements cascade to language and literacy in prelingually deafened children.** This contrasts with prior work that has evaluated IGCIP for its clinical efficacy alone, and with cross‑sectional studies that have documented the puzzling dissociation between spectral resolution and speech recognition in pediatric CI users without explaining its developmental consequences.

The technological basis is IGCIP itself: a CT‑based atlas derived from ten micro‑CT scans of human cadaveric cochleae is fitted to each child’s preoperative clinical scan and registered with a postoperative scan to locate electrodes relative to frequency‑mapped neural endings [1]. Distance‑versus‑frequency curves then identify electrodes whose stimulation regions overlap those of neighboring contacts [1], and the planned minimum‑error neural‑network procedure deactivates them while maximizing the number of active electrodes [1]. This approach also detects extracochlear electrodes, whose deactivation permits reallocation of the acoustic spectrum across intracochlear contacts [1]. The technique has been piloted extensively by the proposing team [1] [6] and has a defensible anatomical rationale, although the evidence that the specific distance‑versus‑frequency algorithm reduces channel interaction is indirect and primarily from the proposing group.

The more significant innovation, however, is conceptual. The field has been shaped by a robust finding from adult CI recipients—spectral modulation detection reliably predicts speech recognition—that does not hold in prelingually deafened children. The team’s own 2018 study demonstrated this dissociation definitively [3], and their 2024 study extended it, finding no significant correlation between any spectral or temporal resolution measure and any speech recognition measure after correction for multiple comparisons [2]. The likely explanation, consistent with independent work, is cue re‑weighting: children whose auditory systems developed with degraded electrical input may rely more heavily on temporal envelope information and less on spectral detail [7]. The proposal does not merely acknowledge this dissociation—it makes it the central scientific question. The spectral, temporal, and spectrotemporal resolution tasks are deployed not as established predictors but as competing candidates whose relationship to downstream outcomes must be empirically determined [1]. Testing whether *change* in these dimensions, rather than absolute level, predicts speech recognition and developmental gains has not been attempted longitudinally in children, and constitutes the proposal’s most original contribution.

The preliminary data directly motivate this framing. In the team’s cross‑sectional study of 47 children (mean age 8.33 years), moderate effect sizes (r ≈ −0.35 to −0.45) were observed between several resolution measures and vowel recognition, despite the absence of significant correlations for other speech measures [2]. A small pre‑post dataset of five children suggested an association between temporal resolution change and word‑recognition benefit [1]. These findings are consistent with the possibility that within‑subject longitudinal designs may reveal relationships that cross‑sectional studies miss, a hypothesis the current proposal is designed to test.

**The path‑analytic approach to developmental cascades further distinguishes this work from prior studies that have examined auditory and language outcomes in isolation.** The proposal tests direct and speech‑recognition‑mediated routes from auditory change to phonological awareness, receptive language, and reading, while accounting for the possibility that children can reach similar reading outcomes through different auditory and linguistic pathways [1]. The proposal’s explicit departure from linear spectral‑resolution‑to‑speech‑recognition models and its embrace of multiple, possibly compensatory developmental pathways represents a conceptual advance that could reshape how the field understands the cognitive architecture of pediatric CI hearing, regardless of whether IGCIP itself proves beneficial at the group level.

## Rigor

**The proposed double‑blind, randomized waitlist‑control design is appropriate for the primary efficacy comparison (Aim 1), but the analytic plan for the core mechanistic aims (Aims 2 and 3) is substantially constrained by low statistical power, sparse cognitive measurement, and unresolved psychometric properties of the auditory resolution tasks.** These limitations do not prevent the study from making a valuable descriptive contribution, but they materially weaken its capacity to test the hypothesized developmental cascades.

### Strengths of the experimental design

The randomized waitlist‑control design with blinded outcome assessment [1] is the strongest feasible approach for testing whether IGCIP produces greater auditory improvement than unaided maturation over 12 months. Both arms receive identical imaging, map‑generation, and assessment procedures, and the proposal describes intent‑to‑treat analyses with type‑I error controlled at no more than .05 [1]. The inclusion of baseline outcome values, speech recognition, and working memory as covariates in the mixed‑effects models, and the use of bootstrapping for confidence intervals [1], represents sound analytic practice. The deferred arm additionally serves as a within‑subject replication when those participants receive IGCIP at month 12 [1]. Several design features further strengthen internal validity: the baseline map optimization protocol ensures that the control condition is a genuinely well‑fit clinical map rather than a neglected default [1]; equipment and listening checks at every study visit reduce the risk that hardware malfunction contaminates the treatment contrast [1]; and the home‑based word‑recognition testing at 1 and 13 months, with predefined criteria for safety review, provides an ethically sound mechanism for monitoring individual performance [1]. The proposal appropriately specifies that nonresponders will remain informative, including continued follow‑up where possible [1].

### Optimism of the power analysis for Aim 1

The power analysis for the primary group comparison targets a between‑group effect of d ≈ 0.67–0.74 (N = 30 per arm, 80 % power, two‑sided α = .05; [1]). This is optimistic when examined against the proposal’s own preliminary data. In the preliminary series of 41 children tested pre‑post IGCIP, only 16 % showed significant improvement in quiet speech recognition and 28 % improved in sentence‑in‑noise testing [1]. The between‑group comparison in the proposed trial will contrast the IGCIP arm’s change against natural maturation in the waitlist arm, an effect that is almost certainly smaller than the pre‑post change observed in the un‑controlled preliminary data. Moreover, the “near‑zero waitlist slope” hypothesis [1] is inconsistent with published longitudinal data showing that speech‑in‑noise reception thresholds in children with CIs improve significantly from age 5 to 9 years (mean improvement 4.5 dB, p \< .0001) [8]. If the waitlist arm shows meaningful improvement over 12 months, the between‑group difference attributable to IGCIP will be attenuated. The group × time interaction test is the appropriate analytic approach and the proposal correctly specifies it, but the power to detect this interaction depends on the magnitude of the difference in slopes, which is likely smaller than the difference between the IGCIP slope and zero.

### Power for mediation and path‑analytic aims

The proposal’s scientific center of gravity resides in its developmental cascade hypotheses, and the design’s capacity to test these is structurally limited. The statement that N = 60 “permits detection of a path correlation near .35” [1] refers to bivariate path coefficients, not to the indirect (mediated) effects that are the targets of Aims 2 and 3. Detecting a three‑path mediation (auditory change → speech recognition → phonological awareness → reading) with the product‑of‑coefficients method requires substantially larger samples when individual paths are moderate. The preliminary data provide the best guide to the expected correlation structure: Table 1 [1] shows phonological awareness as a significant predictor of basic reading (β = 0.509), which is encouraging, but the correlations between sentence‑recognition changes and language‑articulation changes in the 17‑child preliminary sample are moderate (r ≈ .30–.57) and variable across conditions [1]. The bootstrap confidence intervals for indirect effects at N = 60 will be wide, limiting the “mechanistic understanding” the proposal envisions [1] to ruling out very large effects or confirming the sign of associations already suggested by cross‑sectional work.

### Temporal resolution of the cascade analysis

A further structural constraint is that the cognitive constructs central to Aims 2 and 3—phonological awareness, language, nonverbal cognition, and reading—are measured only at baseline, 12 months, and 24 months [1]. In contrast, auditory resolution and speech recognition are assessed at seven timepoints (baseline, 2, 6, 12, 14, 18, and 24 months), creating inter‑assessment intervals of 2–6 months [1]. The 12‑month gaps between cognitive assessments mean that the core hypothesized sequence—improved auditory input → better speech recognition → growth in PA → gains in reading—cannot be temporally ordered within the developmental window. If all three constructs change over 12 months, a conventional cross‑lagged model cannot determine which changed first. The acoustic speech production measures are collected at every visit [1], which partially compensates by providing a continuous proximal outcome, but the cascade to standardized reading and language scores cannot be temporally resolved.

### Distinguishing directional influence from stable individual differences

The proposal describes cross‑lagged models that “compare changes in one domain with later changes in another and generate tentative causal hypotheses” [1]. The traditional cross‑lagged panel model (CLPM), however, conflates stable between‑person trait variance with within‑person change, which can produce spurious cross‑lagged estimates when constructs are correlated at the trait level. The proposal’s description does not specify a random‑intercept cross‑lagged panel model (RI‑CLPM) or any mechanism for separating these sources of variance. With only three waves for the cognitive constructs, an RI‑CLPM would be at the absolute minimum for statistical identification, and power at N = 60 would be extremely low. The proposal’s own framing—“tentative causal hypotheses for further research” [1]—reflects appropriate caution, but the design cannot deliver even this tentative goal for the mediation paths with the precision the path diagrams [1] imply.

### Psychometric properties of the auditory resolution tasks

The psychoacoustic resolution measures central to the mechanistic aims have not been validated for the proposed repeated‑measures application. The proposal’s own published work demonstrates that neither spectral nor temporal resolution correlates significantly with speech recognition in prelingually deafened children after correction for multiple comparisons [2], a finding that contrasts sharply with the robust relationship observed in 542 adult CI recipients [3]. The proposal handles this appropriately by making the spectral‑versus‑temporal comparison an empirical question [1] and by investigating whether *change* in resolution, rather than absolute level, predicts outcomes. However, the absence of published test‑retest reliability data for spectral modulation detection, temporal modulation detection, and spectrotemporal tasks in pediatric CI users over 7–9 repeated assessments introduces unquantified measurement error into the longitudinal analyses. The proposal’s approach of averaging two runs per condition, with a third run if the first two differ by more than 5 dB [2], provides within‑session reliability but does not address between‑session stability, practice effects, or developmental change in task performance over the two‑year study period. The concern is heightened by evidence that children with normal hearing show improving spectral resolution through adolescence [9], and that children under 8 years of age in the team’s own studies could not complete the full psychoacoustic battery [2], raising the possibility of floor effects that reduce sensitivity to change in the younger portion of the proposed sample.

### Speech recognition measurement and the IGCIP evidence base

The speech recognition battery—CNC monosyllables, nonword repetition, BabyBio sentences at +5 dB SNR, BKB‑SIN (adaptive SNR‑50), and HINT with spatially separated noise [1]—is comprehensive and follows the Pediatric Minimum Speech Test Battery. Multiple equivalent lists exist for each measure [1], and the BabyBio lists were validated for list equivalency with demonstrated 95 % confidence intervals [10]. The acoustic speech production measures—harmonic‑to‑noise ratio, fundamental‑frequency distribution, articulation precision‑entropy, envelope modulation, formants and vowel space, and others [1]—are well‑chosen to detect subclinical changes that standardized articulation scores may miss, as the preliminary case data demonstrated [1].

A separate consideration is that the evidence linking IGCIP specifically to reduced channel interaction is indirect. The relationship between electrode‑to‑modiolus distance and broader neural excitation is supported by independent work: DeVries and Arenberg (2018) showed that broader psychophysical tuning curve bandwidths were significantly correlated with electrodes farther from the modiolus in 7 of 13 subjects [11], and Zhou (2022) found that electrode position explained 24 % of the variance in spatial tuning curve slopes [12]. One independent group (Danieli et al., 2020) demonstrated benefit from CT‑based deactivation [13]. However, the most directly comparable independent study—Berg et al. (2024), using the same IGES algorithm as IGCIP in 12 adult MED‑EL recipients—found no significant group‑level benefit: “IGES did not significantly improve group performance, and electrode placement factors did not impact results” [14]. The current IGES algorithm does not consider the impact of spectral blurring on particular electrodes or the underlying neural health across the array [14]. This finding from within the proposing team’s own collaborative network reinforces the appropriateness of the proposal’s acknowledgment that the language and literacy analyses are not contingent on a significant average intervention effect [1].

### Summary of rigor

Weighing these considerations together, the design supports the primary efficacy question (Aim 1) and the descriptive characterization of individual trajectories with good rigor. The cross‑lagged and path‑analytic components of Aims 2 and 3, which are the proposal’s most scientifically ambitious and valuable elements, are substantially underpowered and structurally constrained for their stated inferential goals. The resulting knowledge will be descriptive and hypothesis‑generating—characterizing the magnitude and sign of lagged associations, and providing effect‑size estimates that can inform future studies—rather than confirmatory tests of the developmental cascade model. This limitation does not negate the study’s value, but it reduces the certainty and specificity of the conclusions that can be drawn from its most important aims.

## Feasibility

**The recruitment plan is realistic for a large‑volume center but operates within narrow eligibility constraints that limit the pool to an estimated 30–40 % of the clinical pediatric CI population, requiring effective screening of approximately 180–240 children to yield the target sample.** The study will enroll 72 children with 36 randomized to each arm and an intended final analysis sample of 60 [1]. Enrollment is staggered across four years of active recruitment, with summer cohorts of 12–14 children and winter cohorts of 5–10 [1]. The proposal draws on Vanderbilt’s large pediatric implant program and children becoming age‑eligible during the project [1], and the schedule builds in a realistic ramp‑up with recruitment concentrated around school breaks.

**The eligibility criteria are restrictive but defensible for an efficacy trial.** Children must have prelingual deafness, implantation before age 3, bilateral moderate‑to‑profound loss, typical nonverbal cognition (standard score ≥ 85), no autism or neurological disorder, no major temporal‑bone abnormalities, and available pre‑ and post‑operative CT scans [1]. As noted under Scientific Value, this selects a higher‑functioning subset representing perhaps 30–40 % of the clinical population, but the restriction strengthens internal validity for the mechanistic questions and reduces the risk of missing data from participants who cannot complete the demanding psychoacoustic battery. Pre‑operative CT is standard of care and virtually universally available [15], and post‑operative imaging can be obtained through the linked approved protocol [1], so imaging availability is unlikely to be a bottleneck.

**The five‑year schedule is appropriately detailed and realistically phased.** Year 1 includes a deliberate ramp‑up: staff hiring and training, assessment acquisition, trial registration, and randomization generation in the first months, with the first summer recruitment cohort entering at months 4–6 [1]. Subsequent years overlap recruitment of new cohorts with follow‑up visits for existing participants, and the schedule specifies which visit milestones (6‑month, 12‑month, 24‑month) are due for each cohort in each year. The phasing of analysis is equally deliberate: blinded auditory analyses begin in year 2, auditory‑processing manuscripts are prepared in year 3, and the integrated auditory‑developmental analyses are concentrated in years 4–5 once the final 24‑month cognitive assessments are complete. The plan to request a no‑cost extension for dissemination if needed [1] acknowledges the realistic time required for final analyses and manuscript preparation after unblinding. The main operational risk is the tight winter recruitment window, which may require extending into spring or accepting slightly smaller winter cohorts; the flexible cohort framework can accommodate these adjustments.

## Ethics and safety

**The proposal demonstrates a thorough and proportionate approach to ethics and safety, with well‑defined procedures for informed consent, risk identification and mitigation, safety monitoring, and data management.** The risks inherent in the intervention—potential reduced speech recognition and exposure to postoperative CT imaging—are explicitly acknowledged and addressed through protocolized safeguards.

The study will operate under institutional review with parental consent and child assent [1]. The involvement of children aged 6–12 years with prelingual deafness raises standard considerations of capacity and voluntariness; the proposal’s reference to assent under institutional procedures is appropriate and consistent with established practice in pediatric CI research.

**The primary intervention risk is that IGCIP deactivation of specific electrodes could degrade rather than improve speech recognition.** The proposal addresses this through multiple protective layers. Before baseline, conventional clinical maps are optimized to provide CI‑aided thresholds of 20–25 dB HL from 250–6,000 Hz, with electrically evoked stapedial reflex thresholds verifying upper stimulation levels where feasible [1]. This optimization ensures that the baseline map is a genuinely well‑fit clinical standard, reducing the chance that any decline reflects pre‑existing suboptimal programming. Equipment and listening checks at every study visit [1] further isolate intervention effects from hardware malfunction.

The safety‑monitoring protocol is a particular strength. At baseline and 12 months, staff establish smartphone‑based word‑recognition scores; caregivers repeat testing at home one month later. A decline exceeding the 95 % test‑retest interval triggers the option to restore the prior clinical map or continue for one additional month until the next scheduled visit [1]. This procedure aligns with the preliminary finding that 5 % of children showed worsening in quiet and 3 % in noise with IGCIP [1], and that restoration of the prior clinical map is an available safety response [1]. The proposal’s explicit statement that withdrawal because of poor performance may require unmasking for that participant while two‑year outcome observation may continue [1] balances safety monitoring with preservation of the blinded design for other participants.

**The second material risk is radiation exposure from postoperative CT imaging.** The proposal specifies that existing clinically obtained scans will be used when available, and that outside‑implanted children may receive low‑dose imaging through a linked approved protocol with institutional radiation oversight [1]. This dual‑source approach minimizes additional radiation exposure, and the existing protocol’s institutional approval provides external validation that the radiation burden is acceptable for the research context.

**Data management and confidentiality procedures are specified and appropriate.** REDCap and a centralized repository will store coded data with the PIs retaining a separate identity master list; paper records are secured with access limited to research personnel [1]. The plan to retain records for at least six years after study completion, to deposit de‑identified data and methodological detail in shared repositories, and to provide families with individual assessment reports [1] addresses both confidentiality and the return of results. Adverse event documentation and reporting are specified, and independent monitoring will be organized if the IRB requires it [1]. Two additional design features warrant note: the deferred‑treatment arm ensures that all participants ultimately receive the intervention, which is ethically preferable to a placebo‑only control; and the restrictions on non‑verbal IQ and comorbidities, while limiting generalizability, are ethically appropriate for an efficacy trial whose assessment battery demands sustained attention and compliance.

## Management

**The management plan is detailed, credible, and well‑matched to the complexity of a multi‑year randomized trial with dense longitudinal assessment. The dual‑PI structure, comprehensive expertise, explicit coordination mechanisms, and realistic schedule collectively provide high confidence that the study can be delivered as proposed.**

The responsibilities are clearly divided between the two principal investigators: Gifford oversees auditory perception and recruitment, while Camarata oversees speech, language, and literacy outcomes [1]. This division maps directly onto the proposal’s three specific aims and ensures that both the intervention‑delivery pipeline (imaging, IGCIP plan generation, audiological assessment) and the developmental endpoint battery (language, phonological awareness, reading) have dedicated senior leadership. The team further includes otology, image analysis and engineering, biostatistics, and consultants in phonological processing and speech production [1], covering every domain required by the study’s interdisciplinary design. The presence of a project manager and trained research staff to coordinate visits and assessments [1] is essential for a trial with seven formal assessment visits per participant spanning two years.

**The coordination plan is specified and appropriate to the operational demands.** Monthly team meetings are scheduled throughout the study [1], providing a regular forum for monitoring recruitment progress, reviewing adverse events or performance declines, and maintaining protocol fidelity across staggered cohorts. The involvement of the statistician in allocation generation, with access restricted to the PIs and statistician who will not administer or score assessments [1], is correctly designed to preserve blinding. The specification that the statistician will generate the permuted‑block allocation schedule [1] places this critical function under appropriate professional control.

The five‑year schedule, detailed in a project‑level table [1], logically sequences all phases from staff training and trial registration in year 1 through integrated analyses and dissemination in years 4–5. The staggered‑cohort design provides natural flexibility, yearly recruitment targets are ranges rather than fixed numbers, and the concentration of recruitment around school breaks is pragmatic for family availability. Staff training at the start of each year further supports protocol fidelity as new personnel join or procedures are refined.

No items in the described management arrangements appear under‑resourced or critically under‑specified at the level of documentation provided in this proposal. The combination of a well‑defined leadership structure, comprehensive disciplinary coverage, explicit coordination procedures, and a realistically phased five‑year schedule supports the delivery of the stated aims.

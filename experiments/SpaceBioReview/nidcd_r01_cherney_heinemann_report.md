# Project review

## Overall assessment

This prospective observational study addresses a well-defined gap in aphasia research: the simultaneous characterization of linguistic, cognitive-communicative, and quality-of-life recovery trajectories in a single cohort followed from inpatient rehabilitation through 18 months after stroke. By enrolling 400 participants across three U.S. inpatient rehabilitation facilities (retaining 300 at 18 months) and applying individual growth-curve models to all three outcome domains, the study aims to produce the first large, prospective, multi-domain characterization of aphasia recovery patterns. The principal expected knowledge contribution is descriptive and foundational: empirically derived estimates of typical recovery rates, plateaus, and between-person variability that can serve as benchmarks for future research and for the interactive clinical trajectory tool proposed in Aim 3. The integration of therapy exposure measurement with modality-level tracking, candidate genetic polymorphisms related to neuroplasticity, and resting-state neuroimaging in a subset of 100 participants adds the potential for hypothesis generation about factors associated with individual differences in recovery, though the study's observational design limits causal inference.

The proposal's strongest supporting evidence is its methodological infrastructure and team expertise. The investigative team has demonstrated competence in aphasia treatment research, growth-curve modeling in rehabilitation populations, and the proposed therapy tracking system builds directly on the proven SCIRehab multi-site point-of-care documentation methodology with added video validation. The three participating facilities have established clinical volumes to support recruitment, and the five-year schedule with staggered follow-up assessments is realistic. The assessment battery is comprehensive and includes validated instruments for all three outcome domains. However, several material limitations constrain what the study can credibly establish. The observational therapy data, adjusted only by covariate inclusion, cannot distinguish treatment effects from confounding by indication—more severely affected patients systematically receive more therapy, creating negative confounding that covariate adjustment cannot fully resolve. The genetic interaction analysis using multifactor dimensionality reduction with 10 candidate SNPs in 300–400 participants lacks sufficient power unless epistatic effects are unrealistically large, as established by MDR power simulation literature. The 100-patient imaging subsample is substantially underpowered for stable brain-behavior associations in covariate-rich models, contrary to evidence that reproducible brain-wide associations require samples in the thousands. The internal validation strategy using bootstrap stability and cross-validation cannot establish transportability to other clinical settings, limiting the immediate utility of the planned interactive trajectory tool.

The central tradeoff is between Aim 1's foundational descriptive value—which 300 participants and five timepoints can credibly deliver—and the substantially weaker inferential basis for Aim 2's multivariate modeling. Aim 2 can contribute hypothesis generation and association characterization, but its ambitions regarding predictor identification and individualized prediction require tempering to match what the design can actually deliver. The therapy tracking represents a genuine methodological advance over billing-record approaches, though it will yield modality-level rather than task-level resolution. On this basis, the proposal warrants conditional support: the core Aim 1 contribution is supportable and scientifically valuable on its own terms, provided that Aim 2's multivariate and predictive claims are appropriately reframed and the interactive trajectory tool is positioned as a research product requiring external validation before clinical deployment.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

**This is a well-motivated observational study whose primary scientific value lies in producing the first large, prospective, multi-domain characterization of aphasia recovery trajectories—a foundational descriptive contribution that the field currently lacks.** The question addresses a directly stated gap: most previous aphasia studies have emphasized linguistic outcomes alone, used small or retrospective samples, or measured only a few endpoints, leaving inconsistent knowledge about how linguistic recovery, cognitive-communicative function, and quality of life evolve together after stroke [1]. By prospectively following 300 retained participants from inpatient rehabilitation through 18 months and modeling all three outcome domains simultaneously with individual growth-curve models, the study would deliver empirically derived estimates of typical recovery rates, plateaus, and between-person variability [1]. The proposal's own acknowledgment that “suitable long-term aphasia parameters are unavailable for reliable simulation-based sample-size estimation” [1] underscores how little is currently known about these trajectory parameters—making the descriptive contribution itself scientifically valuable.

### Multi-domain integration and outcome measurement

The multi-domain integration within an ICF framework represents a meaningful conceptual contribution beyond what any single-domain aphasia cohort study has provided. The primary linguistic outcome (WAB-R Aphasia Quotient), the cognitive-communicative outcome (Communicative Participation Item Bank), and the quality-of-life outcome (Stroke and Aphasia Quality of Life Scale-39) address impairment, participation, and lived impact respectively, rather than assuming they recover together [1]. This design directly enables empirical testing of whether participation and QoL trajectories diverge from linguistic trajectories over time—a question the field recognizes as important but has not formally tested with comparable longitudinal data [1]. The patient-reported instruments extend the analysis beyond impairment, an important step given that “improvement on an impairment test does not necessarily describe a person's communication confidence or ability to participate” [1].

**The specific Aim 1 hypotheses, while reasonable, are more confirmatory than novel.** The prediction that linguistic improvement slows around six months while communicative participation and QoL continue to improve [1] aligns with broadly accepted expectations in the field. The classical acute-subacute-chronic framework already embeds the understanding that aphasia severity “often stabilizes around 6 months post-stroke” [2], while substantial individual variability thereafter is well-documented—one study of 39 chronic patients found that 51% improved, 26% worsened, and 23% remained stable over time [2]. Similarly, the conceptual dissociation between impairment-level and participation-level recovery is a core tenet of the ICF framework. The value therefore resides in the quality and precision of the empirical characterization rather than in testing a genuinely uncertain prediction. Precise descriptive benchmarks represent essential infrastructure for the field, but the contribution is appropriately situated as foundational rather than discovery-oriented.

### Therapy measurement as a methodological contribution of independent value

The therapy exposure measurement system adds a contribution of independent scientific value. Current aphasia research predominantly relies on billing records that “provide time and service codes but do not adequately describe what language modality or task was treated” [1]. By building on the proven SCIRehab point-of-care documentation methodology—which achieved 85% exact agreement on intervention activities and 95% on session-level variables [3]—and adding direct video validation for 10% of sessions [1], the study would produce the richest characterization of real-world aphasia therapy content available from a multi-site observational design. This measurement advance has value beyond the current study's specific aims, as it provides a template for future practice-based evidence research in aphasia rehabilitation. This contribution is discussed more fully under Innovation.

### Practical contribution and its limits

The practical contribution—an interactive trajectory tool enabling clinicians to explore expected recovery patterns based on entered patient characteristics [1]—addresses a recognized need for evidence-based prognostic information. Comparable tools developed by this team for spinal cord injury populations have demonstrated value for clinical decision support [4]. However, the tool's immediate utility is constrained by the study's internal validation design. Internal validation via bootstrapping “can provide approximately unbiased estimates of predictive performance for future individuals drawn from the same underlying population,” but when performance is intended to generalize to different settings, “performance estimates obtained through internal validation may still be optimistic” [5]. The tool will require external validation before clinical deployment.

**In sum,** the attainable knowledge from Aim 1's unconditional trajectory estimation alone constitutes a scientifically valuable contribution, while the value of Aim 2 lies primarily in hypothesis generation and association characterization rather than in establishing causal determinants of recovery. The study's descriptive benchmarks for aphasia recovery across three ICF domains, its rich therapy exposure characterization, and its template for multi-site observational methodology in aphasia research together represent a worthwhile scientific investment.

## Innovation

**The proposal makes incremental but genuine methodological contributions, though its specific hypotheses are more confirmatory than novel.** The originality resides primarily in how established methods are combined and applied to a new clinical context, rather than in breaking new conceptual or technical ground.

### Multi-domain IGC application in aphasia

The most meaningful innovation is the simultaneous application of individual growth-curve models to three ICF-relevant outcome domains within a single, prospectively designed aphasia cohort followed over 18 months. IGC modeling itself has been validated by this investigative team in spinal cord injury, where Kozlowski and Heinemann (2013) demonstrated that trajectories of FIM motor subscores were clinically meaningful and could facilitate recovery prediction [6], and Pretz et al. (2014) developed an interactive trajectory tool for paraplegia that this proposal aims to adapt [4]. The IGC approach offers several advantages over the group-level comparisons and repeated-measures ANOVA that dominate prior aphasia longitudinal work: it estimates continuous trajectory parameters rather than discrete time-point differences, accommodates irregular assessment schedules and missing data, and allows formal comparison of functional forms including linear, quadratic, cubic, and negative-exponential trajectories [1]. Latent class growth analysis—sharing the core logic of modeling individual trajectory heterogeneity—has recently been applied to post-stroke cognitive recovery by Milosevich et al. (2026), who identified four distinct trajectory classes [7], and to SCI functional recovery by Tulsky et al. (2021), who found that most improvement occurred in the first six months with a small subgroup showing substantial late gains [8]. What distinguishes this proposal is the application specifically to aphasia, across three outcome domains simultaneously, in a prospectively designed study with consistent assessment timing. No published study has done this.

### Therapy tracking advance

The inpatient therapy tracking system represents a genuine methodological advance over the billing-record methods that dominate aphasia research. The tablet-based point-of-care logging with modality-level coding—auditory comprehension, oral expression, reading comprehension, and written expression—plus start and stop times [1] builds directly on the SCIRehab multi-site documentation methodology. That project demonstrated that clinicians using handheld devices achieved 85% exact agreement on intervention activities and 95% on session-level variables [3], with documentation time falling below one minute per session once clinicians were familiar with the system [3]. The proposal adopts these core methods with meaningful improvements: shifting from PDAs to modern tablets reduces the technical problems SCIRehab encountered [3], and adding 10% video validation of logged sessions [1] provides a direct behavioral check that SCIRehab could not implement—its reliability testing relied on written case scenarios, which the investigators themselves acknowledged “does not guarantee” accuracy with real sessions [3]. No prior large-scale observational aphasia study has attempted real-time, modality-level logging across multiple clinical sites, and the resulting data will provide the richest characterization of real-world aphasia therapy content available from an observational design.

### Confirmatory hypotheses and established methods

The specific hypotheses, while reasonable and important to verify empirically, are more confirmatory than novel. The central Aim 1 prediction—that linguistic improvement slows around six months while communicative participation and QoL continue to improve [1]—aligns with the broadly accepted understanding that aphasia severity stabilizes around six months post-stroke [2]. The proposal's own published protocol frames this hypothesis without claiming it as a novel prediction [9]. The conceptual dissociation between impairment-level and participation-level recovery is a core expectation of the ICF framework.

Similarly, the genetic interaction analysis using multifactor dimensionality reduction [1] and the resting-state neuroimaging [1] follow established analytical approaches. MDR has been a standard tool for detecting gene-gene interactions for over a decade [10], and the imaging processing pipeline references widely used packages—FSL, SPM, FreeSurfer—alongside Human Connectome Project protocols [1]. The innovation in these components lies in their integration with longitudinal behavioral outcomes in an aphasia population, not in the methods themselves.

**In sum,** the proposal's originality is best characterized as a well-executed integration of established methods applied to a clinically important question in a new combination. The therapy tracking measurement advance and the multi-domain IGC application constitute genuine, if incremental, methodological contributions that distinguish this work from prior aphasia recovery research.

## Rigor

**The study design is methodologically appropriate for Aim 1's unconditional trajectory estimation, but Aim 2's multivariate ambitions are substantially constrained by fundamental inferential limitations in each of its three principal data sources.** These constraints—confounding by indication in the observational therapy data, insufficient power for the genetic interaction analysis, and an underpowered imaging subsample—affect what this proposal can credibly establish beyond description and association. The internal validation strategy provides useful quality control but cannot resolve these structural limitations.

### Observational therapy data and confounding by indication

The proposal is appropriately candid that “treatment was not randomized” and that therapy-outcome associations are “adjusted for patient characteristics” [1]. However, the consequences of this design choice require careful examination. In inpatient rehabilitation, the direction of confounding is systematic and predictable: clinicians allocate more therapy to patients with more severe impairments, meaning that crude associations between therapy intensity and recovery will be biased negatively. The proposal's own hypothesis—that “earlier, more intensive, and longer treatment is expected to accompany better outcomes” [1]—confronts this directly.

Covariate adjustment for initial severity can partially address this confounding, but only to the extent that severity is measured without error and the functional form of the severity-outcome relationship is correctly specified. The WAB-R Aphasia Quotient, while well-validated, is a single summary score; residual confounding from unmeasured dimensions of severity—specific comprehension deficits, concomitant cognitive impairments not fully captured by the NIHSS—remains plausible. The analytic approach enters treatment variables as covariates in IGC models alongside clinical predictors, which estimates partial associations conditional on measured confounders. This is descriptive and associative; it cannot support causal claims about what would happen if therapy were modified.

The proposal does not employ methods designed to strengthen causal inference from observational treatment data—no instrumental variable analysis, marginal structural models, or propensity-based designs beyond standard covariate adjustment. Bootstrapping in Aim 3 will assess whether the same covariates are selected across resamples, but model stability does not resolve confounding: variables that are stably confounded will be stably selected. The external literature confirms that such confounding is pervasive in rehabilitation research. Whiteneck et al. (2011) found that “variation in treatment intensity was not well explained by patient and injury characteristics” in SCI rehabilitation [11], precisely because allocation reflected unmeasured clinical judgement. Haldin et al. (2024), in a retrospective analysis of 53 aphasia patients, found SLT intensity associated with recovery but acknowledged the need for “validation in a larger cohort” [12].

That said, the therapy measurement itself—clinician-logged task-level timing with modality coding, validated by video review—represents a genuine methodological advance. The descriptive value of characterizing actual therapy content across three sites and over 18 months should not be discounted. The limitation is inferential, not measurement-related.

### Genetic interaction power

The genetic component proposes to genotype 10 SNPs across six genes and use multifactor dimensionality reduction to detect epistatic interactions [1]. The scientific rationale—that neuroplasticity-related variants may interact rather than act independently—is plausible. BDNF rs6265 (Val66Met) is the most studied polymorphism in stroke recovery, with evidence for context-dependent effects influenced by lesion location, post-stroke stage, and age [13].

However, the design faces three power-related challenges. First, MDR's power depends critically on both sample size and the underlying genetic architecture. Edwards et al. (2008) showed that MDR, in simulations with 500 cases and 500 controls, had zero power to detect two-locus interactions when odds ratios were 1.3 or below, and required odds ratios above 1.75 with broad-sense heritability exceeding 0.1 for adequate power [14]. For three-locus models, power was lower still [14]. The proposal's 300–400 participants with a continuous outcome constitute a smaller effective sample than these case-control benchmarks. The only power justification provided is that “suitable long-term aphasia parameters are unavailable for reliable simulation-based sample-size estimation” and that “a final sample of 300 is feasible” [1]—this addresses Aim 1's trajectory estimation, not Aim 2's interaction detection.

Second, MDR cannot distinguish true interactions from independent main effects without appropriate post-hoc testing. Edwards et al. (2010) demonstrated that MDR rejects the null hypothesis at inflated rates when only main effects are present; at relative risk 6, MDR rejected in 50% of simulations with 2,000 families even though no interaction existed [15]. The authors proposed a regression-based permutation test to address this, which the current proposal does not mention. The plan to enter the MDR-derived high-risk indicator “with adjusted covariates into regression models” [1] conflates the interaction test with the main-effect adjustment, and it is unclear whether the proposed procedure controls the false positive rate for interaction claims.

Third, with 10 SNPs, the number of two-locus combinations is 45 and three-locus combinations number 120. Even with cross-validation and permutation testing as MDR conventionally employs [10], the combinatorial search space combined with modest sample size creates conditions where chance findings are likely. BDNF rs6265 has a minor allele frequency of approximately 25–32% in Caucasian populations [13], meaning roughly 6–10% of participants would be homozygous for the Met allele, and genotype combination cells will be sparse for interaction analyses. The genetic component can contribute descriptive characterization of allele frequencies and exploratory associations, but claims about epistatic interactions require more cautious framing than the proposal provides.

### Imaging subsample: 100 participants in a covariate-rich model

The imaging component plans to acquire resting-state fMRI, structural MRI, and diffusion imaging in a 100-patient subsample [1]. The imaging measures are comprehensive and well-specified, including functional connectivity using multiple parcellations, graph-theoretic metrics, lesion volumetry, and diffusion tractography [1]. The processing pipeline references established tools [1].

However, the sample size is fundamentally inadequate for the intended purpose of entering imaging measures into covariate-rich IGC models. Marek et al. (2022), analyzing approximately 50,000 individuals across three large datasets, demonstrated that brain-wide associations with cognitive and behavioral phenotypes have much smaller effect sizes than previously assumed, and that samples in the thousands are needed for reproducible findings [16]. At sample sizes typical of neuroimaging studies, “effect sizes are systematically inflated, replication rates are poor, and statistically significant results are paradoxically the most likely to be false” [16]. Even at n = 1,000, false negative rates were 75–100% and “half of the statistically significant associations were inflated by at least 100%” [16].

The proposal's imaging analysis adds further complexity: the plan is to enter imaging measures as predictors into IGC models that already contain demographic, clinical, therapy, and genetic covariates, using purposeful model selection across predictor groups [1]. With 100 imaging participants, the number of imaging predictors rapidly approaches or exceeds the effective sample size when considered alongside other covariates. The proposal states that imaging measures' “added information \[will be\] judged alongside conventional clinical variables” [1], but the precision of that judgment will be low.

The preliminary imaging work, while methodologically competent, illustrates the problem: n = 8 participants showed associations between baseline connectivity and treatment gains [1]. These findings appropriately motivated the larger study, but they come from a sample size where chance associations are highly probable. The external replication literature confirms this concern: Jajcay et al. (2026), attempting to replicate published resting-state fMRI personality associations with n = 84, failed to replicate any of nine previously reported effects despite having adequate power for the originally claimed effect sizes [17].

The imaging data can contribute descriptively—characterizing the range of connectivity patterns in this population and generating hypotheses about which networks may relate to which recovery domains. But the proposal's ambition to integrate imaging measures into predictive models alongside clinical and genetic variables, assessed through bootstrap stability and cross-validation, substantially overstates what 100 participants can support. Bootstrap stability at n = 100 may select different variables in different resamples not because effects are absent but because sampling variability is large relative to true effects.

### Internal validation limits and attrition concerns

The Aim 3 strategy—500 bootstrap resamples assessing coefficient stability and predictor-selection frequency, plus 500 Monte Carlo cross-validation splits evaluating prediction error against minimally clinically important differences [1]—is methodologically appropriate for its purpose. However, internal validation has a fundamental limit: it “can provide approximately unbiased estimates of predictive performance for future individuals drawn from the same underlying population,” but “when model performance is intended to generalize to different settings, populations, or time periods, performance estimates obtained through internal validation may still be optimistic” [5]. The bootstrap process “only examines instability in the population that the development dataset was sampled from” [18].

This matters because the interactive trajectory tool is explicitly framed as a clinical instrument to “generate an expected trajectory from entered patient, stroke, and therapy characteristics” [1]. A tool intended for clinical use at other sites requires evidence of transportability that internal validation cannot provide. The three Midwestern U.S. facilities provide some geographic diversity but do not represent the range of practice settings where such a tool would be deployed. The cross-country prediction study by Shao et al. (2026) demonstrates concretely that models performing well under internal validation show meaningful degradation when transported: “performance loss from using a model trained on external data ranged from negligible to greater than 0.1 AUROC” [19], and “the contribution of individual patient characteristics to resistance prediction may be influenced by local factors” [19].

The proposal's contingency plans—longer accrual, added recruitment resources, or a fourth site [1]—address slower-than-expected recruitment but do not address the structural drivers of attrition. The STRONG study, which enrolled 763 acute stroke patients across 28 U.S. sites, retained approximately 63% at 12 months; critically, “75% of drop-outs occurred prior to Visit 2” and were driven by “mortality and inability to travel the distance back to the enrollment site” [20]. The proposal's enrollment from IRF rather than acute care improves retention prospects—patients have already survived the highest-risk period—and the projected 25% total attrition [1] is not unreasonable. However, adding a fourth site or extending accrual does not reduce mortality or eliminate travel barriers, and a more robust contingency would include explicit tracking of reasons for attrition and sensitivity analyses under plausible non-ignorable missingness assumptions, since the proposal acknowledges that “dropout is especially important because it may challenge” the missing-at-random assumption [1].

### Summary

These limitations do not undermine Aim 1: 300 prospectively followed participants with five timepoints can credibly estimate unconditional recovery trajectories across three outcome domains with adequate precision. The design's core descriptive contribution is well-supported. For Aim 2, the observational therapy data support descriptive associations only; 10 candidate SNPs in 300–400 participants lack power for reliable epistatic interaction detection unless effects are large; and 100 imaging participants cannot provide stable estimates in covariate-rich models. The internal validation strategy is thorough for its purpose but cannot substitute for the external validation that clinical deployment would require.

## Feasibility

**The proposal demonstrates robust operational feasibility grounded in established infrastructure, proven methodology, and a research team with directly relevant expertise.** The post-discharge therapy tracking introduces measurement uncertainty that affects the precision of treatment covariates rather than the feasibility of data collection itself.

### Recruitment and retention

The recruitment framework is well-supported by the three participating inpatient rehabilitation facilities—Shirley Ryan AbilityLab, Alexian Brothers Rehabilitation Hospital, and Mary Free Bed Rehabilitation Hospital [1]. These are established IRFs with the clinical volumes necessary to enroll 400 participants over approximately 30 months while retaining 300 at 18 months [1]. The projected 25% total attrition (15% by six months, additional 10% by 18 months) is more optimistic than the 37% attrition at 12 months observed in the multi-site STRONG study [20]. However, the proposal's enrollment from IRF rather than acute care meaningfully improves retention prospects: participants have already survived the highest-risk period and demonstrated capacity to engage in rehabilitation. The contingency plans—longer accrual, added recruitment resources, or a fourth site [1]—are credible given the investigators' experience across three established facilities and the five-year schedule's flexibility.

### Inpatient therapy tracking feasibility

The inpatient therapy tracking system builds directly on the SCIRehab multi-site point-of-care documentation methodology with two meaningful improvements: shifting from PDAs to modern tablets, which reduces the technical problems SCIRehab experienced with devices needing reboot and software reinstallation [3], and adding 10% video validation of logged sessions [1]. SCIRehab achieved 85% exact agreement on intervention activities and 95% on session-level variables across six rehabilitation facilities [3], with documentation time falling below one minute per session once clinicians were familiar with the system [3]. The survey of 56 speech-language pathologists at participating sites confirming that targeted modalities can be identified [1] provides additional site-specific feasibility evidence.

The multi-site challenge is real but addressed through the training and reliability framework. SCIRehab demonstrated that an average patient was treated by 39 different clinicians [21] and that “supplemental documentation added to traditional charting increases the documentation burden,” with clinical responsibilities that “always come first” [3]. The proposal's requirement that clinicians exceed 90% reliability before independent logging, combined with the remediation pathway of research assistant observation for those needing additional practice [1], provides a structured response to these challenges. Reliability testing with quarterly refreshers, following the SCIRehab model that used written case scenarios as continuing education [3], sustains quality over time.

### Assessment battery and technical components

The assessment battery is ambitious, with follow-up visits lasting approximately three to four hours, but is practically mitigated through the option to split visits across consecutive days and conduct home assessments where appropriate [1]. The genetic component uses standard commercial Oragene saliva collection kits and TaqMan allelic-discrimination assays with well-specified protocols [1], presenting no feasibility concerns. The imaging protocol at the Shirley Ryan AbilityLab employs established acquisition sequences and processing pipelines using FSL, SPM, and FreeSurfer [1] that are routine for the team's demonstrated expertise. Data management through REDCap with common case-report forms and central checks [1] provides a proven infrastructure for multi-site coordination.

### Post-discharge therapy tracking: measurement uncertainty

The principal feasibility concern is measurement-related rather than operational. The post-discharge therapy tracking system relies on weekly patient or caregiver checklists supplemented by research assistant phone calls—weekly for two months after discharge, then monthly [1]. No evidence was identified demonstrating that patient or caregiver self-report of therapy utilization in an aphasia population achieves accuracy sufficient for use as continuous covariates in growth-curve models. The language impairment that defines the study population may compromise recall accuracy, and caregiver reports, while valuable, introduce heteroscedasticity since not all patients have consistent caregivers. The shift from weekly to monthly calls after two months creates a measurement density discontinuity that complicates time-varying covariate analysis.

This affects the precision of post-discharge treatment covariates rather than the feasibility of collecting the data. The described contact schedule can gather broad indicators of service engagement—whether someone received outpatient SLP, joined a community group, or had no services—but is unlikely to support fine-grained distinctions such as 60 versus 90 minutes of modality-specific therapy per week. The implication is that post-discharge treatment-outcome associations in Aim 2 will have lower precision than their inpatient counterparts, a limitation that should temper expectations without undermining the study's core descriptive contribution.

## Ethics and safety

**The ethical framework is appropriate and proportionate to an observational study's risk profile, with specific mitigations tailored to the aphasia population that distinguish this proposal from research less attentive to communication vulnerability.**

### Consent and communication access

The consent procedures directly address a barrier that has led other studies to exclude people with aphasia. The proposal commits to using supported conversation techniques during recruitment and assessment, ensuring that aphasia “does not itself prevent understanding” [1]. This is a documented, trainable approach using multimodal communication—gesture, drawing, keyword writing, verification—to support comprehension and expression, and its explicit embedding in the consent process addresses the material risk that people with aphasia may provide consent without adequate understanding when procedures are not adapted. The Tierney-Hendricks et al. (2026) scoping review confirmed that “persons with aphasia and cognitive impairments were often excluded from studies” [22] of transitional care interventions; this proposal's approach actively counters that pattern.

### Burden and risk mitigation

The burden-reduction measures respond proportionately to the three-to-four-hour assessment sessions. Frequent breaks, the option to split visits across consecutive days, and home assessments where appropriate [1] directly reduce fatigue and frustration—risks that are real in a population for whom language tasks are cognitively demanding. Risks are explicitly identified as “fatigue, frustration, MRI discomfort, and loss of confidentiality” [1], and each receives a specific mitigation. MRI eligibility screening is standard and appropriate, covering implants, claustrophobia, pregnancy considerations, and other contraindications before research scanning is offered [1]. Participants who cannot undergo MRI can still contribute the core behavioral data, preserving their inclusion in the study's primary aims [1].

### Oversight and data protection

Northwestern University serves as the single IRB, streamlining ethical review and ensuring consistent standards across sites [1]. The Data Monitoring and Oversight Committee integrates clinical, imaging, genetic, epidemiologic, and patient/family perspectives, with annual reviews of safety, burden, progress, and data integrity [1]. The quality-monitoring procedures reinforce this oversight: 10% of assessment sessions videotaped and rescored, 10% of case-report forms rechecked, and a separate 10% data-entry sample verified [1], with adverse events communicated through site leadership to the co-PIs and institutional oversight. These measures are proportionate to the study's risk level.

Data protection follows appropriate separation: research records are kept separate from medical records and use codes rather than names, with password-protected electronic files, secured physical records, and restricted access [1]. The de-identified data sharing plan specifies that the REDCap dataset will be available on request under an agreement prohibiting reidentification and unauthorized redistribution, defining reporting responsibilities, and requiring destruction after analyses [1]. The shared data—behavioral and patient-reported outcomes, treatment exposure, relevant imaging measures, and candidate-marker information—constitute the core scientific outputs without identifiable elements. These arrangements meet the standards expected for an observational behavioral-imaging-genetic study in a communication-vulnerable population.

## Management

**The management structure, team expertise, and coordination mechanisms are well-conceived and appropriately matched to the study's multi-site, multi-method observational design.**

### Team expertise and responsibilities

The investigative team combines complementary expertise that maps directly onto the study's three aims. Leora Cherney brings depth in aphasia treatment research, including the dose-controlled script training trials that inform the therapy measurement framework [23] [24]. Allen Heinemann contributes the growth-curve modeling expertise demonstrated in SCIRehab IGC applications [6] and the interactive trajectory tool development for spinal cord injury [4]. Additional team members cover genomics and neuroimaging, ensuring no technical component lacks appropriate senior supervision. The assignment of responsibilities follows this expertise logically, though the supplied proposal excerpts reference these roles in summary form rather than providing detailed CVs or allocated effort levels.

### Site coordination and quality assurance

Three established IRFs provide geographic diversity across the Midwest while maintaining manageable oversight from the lead site [1]. Dedicated research assistants at each facility will screen admissions daily, coordinate assessments, and maintain post-discharge contact [1], providing the sustained personnel presence that longitudinal retention demands. REDCap supports common case-report forms and central data checks [1], a practical choice for multi-site standardization. NIHSS certification, standardized training, and continuous speech-language pathology supervision further support cross-site consistency [1]. The Data Monitoring and Oversight Committee, with clinical, imaging, genetic, epidemiologic, and patient/family perspectives, reviews safety, burden, progress, and data integrity annually [1].

The quality-monitoring procedures are proportionately specified: 10% of assessment sessions videotaped and rescored, 10% of case-report forms rechecked, and a separate 10% data-entry sample verified [1]. The inclusion of patient/family representation on the oversight committee is appropriate for research involving patient-reported outcomes and quality-of-life endpoints in a communication-vulnerable population.

### Schedule and contingencies

The five-year schedule is realistically constructed. Site approvals, database setup, and staff training occupy the first two quarters, followed by approximately 30 months of accrual with staggered follow-up assessments extending through year five [1]. Therapy data extraction and cleaning proceed in parallel with ongoing follow-up, and growth-model development, hypothesis testing, bootstrap stability assessment, and cross-validation concentrate in the final year when sufficient longitudinal data have accumulated—avoiding the common pitfall of deferring analysis until data collection completes. The explicit contingency for extending accrual or adding a fourth site [1] demonstrates planning for foreseeable recruitment variation. These arrangements support delivery of the stated aims at the level documented in this proposal.

# Project review

## Overall assessment

The PRECISE project aims to test whether a telephone-based patient navigation program, guided by a prediction model, can improve completion of follow-up colonoscopy among safety-net patients with a positive fecal immunochemical test (FIT). The study uniquely combines the external validation of an existing risk-prediction model with a pragmatic, patient-randomized trial at 28 Sea Mar Community Health Centers. It will evaluate the effectiveness, costs, and cost-effectiveness of navigation, targeting patients with a moderate or low predicted probability of completing colonoscopy on their own. The most valuable expected knowledge is the rigorous, randomized evidence on whether this prediction model can effectively direct scarce navigation resources, providing both a more definitive estimate of navigation’s effect in a Federally Qualified Health Center (FQHC) setting and data on the economic value of a targeted strategy.

The proposal is strongly supported by extensive preliminary work from the STOP CRC trial, which established the prediction model and documented low follow-up rates, and by a robust partnership with Sea Mar, providing access to a large, diverse patient population and integrated data systems. The power analysis is well-grounded, and the two-phase design is logical. The main limitations materially affecting the judgment are the prediction model’s modest discrimination (c-statistic of 0.65), which may degrade during external validation, and the design’s limited statistical power to detect meaningful treatment-effect heterogeneity between risk strata. Furthermore, the patient-randomized design within the same clinics presents an unmitigated risk of contamination, and outcome ascertainment via EHR data is known to be incomplete, potentially introducing differential bias. These issues do not negate the value of the rigorous effectiveness trial but do constrain the project’s ability to definitively achieve its central innovation of precision targeting.

Weighing the strengths against the limitations, this is a strong and well-planned study that addresses a critical public health problem. The pragmatic trial will provide a definitive estimate of navigation effectiveness and valuable cost data, representing a clear advance over the existing evidence base. The principal trade-off is that the project’s most innovative element—the risk-stratified targeting of navigation—is undermined by the prediction model’s modest and uncertain discrimination and limited power for interaction testing. While the proposal acknowledges these limitations, they reduce the likelihood that the study can definitively answer its central question of *for whom* navigation is most beneficial. Nonetheless, a rigorously estimated average effect with economic evaluation remains a highly valuable and supportable contribution, even if the heterogeneity analysis is primarily exploratory.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**The scientific value of PRECISE is high, grounded in a critical public health question with direct consequences for colorectal cancer mortality.** Addressing low rates of follow-up colonoscopy after a positive fecal immunochemical test in safety-net settings is a pressing need, given documented one-year completion rates as low as 33–56% in Federally Qualified Health Centers compared with over 70% in integrated health systems [1]. Failure to complete colonoscopy after an abnormal FIT is associated with a 2.4-fold increased risk of CRC death [2], making effective and scalable interventions a priority. The knowledge PRECISE aims to generate—whether a targeted telephone navigation program can improve completion, at what cost, and for whom—carries substantial practical value for health systems that must allocate scarce navigation resources efficiently.

**The project’s most important expected contribution is generating the first rigorous, randomized evidence on whether a prediction model can direct navigation to patients most likely to forgo colonoscopy.** Prior navigation trials demonstrated average effectiveness but did not address this targeting question [3] [4]. The STOP CRC prediction model identified patients with widely varying adherence probabilities, with quintile separation from approximately 25% to 93% in six-month colonoscopy completion during development [5]. The logical next step—using the model prospectively to test whether navigation effectiveness varies by predicted risk—is what PRECISE proposes [5].

Even if the model’s ability to identify differential treatment effects proves limited, the trial will deliver a more definitive estimate of telephone navigation’s average effectiveness in an FQHC population than any prior study. The published results from the now-completed trial confirm this value: the observed 13.0 percentage-point improvement in one-year colonoscopy completion (55.1% vs. 42.1%) represents the largest randomized evaluation of navigation for follow-up colonoscopy in an FQHC setting, with roughly twice the sample of any predecessor [6].

**The scientific value is further enhanced by two features that distinguish PRECISE from earlier work.** The comprehensive economic evaluation from a health-plan perspective will produce incremental cost-effectiveness estimates per additional colonoscopy, adenoma detected, and cancer detected, along with numbers needed to navigate by probability stratum [5]. These data directly address the implementation question of whether targeted navigation represents good value for money—a dimension absent from prior navigation trials. Second, the qualitative component, comprising approximately 60 patient interviews and 25–30 staff interviews, will document barriers, experiences, and implementation considerations in a diverse, 37% Latino FQHC population [5]. This evidence will inform future scale-up efforts regardless of the quantitative findings on effect modification.

Importantly, the value of the study does not depend solely on demonstrating that navigation effects differ by predicted risk. A rigorously estimated average effect with detailed cost data remains a meaningful contribution that advances the field beyond what STOP CRC and prior navigation trials separately established. The prediction model’s external validation itself adds knowledge about the transportability of risk models across health systems—a finding with implications beyond this single study. The proposal’s explicit acknowledgment that the study will “clarify whether and how health systems can target navigation sustainably” [5] reflects an appropriate framing that balances the innovative targeting hypothesis against the more certain contribution of a well-powered effectiveness trial.

## Innovation

**PRECISE is genuinely innovative in its design—it is, at the time of the proposal, the first study to combine external validation of a prediction model with a prospective randomized trial to test risk-stratified navigation for colonoscopy follow-up.** This goes meaningfully beyond the two bodies of prior work it builds upon. Earlier navigation trials, such as those by Green et al. (2014) and Raich et al. (2012), tested average effects of navigation on diagnostic resolution or colonoscopy completion but did not address whether navigation can be efficiently targeted to patients most likely to benefit [3] [4]. Conversely, the STOP CRC prediction model developed by Petrik et al. (2020) demonstrated that EHR-derived variables could separate patients into adherence quintiles, but the authors explicitly called for “further research … to test the effectiveness of interventions for patients who have a low and moderate probability of completing follow-up colonoscopy” [7]. PRECISE directly answers that call by prospectively using the model to classify patients and then randomizing within predicted-adherence strata [5].

**The innovation operates on several interdependent levels, each representing a novel contribution in the FQHC context.** First, the targeting logic itself—excluding the highest-adherence quintile (approximately 93% six-month completion in development) from randomization and focusing navigation resources on the remaining 80% of patients—is a conceptually novel application of the Chronic Care Model’s decision-support domain [5]. No prior trial had prospectively tested whether a risk-prediction tool could guide the allocation of navigation in real time within clinic workflows. Second, the proposal’s use of clinic-embedded staff rather than a research-only navigator service represents a methodological innovation aimed at sustainability [5]. Third, the economic evaluation plans to estimate cost-effectiveness by predicted-adherence stratum, generating data on numbers needed to navigate that no prior trial has provided for FQHC-based telephone navigation [5].

The proposal also introduces innovation in its combination of methods. The integration of a formal external validation phase using a temporally distinct Sea Mar cohort with a subsequent pragmatic trial, all using the same health system’s EHR infrastructure, creates a coherent pipeline from model assessment to prospective clinical application [5]. The qualitative component, which purposively samples both probability groups and patients who did or did not complete colonoscopy, adds a dimension for understanding how navigation experiences differ across risk strata [5].

**The principal constraint on the innovation’s impact is the prediction model’s modest discrimination and the consequent difficulty of detecting treatment-effect heterogeneity.** A c-statistic of 0.65—and the realistic possibility of degradation during external validation, as documented when a related STOP CRC model dropped to a bootstrap-corrected c-statistic of 0.55 at Sea Mar [8]—limits the extent to which meaningfully distinct risk strata can be created. The proposal’s own power calculations indicate that the interaction test requires very large odds ratios (2.17–2.98) to reach significance [5], meaning the trial is well-powered for its main effect but underpowered for its most innovative question. The published results subsequently confirmed that treatment-effect heterogeneity was not significant (P = 0.16), and the pattern ran opposite to the targeting hypothesis: the highest-probability group showed a 14.8 percentage-point benefit versus only 2.7 points in the lowest-probability group [6].

This does not retroactively diminish the originality of asking the question in a properly designed trial—testing a plausible hypothesis with appropriate methods is how the field advances—but it means the innovation should be understood primarily as a methodological contribution that demonstrates how risk-stratified trials can be conducted, rather than as a demonstration that precision navigation works as hypothesized.

## Rigor

**The rigor of PRECISE is good overall, reflecting a carefully designed two-phase study with a well-justified power analysis, but it is constrained by three interrelated limitations that affect the strength of causal inference.** These are the prediction model’s modest and uncertain discrimination, a patient-randomized design vulnerable to contamination, and differential outcome ascertainment through EHR data.

**Study structure and model validation.** The proposal’s two-phase structure is logically sound. Phase I externally validates the prediction model on a temporally distinct historical Sea Mar cohort, assessing discrimination (c-statistic), calibration, and explained variation, with a prespecified threshold of a ≥0.05 decline in c-statistic triggering model updating [5]. Only then does Phase II use the validated model to stratify prospective patients for the randomized trial. This sequential design appropriately defers the trial’s stratification logic to empirical performance rather than assuming the development model transports unchanged. The proposal explicitly acknowledges that calibration and similar results at nine months “support further external validation rather than assuming the development model transfers unchanged” [5].

Published experience from the same research group is cautionary: when a related STOP CRC model was recalibrated at Sea Mar, the bootstrap-corrected c-statistic dropped to 0.55, exceeding the ≥0.05 threshold and requiring complete model redevelopment [8]. The proposal includes appropriate model-updating provisions, but if coefficients are re-estimated at Sea Mar, the trial would be stratifying on a model that has not itself been externally validated, and the resulting strata would lack independent performance characterization.

**Power analysis.** The power analysis is transparent and grounded. The 44% usual-care completion rate in the moderate/low adherence population is not directly observed but is internally consistent: with an overall 54% one-year completion in STOP CRC and the top quintile completing at approximately 93%, the remaining 80% must average roughly 44% [5]. Independent pre-trial Sea Mar data documented 43% one-year colonoscopy completion, closely matching this assumption [9]. The detectable improvement of 12.9 percentage points (from 44% to 56.9%) with the design effect from an assumed intraclass correlation of 0.03 is clinically meaningful and comparable to effects in similar FQHC trials, including the Ganguly et al. (2026) SCORE trial (69.0% vs. 38.7%) [10] [5]. The proposal appropriately presents detectable differences both with and without the clustering penalty (12.9 vs. 9.2 percentage points), conveying transparently what the design effect costs in precision.

**Limitation 1: constrained power for interaction testing.** The prediction model’s modest discrimination limits the risk-stratified design’s inferential power. The development model’s bootstrap-corrected c-statistic of 0.65 reflects modest separation [5]. The detectable interaction odds ratios for the moderation analysis—2.98 with the design effect and 2.17 without—are very large relative to the expected main effect [5]. This means the trial is well-powered for its primary effectiveness question but underpowered for its central innovation of detecting treatment-effect heterogeneity across risk strata. The published results confirm this: no significant effect modification was found (P = 0.16), and the pattern ran opposite to the targeting hypothesis [6].

**Limitation 2: contamination risk.** The patient-randomized design within the same clinics creates contamination risk that is recognized but inadequately addressed. Navigation is delivered by clinic staff with EHR access in the same 28 clinics where usual-care patients are managed [5]. Staff other than the statistician are blinded to predicted probability but “assignment to navigation is not blinded” [5]. The proposal plans to sample 10% of usual-care encounters to “assess changes and possible contamination,” but this monitoring is configured primarily for fidelity rather than systematic spillover detection [5]. It is unlikely to detect subtle forms of contamination, such as clinic staff altering their referral coordination behavior after exposure to the navigation program, or navigated patients consuming scarce appointment slots and delaying care for controls. A cluster-randomized design would have prevented this at the cost of requiring more clusters.

**Limitation 3: differential outcome ascertainment.** Outcome ascertainment through EHR codes introduces potential differential measurement bias. The proposal acknowledges that EHR codes capture approximately 80% of colonoscopy reports, with chart audits supplementing missing cases by a blinded auditor [5]. However, navigation generates six structured telephone contacts per patient, each documented in the EHR [5]. This systematically creates more opportunities for colonoscopy completion to be documented in the navigation arm. If a completed colonoscopy is more likely to be coded and filed when a navigator is actively tracking the patient, the measurement error becomes differential—potentially favoring the intervention arm. The blinded auditor correctly classifies cases within the audited subset, but differential selection into the audit set remains. The Coronado et al. (2021) Sea Mar analysis acknowledged that “we cannot be sure that all records of completed colonoscopies were in the EHR” [9].

**Interaction of threats and analytical approach.** These threats interact: contamination may inflate the effect estimate, differential ascertainment may further inflate it, and the random-effects model correctly handles clustering variance but cannot correct for bias in the point estimate. The proposal’s multilevel logistic regression with clinic as a random effect is appropriate for the design [5] [11], but the choice not to stratify randomization by clinic—contrary to standard recommendations for multicentre individually randomized trials [11]—means chance clinic-level imbalances between arms are possible with only 28 clinics. The trial can still produce useful evidence about navigation effectiveness, but the estimated effect should be interpreted as an upper bound, and the risk-stratified analyses should be treated primarily as exploratory.

## Feasibility

**The feasibility of PRECISE is good, grounded in solid planning data from the STOP CRC trial and pre-trial analyses at Sea Mar, with core logistical assumptions confirmed by subsequent published results.** The proposal demonstrates a realistic understanding of the patient population, clinic infrastructure, and enrollment dynamics necessary to execute a pragmatic randomized trial in a large FQHC system.

**Enrollment and volume projections are well-supported.** Sea Mar operates 28 participating nonspecialty clinics serving approximately 29,000 screening-eligible patients, with a 45% FIT completion rate and an estimated 9% positivity rate, yielding roughly 1,186 positive FIT results annually [5]. The proposal projects that approximately 80% of these (949 patients) will fall into the moderate or low predicted-adherence strata, with 924 patients randomized over an 18-month enrollment window [5]. The published trial results validate these projections: 1,200 individuals with abnormal FIT results were identified, and 985 were randomized (967 in the primary analysis), slightly exceeding the planned target [6]. The independent pre-trial Sea Mar analysis of 711 patients with abnormal FIT over one year documented 43% one-year colonoscopy completion [9], closely matching the proposal’s assumed 44% usual-care completion rate [5]. Both the published trial’s usual-care arm (42.1%) and the overall completion rate among moderate/low adherence patients in the development data confirmed that the planning assumptions were well-calibrated [6] [5].

The patient-randomized design with 28 clinics and an average cluster size of 33 patients is appropriately powered. The design effect of approximately 1.96 at an intraclass correlation of 0.03 is modest, and the Castañeda et al. (2023) Hub-and-Spoke protocol similarly assumed power to detect 13–19 percentage-point differences in comparable CHC settings with ICC of 0.02–0.06 [2].

**Timeline dependencies are logical and include reasonable buffer.** Model validation (months 4–7) completes before randomization (months 12–14), providing a five-month window for recalibration if the prespecified c-statistic decline threshold is triggered [5]. Staff training (months 14–17) precedes the 20-month intervention delivery window (months 15–34), with qualitative interviews, chart abstraction, and analysis extending into the final year. The quarterly advisory board meetings, running throughout all five years, provide a structured mechanism for ongoing oversight and for surfacing operational problems before they threaten key milestones [5].

**Operational risks are manageable but warrant attention.** The principal concern is navigator turnover. The proposal’s planning estimate of 970 hours for 462 navigated patients assumes a single navigator position [5]. The published trial ultimately employed nine different staff members in this centralized role over the study duration [6], a turnover rate amplified by—but not entirely attributable to—the COVID-19 pandemic [6]. The proposal does not specify contingency plans for navigator departure, such as cross-training backup staff or maintaining a pool of trained candidates.

The documented 79.7% patient reach rate within 18 call attempts [12] demonstrates that trained staff can contact most patients, but the 20% unreachable fraction limits the intervention’s ceiling. The dependence on EHR codes for primary outcome ascertainment carries a known 20% data gap requiring supplemental chart audits [5], a limitation acknowledged in the published Sea Mar analysis [9] that is manageable but not fully correctable within the proposed design. These risks are real but individually modest; none alone threatens the feasibility of completing the trial as designed.

## Ethics and safety

**The ethics and safety plan is appropriate for the nature of the study, which delivers a supportive service to encourage adherence to a clinically indicated follow-up procedure rather than testing an investigational intervention or withholding standard care.** PRECISE does not involve an experimental drug, device, or procedure; the navigation program assists patients in accessing colonoscopy that has already been clinically recommended based on a positive FIT result. The ethical framework is proportionate to this low-risk context.

The proposal incorporates the standard protections expected of a pragmatic health services trial. IRB approval is scheduled in the first three months of the five-year timeline [5]. De-identified data with study IDs support risk classification, and secure quarterly EHR transfers with controlled clinical-record access govern data handling [5]. Patients allocated to navigation give verbal consent for recorded telephone contacts, with 25% of calls recorded during the first three months and 5% thereafter, a monitoring approach that balances fidelity assessment against privacy intrusion [5]. The advisory board, which includes patient and community representatives alongside clinicians, researchers, and policymakers, provides quarterly oversight and is specifically tasked with interpreting de-identified qualitative findings [5]. This structure ensures community perspectives inform both study conduct and interpretation of results.

**Two ethical features of the design are satisfactorily addressed.** First, the trial excludes the highest-adherence quintile from randomization and places these patients under surveillance only. This design choice raises the question of whether any group is denied potential benefit. The rationale is evidence-based: in the development data, the top quintile achieved approximately 93% six-month colonoscopy completion without navigation [5], leaving little room for improvement and making navigation a likely inefficient use of scarce resources in this subgroup. The proposal also specifies that if navigation proves effective, it will subsequently be offered to usual-care and surveillance patients who have not completed colonoscopy [5], ensuring that no patient is permanently denied access to a beneficial service identified during the trial.

Second, navigation is an addition to, not a replacement for, usual clinical care. The proposal explicitly states that navigation “helps patients access existing care and does not replace clinically indicated colonoscopy referral or treatment” [5]. Both arms receive the existing notification and referral process; navigation provides supplementary support for barriers such as bowel preparation, transport, scheduling, and understanding of the procedure [5]. This additive design mitigates concerns about withholding needed services.

The qualitative interview component, which will sample patients who did and did not complete colonoscopy across both probability groups, addresses participant burden through purposive sampling of approximately 60 patients and 25–30 staff, with interviews conducted in English or Spanish according to participant preference [5]. Transcripts will be coded using content analysis with disagreements resolved by consensus, and the advisory board will help interpret de-identified findings. No material risks to participants, communities, or data are evident beyond those inherent in routine health services research.

## Management

**The management plan is well-organized, with a strong institutional partnership, an appropriately composed multi-disciplinary team, and a five-year schedule that logically sequences the project’s interdependent phases.** The proposal documents a substantive collaboration with Sea Mar Community Health Centers, which is critical for a pragmatic trial embedded within a functioning FQHC system [5]. Sea Mar’s medical director, Ricardo Jimenez, is a named partner, and the health center’s commitment of staff, training, tracking, and a clinical champion represents genuine institutional support, not merely a data-use agreement or a letter of access. This partnership is the foundation for nearly every operational element: patient identification from the integrated EHR, risk stratification using clinical data, delivery of navigation by clinic-embedded staff, and outcome ascertainment through the health record [5]. The published trial results confirm that this partnership functioned as planned, with a Sea Mar analyst performing randomization for 892 of the 985 enrolled patients and EHR integration enabling the identification of eligible patients from a rolling pool of FIT-positive results [6].

**Team composition and navigator staffing.** The assembled team includes expertise in prediction modeling, gastroenterology, economic evaluation, qualitative research, and health-information systems, all of which map directly onto the specific aims [5]. The centralized, telephone-based navigation model using clinic staff with medical-record access is a design strength: it embeds the intervention within existing workflows rather than creating a parallel research-only service, which directly supports the project’s sustainability aim [5]. The training plan—two didactic days followed by eight weeks of coaching, with competency and fidelity checklists—mirrors the structured approach from the New Hampshire Colorectal Cancer Screening Program, whose materials PRECISE adapts [5].

**Navigator turnover is the principal management risk**, recognized in the proposal but lacking detailed contingency plans. The planning estimate of 970 hours for 462 navigated patients assumes a stable navigator capable of sustained bilingual delivery [5]. The published trial ultimately required nine different staff members to fill this single centralized role over the study duration [6], a turnover rate amplified by—but not entirely attributable to—the COVID-19 pandemic [6]. High turnover in a position that demands rapport-building, barrier assessment, and scheduled longitudinal contact risks fidelity, delays, and loss of institutional knowledge. The proposal does describe weekly navigator meetings for the first three months and bi-weekly meetings thereafter, along with recorded-call review and booster training [5], which would help maintain quality during personnel transitions. Cross-training backup navigators or formalizing a pool of qualified candidates from the outset would strengthen the management plan.

**Schedule integration.** The five-year schedule integrates the project’s interdependent activities with reasonable sequencing and buffer. Model validation (months 4–7) completes before randomization (months 12–14), providing a five-month window for recalibration if needed [5]. Staff training precedes the 20-month intervention delivery window, with qualitative interviews, chart abstraction, and analysis extending into the final year. The schedule acknowledges the dependency of the trial on Phase I results and builds in adequate time for the labor-intensive process of chart auditing for the 20% of colonoscopy reports not captured by EHR codes [5]. The quarterly advisory board meetings, running throughout all five years, provide a structured mechanism for ongoing oversight [5].

**In summary, the management arrangements are sound overall**, with clear roles, complementary expertise, and a realistic schedule supported by an operational partnership that has demonstrated its viability in the subsequent trial conduct. The plan would benefit from explicit contingency provisions for navigator continuity, but the existing training, supervision, and fidelity monitoring mechanisms provide a reasonable foundation for managing this risk within a well-supported project.

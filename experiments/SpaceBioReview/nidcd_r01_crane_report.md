# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project investigates how humans combine visual and inertial information to perceive self-motion heading, decide whether the two signals originate from a single event, and adapt when the signals remain consistently discrepant. Experiments will map explicit common-cause (unity) judgments and independent visual and inertial heading estimates across the full 360° horizontal plane in healthy participants, manipulate timing, motion profile, and visual-field size to test the evidence for common causation, and extend the paradigm to individuals with unilateral vestibular loss. The most valuable expected knowledge is the systematic characterisation of the dissociation between explicit causal attribution and continued sensory crosstalk—a distinction that computational models of multisensory integration currently cannot explain—and evidence on whether persistent heading errors after vestibular damage can be durably reduced through perceptual adaptation.

The central innovation is well supported by converging evidence from prior work: Acerbi et al. (2018) demonstrated that joint analysis of unity and heading discrimination constrains causal inference models, but tested only within ±25°, while the Crane laboratory has established the necessary psychophysical methods, coordinate-frame properties, and adaptation feasibility in smaller-scale studies. The preliminary data (four participants) show the predicted dissociation between unity judgments and inertial heading bias. The main limitations concern sample sizes for the adaptation and clinical experiments, which rest on small pilots (n=1 for heading-offset adaptation; n=5 for chronic-lesion adaptation) whose effect-size estimates have wide uncertainty, and the longitudinal acute-lesion protocol, where the assumed 50% attrition may be optimistic relative to published retention rates for comparable populations. A further interpretive caveat is that the forced-choice point-of-subjective-equality shift in the adaptation experiments cannot definitively distinguish genuine perceptual recalibration from a shift in decision criterion, a limitation the field broadly acknowledges.

The proposal presents a well-motivated, mechanistically rich programme of work that advances a clear conceptual distinction—explicit unity versus implicit cross-modal influence—and tests it across a carefully chosen set of parametric experiments. The main interpretive limitation, the inability to fully separate perceptual recalibration from criterion shift in the adaptation outcomes, is a recognized constraint in human psychophysics and does not undermine the core descriptive and mechanistic aims. Provided the longitudinal acute-lesion recruitment proves feasible and the adaptation effect sizes are within the range assumed, the current plan offers a sufficient route to its stated goals.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**Rating: Excellent (A), confidence high (B).** The project addresses a fundamental gap in multisensory perception: whether the brain treats visual and inertial heading signals as arising from a common cause, how the two signals interact when causal attribution is uncertain, and whether persistent heading errors after vestibular damage can be durably reduced through perceptual adaptation. The attainable knowledge is both descriptive and mechanistic, with translational potential.

**Causal inference across the full horizontal plane.** The core descriptive contribution is a systematic characterisation of the relationship between explicit common-cause (unity) judgments and continued cross-modal influence (heading bias) across the full 360° horizontal plane. Prior work has approached this question from two directions, neither of which fully answers it. Acerbi et al. (2018) demonstrated, in 11 participants, that joint analysis of explicit unity judgments and implicit heading discrimination constrains causal-inference models—implicit heading data alone could not rule out forced-fusion—but that study tested headings only within ±25° of straight ahead with disparities of 0–40° [1]. De Winkel et al. (2015) tested the full 360° range yet asked only for a single combined heading estimate without independent modality-specific estimates or unity judgments; five of nine participants appeared to use forced fusion even at 90° discrepancies, but the interpretive problem is that forced fusion and causal inference are difficult to distinguish without independent modality-specific estimates and explicit causal judgments [2]. The Crane laboratory's own prior work provides essential building blocks—retinal versus body-centred coordinate frames (Crane 2015) [3], reliability-based weighting near Bayesian ideals (Crane 2017) [4], and the temporal integration window for heading bias (Rodriguez & Crane 2021) [5]—yet none of these studies measured explicit causal attribution. What the current project adds is the simultaneous measurement of explicit unity judgments and independent visual and inertial heading estimates under identical stimulus conditions across the full horizontal plane. A recent review by Sun et al. (2026) identifies as a key open question whether flexible visual-vestibular interactions reflect genuine causal inference or merely changes in cue weighting and decision strategy [6]; the proposed dual-measurement approach directly addresses this gap.

**Parametric dissection of common-cause evidence.** The parametric experiments in Aim I exploit three well-chosen dimensions to probe the evidence for common causation. Temporal offsets (50-ms steps through 200 ms) test whether the temporal integration window for unity judgments differs from the approximately 250-ms window already established for heading bias [5]; a difference would support partial independence of the two processes. Motion profile manipulation (varying acceleration and duration) extends the recent finding by Yakouma, Anson & Crane (2025) that an inverted visual velocity profile reduced cross-modal bias to approximately 64% of the congruent level [7], testing whether the same manipulation differentially affects unity and bias. Visual-field size (98°, 45°, and 20°) examines whether peripheral optic flow, which strongly drives vection, influences unity and bias through separable mechanisms [8]. Each manipulation probes a distinct aspect of the evidence the brain might use to infer a common cause, and the within-subject comparison of unity and bias under each manipulation provides stronger constraints on computational models than either measure alone.

**Clinical extension.** The mechanistic value extends beyond normal perception. Experiment IC translates the causal-inference paradigm to individuals with unilateral vestibular loss, where reduced inertial reliability is predicted to increase visual influence and broaden common-cause boundaries [8]. Knowing whether this operates differently at different heading directions—the Crane lab has already shown direction-dependent biases in healthy participants [8]—matters for understanding how vestibular pathology alters multisensory processing in ecologically relevant conditions.

**Heading error time course and durable correction.** The adaptation aims offer a different kind of value: establishing whether heading errors after vestibular damage are durable correction targets. The longitudinal acute-lesion design (IIIA) will distinguish between two competing accounts of persistent heading bias—an otolith-mediated sway asymmetry versus residual rotational compensation—by comparing the time course of heading errors, sway, and rotational thresholds [8]. This mechanistic question has clinical significance because it identifies which sensory signal drives the persistent error and therefore which should be targeted in rehabilitation. The chronic-lesion adaptation experiment (IIIB) tests whether either of two adaptation methods (heading-offset or rotating virtual environment) produces durable correction when applied repeatedly, with retention tracked weekly [8]. The preliminary data in five chronic patients show a mean deviation of 7.5 degrees, strong first-session correction, and some retention at one week in four of five [8]. The proposal appropriately describes this as a mechanistic study and explicitly states that a subsequent randomised clinical trial would be needed to test functional benefit [8]; its contribution is to establish whether durable perceptual correction is achievable, which would justify that trial.

**Methodological and normative contributions.** The project will also deliver validated measurement methods and normative data—the full 144-condition mapping of unity and bias in healthy participants, with parametric variants, provides a reference dataset that the broader vestibular research community can use for comparison with clinical populations and for constraining computational models. The methodological contribution of combining forced-choice psychophysics with continuous estimates across independent modalities is itself non-trivial; the preliminary data already demonstrate that the dissociation between unity and bias is measurable and systematic [8].

## Innovation

**Rating: Excellent (A), confidence high (B).** The project's central innovation is the systematic separation of explicit common-cause (unity) judgments from continued cross-modal influence (heading bias), combined with independent visual and inertial heading estimates, all measured across the full 360° horizontal plane. This combination is absent from the existing literature and addresses an identified gap in computational models of multisensory integration.

**What distinguishes this from prior work.** Prior work has approached the problem from two directions, neither complete. Acerbi et al. (2018) explicitly compared unity judgments with implicit heading discrimination in the same 11 participants and demonstrated that joint analysis of both tasks was required to rule out forced-fusion—implicit heading data alone could not distinguish causal-inference strategies [1] [6]. That study, however, tested headings only within ±25° of straight ahead with disparities of 0–40° [1], a narrow range that cannot reveal how the unity–bias relationship behaves at larger headings where the Crane laboratory has shown systematic, direction-dependent biases. De Winkel et al. (2015) tested the full 360° range but asked only for a single combined heading estimate without independent modality-specific estimates or explicit unity judgments [2]. The Crane laboratory's own prior work provides individual pieces of the puzzle—retinal versus body-centred coordinate frames (Crane 2015) [3], reliability-based weighting near Bayesian ideals (Crane 2017) [4], the temporal integration window for heading bias of approximately 250 ms (Rodriguez & Crane 2021) [5], and the finding that an inverted visual velocity profile reduces cross-modal bias to approximately 64% of the congruent level (Yakouma, Anson & Crane 2025) [7]—yet none of these studies measured explicit causal attribution. The current proposal brings all three elements together for the first time.

**Empirical grounding of the central prediction.** The preliminary data from four participants demonstrate the predicted dissociation: unity judgments drop below 4% at offsets of at least 90°, yet inertial estimates remain biased toward the visual heading at offsets of 30–120° in every participant, with the effect especially strong for non-cardinal directions [8]. This pattern directly motivates the central prediction that cross-modal influence spans a larger discrepancy range than explicit common-cause perception [8].

**Theoretical motivation of the parametric tests.** The parametric manipulations in Experiment IB are theoretically well-motivated because each probes a distinct aspect of the evidence for common causation. Temporal offsets test whether the window for unity differs from the approximately 250-ms window already established for bias [8] [5]; motion profile tests whether the same stimulus manipulation differentially affects unity and bias, building directly on the Yakouma et al. (2025) finding of reduced bias with an inverted velocity profile [7]; visual-field size tests whether peripheral optic flow, which strongly drives vection, influences unity and bias through separable mechanisms [8]. Each manipulation is chosen to address a distinct theoretical question, and the combined design allows a systematic mapping of how the evidence for common causation affects unity judgments and cross-modal influence.

**Clinical extension and field-level contribution.** The clinical extension to unilateral vestibular loss adds novelty by translating a basic-science causal-inference paradigm to a population in whom vestibular reliability is reduced, testing whether the unity–bias relationship is systematically altered [8]. A recent review by Sun et al. (2026) identifies as a key open question whether flexible visual-vestibular interactions reflect genuine causal inference or merely changes in cue weighting and decision strategy [6]; the proposed dual-measurement approach across the full horizontal plane addresses this directly and would provide the most complete behavioural characterization of visual-vestibular causal inference to date.

## Rigor

**Rating: Good (B), confidence high (B).** The project employs well-established psychophysical methods—forced-choice tasks fitted with cumulative Gaussian functions, bootstrap confidence intervals, and interleaved staircases—that are standard in the field and appropriately applied to the research questions [8]. The 144-condition mapping in Experiment IA provides a thorough descriptive characterization of unity judgments and heading bias across the full horizontal plane, with contingency plans for denser sampling if 30-degree increments prove too coarse [8]. These core methods are a strength and support confident inference for the descriptive and parametric aims. Three limitations temper the rating from excellent to good, none of which is fatal to the project's main goals but each of which affects the certainty of specific inferences.

**Criterion shift versus perceptual recalibration in adaptation experiments.** The adaptation experiments (IIA, IIB, IIIB) measure recalibration as the shift in the point of subjective equality (PSE) derived from forced-choice psychometric functions [8]. This is the standard approach in the visual-vestibular recalibration literature: Zeng et al. (2023) and Zaidel et al. (2011) both used the same method, defining recalibration as the post-minus-pre PSE shift and interpreting it as a perceptual change—the heading that participants "would supposedly perceive as straight ahead" [9] [10]. However, a PSE shift in a two-alternative forced-choice task is mathematically compatible with either a genuine change in the sensory representation of heading or a shift in the decision criterion separating left from right responses.

The proposal includes several design features that reduce this concern: no correctness feedback during testing removes the most direct source of explicit response-strategy learning [8]; visual and inertial estimates are obtained in separate trial blocks, so any response strategy would need to apply differentially across modalities to produce the asymmetric shifts observed in the preliminary data [8]; and the adaptation task differs structurally from the test task, reducing simple carryover of the adaptation response rule [8]. These features mitigate but do not eliminate the criterion-shift concern, because no independent criterion measure such as confidence ratings or signal-detection modelling is incorporated. This is a recognized limitation in human psychophysical adaptation research generally, and not unique to this proposal.

The generalization testing, in which continuous estimates probe whether adaptation transfers beyond trained headings [8], provides partial but not definitive protection. If adaptation were purely a response strategy, it should generalize broadly; genuine perceptual recalibration often shows specificity to the trained range. However, the generalization testing is placed as a secondary measure ("in selected experiments") rather than an integral feature of every experiment, which weakens its protective value: the proposal does not specify which experiments will include generalization testing, what the generalization range will be, or how many participants will contribute generalization data [8]. A positive finding—that adaptation is local to the trained heading range—would strengthen the case for perceptual recalibration, but a null generalization result could mean either genuine global recalibration or a global response strategy, and the design does not fully distinguish these alternatives.

**Small-pilot uncertainty in effect-size estimates.** The sample-size calculations for the adaptation and clinical experiments rest on very small pilots. The heading-offset adaptation effect size (8-degree expected shift, SD=10) is informed by a single participant showing a 16.2-degree shift [8]. The chronic-lesion adaptation pilot (n=5) yielded a mean deviation of 7.5±3.4 degrees, but the SD of change after repeated adaptation cannot be reliably estimated from this sample [8]. The proposal appropriately uses conservative assumptions in power calculations and plans larger samples (n=15 for IIA; n=20+ for IIIB) [8], but the uncertainty in the underlying effect-size estimates means the confidence intervals around the adaptation effects will be wider than the formal power calculations suggest. The primary mechanistic aims, however, do not depend on precise effect-size estimation; they depend on detecting and characterizing the adaptation phenomenon, for which the planned sample sizes are adequate.

**Longitudinal acute-lesion attrition assumptions.** The longitudinal acute-lesion design (IIIA) assumes approximately 50% attrition over 12 months [8]. Published data from comparable populations show 75–85% completion rates for substantially shorter protocols: Hao et al. (2025) reported 75% completion at post-operative day 1 and over 85% at 90 days in vestibular schwannoma patients [11]; Sjögren et al. (2026) saw 38% of initially recruited acute unilateral vestibulopathy patients exit before final analysis for technical and methodological reasons alone [12]. A year-long protocol with weekly-then-monthly visits in acutely ill patients travelling from distance may face higher attrition. The proposal acknowledges this [8] but does not discuss how differential attrition would be handled analytically; the etiological heterogeneity (neuritis, gentamicin, surgical) further complicates interpretation because attrition may not be random across etiologies.

**Statistical analysis quality.** The analysis plan is otherwise sound. Repeated blocks improve within-subject precision for continuous estimates [8]; the conservative p ≤ 0.01 criterion and resampling-based individual inference are appropriate for the psychophysical design [8]. The Geno et al. (2024) data from the Crane laboratory indicate that the 15-degree SD assumption for Experiment IA is substantially larger than the published between-subject SD of 5–6 degrees for inertial heading PSE [13], making the sample-size calculations conservative for the core descriptive mapping. A 15-degree SD could be justified if the bias of interest is the change in bias induced by visual-inertial discrepancy (the attraction effect), which may have larger variability than baseline PSE; the proposal is not explicit about this distinction. The minimum detectable change (MDC) for heading PSE across visits is 9.3–9.6 degrees [13], which means individual-level changes smaller than about 9–10 degrees cannot be reliably distinguished from measurement error; group-level analyses, which the proposal plans, will therefore be essential for the clinical experiments.

## Feasibility

**Rating: Good (B), confidence high (B).** The project builds on a well-equipped laboratory with validated hardware, established psychophysical protocols, and direct access to both healthy participant pools and clinical populations. The principal feasibility considerations concern recruitment-dependent aims rather than technical capability.

**Laboratory infrastructure.** The six-degree-of-freedom hexapod motion platform delivers controlled translations and rotations synchronized with visual displays of up to 98° azimuth, with a second platform available for parallel configurations and a custom multi-axis sled providing greater translation and unlimited yaw range for conditions exceeding platform limits [8]. Virtual-reality systems with head tracking and binocular disparity, video eye tracking, accelerometers, and rate gyros provide the stimulus delivery and movement characterization required for all proposed experiments [8]. The psychophysical methods—forced-choice tasks, interleaved staircases, cumulative Gaussian fitting with bootstrap confidence intervals—are established in the laboratory's prior publications [8] and have been successfully applied to the specific stimulus and response modalities proposed here (Crane 2015; Crane 2017; Rodriguez & Crane 2021) [3] [4] [5]. No novel equipment development is required.

**Participant access.** The proposal states an anticipated approximately 120 normal controls and 30 participants with vestibulopathy over five years, drawing on a screened institutional database, recurring healthy participants, and patients from otolaryngology clinics [8]. The PI's demonstrated ability to recruit and test participants for heading-perception studies—47 healthy adults in the Geno et al. (2024) psychometrics study alone [13]—provides confidence that the healthy-participant portions of Aims I and II, which form the project's mechanistic core, are readily achievable.

**Longitudinal acute-lesion recruitment.** The critical feasibility dependency is recruitment for the longitudinal clinical studies. Experiment IIIA requires approximately 32 recently lesioned participants to yield 16 completers after the assumed 50% attrition over a 12-month protocol with weekly visits in the first month, monthly for six months, then at nine months and one year [8]. As discussed under Rigor, published retention data from comparable populations suggest this assumption is plausible but not conservative: Hao et al. (2025) reported 75% completion at post-operative day 1 and over 85% at 90 days in a substantially shorter protocol [11]; Sjögren et al. (2026) saw 38% of initially recruited patients exit before final analysis for technical and methodological reasons alone [12]. The proposal acknowledges that serial visits may be difficult for patients travelling long distances [8] and that slow-growing lesions will make effective onset uncertain [8]. These factors, combined with the etiological heterogeneity requiring separate analyses, mean the acute-lesion time-course data may be sparser than planned. Experiment IIIB benefits from a larger and more accessible chronic-lesion population, and the proposal's plan to enrol 20 or more participants, with larger baseline deviations above ten degrees preferred [8], is feasible given clinical access.

**Adaptation protocol tolerability and pilot support.** The adaptation feasibility is supported by pilot data, though from very small samples. The heading-offset method produced a 16.2-degree shift in one participant [8]; the rotating virtual environment produced a 12-degree shift [8]; and five chronic patients showed first-session correction with some retention at one week in four of five [8]. These pilots demonstrate that the adaptation protocols are tolerable and capable of producing measurable heading shifts, but they provide limited information about the range of individual responses or the durability of correction beyond one week—questions the current project is designed to answer. The explicit provisions for adjusting session number, exposure duration, and rotational speed in response to discomfort [8] appropriately manage tolerability risk. The preliminary DHI data show a mean change of −0.8 (range −4 to 2) in the five chronic patients [8], indicating no systematic symptom worsening in that small sample.

**Schedule and operational demands.** The five-year schedule integrates the three aims in a logical sequence, with Year 1 establishing normal causal-inference methods and beginning all adaptation and patient studies in parallel, and later years extending parametric manipulations and completing patient data collection [8]. The parallel early execution of multiple aims places simultaneous demands on personnel and clinical recruitment, though the PI plus a full-time technician [8] and the availability of a second platform for parallel configurations [8] provide some buffer. Overall, the mechanistic aims are well-supported by existing capability, while the clinical aims carry manageable but real recruitment risk that the proposal acknowledges and partially mitigates.

## Ethics and safety

**Rating: Excellent (A), confidence high (B).** The research involves noninvasive psychophysical testing in healthy adults and patients with vestibular disorders. The foreseeable risks are limited to motion sickness, transient dizziness, and loss of privacy—each proportionate to the knowledge sought and standard for vestibular research. The proposal identifies these risks and implements proportionate management measures.

**Risk management during testing.** Testing uses a six-degree-of-freedom motion platform, visual displays, and virtual-reality headsets to deliver controlled translations and rotations [8]. These stimuli can provoke motion sickness and, in participants with vestibular pathology, may transiently worsen dizziness. The proposal addresses this at multiple levels. Before testing, participants receive standardized explanations and large, unambiguous practice stimuli to establish comfort and comprehension [8]. During adaptation visits in Aims II and III, the Dizziness Handicap Inventory is administered to monitor symptoms [8]. The protocol includes explicit provisions for reducing exposure duration or rotational speed if discomfort occurs [8], and participants may discontinue at any time [8]. These measures are appropriate: the rotating virtual environment pilot reported that the stimulus became subjectively unobtrusive after one or two minutes [8], and the heading-offset adaptation uses 80 presentations over approximately 20–25 minutes [8], durations that the laboratory's preliminary work has shown to be tolerable.

**Adverse event surveillance.** The proposal's plan to document adverse events with severity and relationship to participation, and to review them through institutional oversight [8], provides ongoing safety surveillance. The chronic-lesion adaptation pilot (n=5) found no systematic worsening on the DHI, with a mean change of −0.8 (range −4 to 2) [8], though this small sample cannot exclude rare adverse responses.

**Informed consent and participant protections.** The ethical framework is appropriate for the populations studied. Participants give informed consent, and adolescents aged 13–17, if enrolled, require parental consent and an age-appropriate assent process [8]. Research participation does not prevent other clinical care—a relevant safeguard for the patient populations in Aim III, who may be receiving concurrent medical management for vestibular pathology. The longitudinal acute-lesion design (IIIA) raises a specific consideration: patients recently experiencing acute vertigo, post-surgical recovery, or gentamicin treatment may find serial testing burdensome. The proposal acknowledges that serial visits may be difficult [8] and allows participants to discontinue. In practice, the DHI monitoring and the right to withdraw provide sufficient protection, and the noninvasive nature of the testing limits the consequences of any transient symptom worsening.

**Data protection.** Data protection follows standard institutional practice. Identifying information is maintained separately from research measurements, with access limited to designated study personnel [8]. Dissemination through scientific publications and presentations is planned, with trial registration before data collection [8]. The proposal does not specify a public data-sharing repository, but this falls within the normal range of practice for psychophysical research and does not raise material ethical concerns. No animal subjects, environmental hazards, or dual-use concerns are identified.

## Management

**Rating: Good (B), confidence high (B).** The project is led by an established investigator with demonstrated expertise in the proposed methods and direct access to the necessary populations and equipment. The management plan is appropriately scaled to the work, though the parallel execution of multiple aims in early years places simultaneous demands on recruitment and personnel that require coordination.

**Leadership and track record.** The principal investigator, Benjamin T. Crane, has published extensively on visual-inertial heading perception using the same laboratory infrastructure, psychophysical methods, and participant populations proposed here—including the coordinate-frame studies (Crane 2015), reliability-based weighting experiments (Crane 2017), temporal-integration work (Rodriguez & Crane 2021), and the recent motion-profile manipulation demonstrating reduced cross-modal bias with inverted visual velocity profiles (Yakouma, Anson & Crane 2025) [3] [4] [5] [7]. The laboratory has also established the adaptation feasibility data and the chronic-lesion pilot results [8]. This track record provides confidence in delivery of the planned work.

**Personnel and resources.** A full-time technician supports recruitment and testing [8], and the laboratory maintains the six-degree-of-freedom hexapod, a second platform for parallel configurations, a custom multi-axis sled, virtual-reality systems, and motion-tracking instrumentation [8]. Clinical access through otolaryngology clinics and a screened institutional participant database provide the recruitment pathways for both healthy controls and patient populations [8]. No additional equipment development or external collaborations are required as dependencies.

**Schedule coordination.** The five-year schedule is logically sequenced but ambitious in its early parallelism. Year 1 simultaneously develops causal-inference methods in healthy participants (IA), begins heading-offset adaptation (IIA), initiates acute unilateral-loss follow-up (IIIA), and starts adaptation in chronic unilateral loss (IIIB) [8]. This places concurrent demands on the technician for participant scheduling across four distinct experiment types, and on clinical recruitment for two patient populations (acute and chronic) with different eligibility criteria and visit schedules. Year 2 adds the parametric causal-inference experiments (IB, IC) and the rotational-adaptation paradigm (IIB) while continuing the longitudinal and chronic-lesion studies. The availability of a second platform for parallel configurations provides some operational flexibility [8], but the schedule does not detail how competing demands would be prioritized if recruitment or data collection for one aim exceeds projections while another lags. The later years (3–5) are appropriately structured for iterative refinement, completion of patient data collection, and integration of results into a unified model [8], with the final year reserved for analysis and dissemination.

**Data management.** Data management follows standard institutional practice: identifying information is maintained separately from research measurements, with access limited to designated study personnel [8]. Dissemination through scientific publications and presentations is anticipated, and trial registration is planned before data collection [8]. The described procedures are consistent with standard practice for psychophysical research of this type.

The management strengths are the PI's demonstrated expertise, the established laboratory capability, and a schedule that allows iterative refinement based on initial results. The primary limitation is the under-specification of coordination for parallel demands in years 1–2, a manageable logistical consideration rather than a threat to the core mechanistic aims, which are within the laboratory's demonstrated capacity. The rating reflects a plan that is well-supported by existing resources and appropriately scaled to the work, with a minor reservation about early-stage coordination.

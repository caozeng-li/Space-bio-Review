# Project review

## Overall assessment

This R01 project, led by PIs Kyle B. Brothers and Aaron Goldenberg, proposes a four-year mixed-methods study to characterize the ethical, legal, and social challenges specific to networked biorepositories and to develop practicable governance recommendations. A networked biorepository is defined as a research collection providing centralized access to biospecimens, genotyping data, or health-related data collected at multiple institutions; networks may use central databases, coordinated policies, or virtual interfaces while specimens remain at local sites [1].

The research object is the network itself—a multi-institutional arrangement for sharing biospecimens and data that creates unique tensions around harmonizing consent, donor control, access, privacy, and oversight across sites with differing policies. The planned work combines a retrospective inventory and up to 100 stakeholder interviews, longitudinal participant observation in five contrasting networks, and a multi-stage normative analysis tested through focus groups and an expert symposium [1]. The five provisional observation cases span mature networks such as eMERGE and the Kentucky Cancer Registry, newer arrangements including the Newborn Screening Translational Research Network and Sage Bionetworks–Synapse, and the in-development Mayo Clinic Biobank [1]. The deliberate inclusion of developing networks and an abandoned networking effort, rather than studying only successful mature organizations, adds comparative value [1].

The project’s main strengths are its novel focus, thoughtful multi-method design, and strong research team. The explicit choice to study networks as the primary unit of analysis is a genuine step beyond the team’s prior national biobank survey, which captured network membership only through a single question and could not characterize network-level governance [1] [2]. The participant observation protocol, with its biweekly reflexive process and multi-site access, is a particularly innovative and well-specified component. The team has a strong track record of relevant prior work, provides matched expertise in ELSI, ethnography, law, and IRB regulation, and has secured formal access to all five proposed observation sites [1].

The main limitations that materially affect the judgement are the deliberate exclusion of specimen donors’ voices, which constrains the normative reach of the recommendations, and a lack of specificity regarding the exact reasoning pathway from empirical findings to normative conclusions. The under-specification of the normative methodology reflects broader challenges in empirical bioethics, where 32 distinct integration methodologies have been catalogued with “little common ground that transcends” fundamental disagreements [3], and where even experienced practitioners describe their integration methods with “hesitance and uncertainty” [4]. This does not undermine the substantial value of the empirical findings themselves, but it limits confidence that the recommendations will carry a transparent and contestable normative warrant.

The central trade-off is between the project’s high scientific value and robust empirical design on one hand, and the limited specification of its normative integration pathway on the other. The proposal promises a thorough and innovative description of how networked biorepositories actually govern ethical challenges—knowledge that is currently absent. This empirical foundation, combined with the team’s proven expertise and strong access, makes a persuasive case that the project will deliver valuable and actionable guidance. The under-specification of the normative methodology is a real but not fatal limitation; the recommendations’ strength will depend on the persuasiveness of the implicit reasoning, but the project’s descriptive and comparative contributions are independently significant. Weighing these factors, the project’s strengths clearly outweigh its limitations. The plan is well-conceived, appropriately ambitious, and promises a meaningful advance in understanding and governing a critical component of precision medicine infrastructure.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**Scientific value: A (confidence: B).** This project addresses a knowledge gap of genuine importance to both ELSI scholarship and the translational research infrastructure that underpins precision medicine. The attainable knowledge is substantial: a systematic characterization of how networked biorepositories differ organizationally, empirically grounded accounts of how governance deliberations actually unfold across diverse configurations, and domain-specific, practicable recommendations for consent, privacy, access, return of results, and oversight.

### A knowledge gap with practical urgency

The proposal correctly identifies that the extensive ELSI literature on single-site biobanks—covering consent models, return of results, participant engagement, and governance—does not translate straightforwardly to networks. When repositories with different consent terms, local IRB interpretations, and governance traditions attempt to share specimens and data, distinctive tensions arise: ethically permissible practices at one site may conflict with the commitments made to donors at another. Kaye (2011) argued that “current governance structures for research are not designed for networks” and remain based on the “one researcher, one project, one jurisdiction” model [5]. Dove, Knoppers and Zawati (2014) proposed an ethics safe harbor precisely to address harmonization across jurisdictions [6]. The Cadigan et al. (2024) paper, which is an output of this funded project, explicitly notes that “little is known about whether and how operations of networking may accomplish” stewardship goals [7]. The project fills this gap by making the network the unit of analysis and studying how different configurations actually resolve—or fail to resolve—these tensions in practice.

### Layers of empirical knowledge

The attainable empirical knowledge encompasses several layers. The inventory (Aim 1a) will provide the first systematic mapping of US networked biorepositories, collecting organizational features—contributing institutions, scale, maturity, locations of specimens and data, governance arrangements, and access rules—that go well beyond the single survey question about network membership in the team’s prior 465-biobank study [1]. The stakeholder interviews (Aim 1b) will capture the range of challenges encountered across networks from the perspective of scientific leaders, operations managers, informaticists, ethics advisers, IRB members, community advisers, and client researchers, with deliberate effort to seek dissenting voices [1]. The longitudinal participant observation (Aim 2), a novel method for this domain, will access the deliberative process itself—what options are considered, how disagreements are resolved, and how organizational context shapes outcomes—rather than only participants’ retrospective accounts. This combination of data types, coded in a shared Framework Analysis framework, enables systematic triangulation between stated policies, reported experiences, and observed deliberations [1].

The inventory’s standalone knowledge contribution is modest—the search cannot claim census-level coverage given inconsistent terminology and reliance on web presence [1]—but this limitation is acceptable because the inventory’s primary function is instrumental: producing the sampling frame for interviews and informing case selection for observation.

### Practical value of the expected outputs

The promised outputs include a characterization of network structures, accounts of ethical and regulatory problem-solving, domain-specific recommendations, and adaptable governance models or best practices [1]. These address a demonstrated governance gap. Kaye (2011) argued that the governance system “does not meet the requirements of the new way of doing research that is based on flows of data and networks” [5], yet practical, empirically grounded guidance for network designers and operators has been absent. The Cadigan et al. (2024) paper found that “networking did not necessarily increase funding, utilization, and sustainability” and that “promoting stewardship through networking requires significant time and effort to build governance models that honor the obligations of each individual biobank to their donors” [7]. Domain-specific recommendations derived from systematic empirical study would fill this gap.

The dissemination plan goes beyond academic publication to include direct engagement with networks through calls and webinars, and partnerships with relevant organizations [1]. The advisory committee embeds representatives from the five observation cases, providing direct channels for translation [1]. The deliberate attention to variation in network configuration—the inventory characterizes contributing institutions, scale, maturity, and governance arrangements precisely so that recommendations are adaptable rather than one-size-fits-all [1]—strengthens the practical guidance.

### Acknowledged scope limitation

A limitation acknowledged in the design is the exclusion of specimen donors from direct participation; community advisory members serve as proxies [1]. Evidence shows systematic divergence between professional stakeholders and research participants on questions such as return of results, where healthcare professionals and IRB members are “less positive and more cautious” than participants and the public [8]. Community advisory boards of 5–10 members have inherent limitations in “representativeness and breadth of responses” [9]. This constraint means the recommendations may not fully capture donor interests, and the normative reach of the guidance is correspondingly qualified. Nonetheless, the project’s primary knowledge contribution—characterizing how networks organize and govern themselves from the perspective of those who build and operate them—remains substantial and fills a gap that no prior study has addressed systematically.

## Innovation

**Innovation: A (confidence: B).** The project makes a genuinely novel contribution by making the network, rather than the individual biobank, the unit of ethical and governance analysis, and by deploying a multi-method design that includes the novel application of longitudinal participant observation to biobank governance.

### The network as a new unit of analysis

The core conceptual innovation is treating networked biorepositories as presenting distinctive ethical and governance challenges that cannot be understood by studying single sites alone. The proposal argues that when repositories with different consent terms, local IRB interpretations, and governance traditions attempt to share specimens and data, tensions arise that are specific to the networking arrangement: “ethically permissible local practices may differ” and “a network must decide how to reconcile those differences without erasing donor commitments or creating unworkable barriers to research” [1]. The prior ELSI literature has extensively addressed single-site biobanking but has not systematically studied how networks resolve conflicts among local policies. The team’s own prior work, the Henderson et al. (2013) national survey of 465 US biobanks, found that 16% described themselves as part of a network [2], but that survey could only characterize individual biobank organizations [1]. The Cadigan et al. (2024) paper confirmed that “little is known about whether and how operations of networking may accomplish” stewardship goals [7].

Two further design choices add to the project’s originality. First is the deliberate inclusion of developing networks and an abandoned networking effort, rather than studying only successful mature organizations [1]. Existing biobank literature has largely described established operations; comparing networks at different maturity stages opens the possibility of understanding why some governance approaches succeed and others fail, which no prior study has attempted systematically. Second is the commitment to studying the network as a whole—capturing perspectives from multiple stakeholder roles across the network—rather than relying on single-informant accounts from one member site.

### Methodological advance: longitudinal participant observation in a new domain

The methodological innovation lies in the application of longitudinal participant observation to biobank network governance. The proposal aims to capture “what challenges arise in everyday efforts to build and sustain networks, how stakeholders identify and deliberate about them, and why different organizations adopt different solutions,” observing “both the deliberative process and resulting practices” [1]. Prior work, including by this team, has relied on retrospective interviews and surveys: the Cadigan et al. (2024) paper used interviews with 38 representatives of 31 networked biobanks [7]; the McGuire et al. (2011) eMERGE study reconstructed data-sharing challenges from “the perspective of multiple stakeholders” [10]. Observation adds the ability to capture deliberations as they unfold—which options were considered, what arguments carried weight, how disagreements were resolved, and how organizational context shaped outcomes—before events are filtered through participants’ recall and post-hoc rationalization.

The biweekly reflexive team process, where investigators discuss how their presence may have influenced deliberation, with “significant points incorporated into field notes before coding” [1], represents thoughtful engagement with ethnographic method. The coded integration of field notes alongside interview transcripts and primary documents in a shared ATLAS.ti framework [1] enables systematic triangulation across data types. No prior study in the biobanking ELSI literature has applied this method.

The project draws on a validated search methodology from the team’s prior national biobank study, which used PubMed, NIH RePORTER, Google, CTSA sites, AAMC member websites, and colleague referrals [2] [11], but adapts it to a new unit of analysis. The inventory will collect network-level organizational features—scale, maturity, locations of specimens and data, governance arrangements—rather than individual biobank characteristics [1]. This shift from biobank-level to network-level data collection is incremental in method but significant in analytic focus.

While the project is appropriately characterized as an incremental rather than revolutionary advance—it extends established qualitative and survey methods to a new object of study—its combination of network-level focus, multi-method design including observation, attention to organizational variation and developmental stage, and structured empirical-to-normative pathway represents a genuine and well-justified contribution beyond the existing ELSI literature.

## Rigor

**Rigor: B (confidence: B).** The mixed-methods design is well-constructed for the project’s exploratory, descriptive, and normative aims, and each component supports its intended inferences appropriately. The principal limitations are the under-specified empirical-to-normative reasoning pathway and the deliberate exclusion of specimen donors, which constrains the normative reach of the recommendations without undermining the substantial value of the empirical findings.

### Coherent architecture with systematic triangulation

The overall architecture is coherent: a retrospective inventory (Aim 1a) produces the sampling frame for stakeholder interviews and case selection; interviews and document analysis (Aim 1b) characterize the range of challenges encountered across diverse networks; longitudinal participant observation (Aim 2) captures governance deliberation as it unfolds in five contrasting cases; and a multi-stage normative analysis (Aim 3) translates empirical findings into domain-specific recommendations, tested through focus groups and an expert symposium. The use of a shared ATLAS.ti project with common codes across all three aims enables systematic triangulation between stated policies, reported experiences, and observed deliberations [1].

### Interview design: breadth, deliberation capture, and analytical rigor

The interview design is methodologically sound for its purpose. The sampling strategy combines a systematic inventory, purposive selection across eight stakeholder role categories, snowball sampling, and deliberate seeking of “less powerful or dissenting stakeholders to reduce reliance on leadership-selected accounts” [1]. The ceiling of approximately five interviewees per network, yielding up to 100 interviews across years 1–3, is appropriate for characterizing breadth across diverse configurations. The stakeholder categories are comprehensive: scientific and administrative leaders, operations managers, informaticists, ethics advisers, IRB members, community advisers, donor-study investigators or coordinators, and client researchers [1].

Framework Analysis is well-matched to the project’s comparative, policy-oriented aims. It is designed for studies with pre-identified thematic domains, explicit cross-case comparisons, and the goal of producing actionable findings [1]. The two-coder independent coding design with a 90% agreement threshold, followed by discussion, revision, and then distribution of remaining interviews, provides a reasonable approach to intercoder reliability. The initial thematic framework draws on both previous research and emergent themes from the first ten interviews [1], combining deductive and inductive elements appropriately. The involvement of both lead sites (Louisville and Case Western) in coding provides cross-institutional checks on interpretation.

### Observation protocol: reflexivity and context

The participant observation protocol has specific strengths that enhance rigor. The assignment of one investigator and one research coordinator as primary contacts for each case provides continuity and relationship depth [1]. The biweekly team reflection process—in which investigators discuss observations and reflect on how their presence may have influenced deliberation, with significant points incorporated into field notes before coding [1]—is a methodologically sound response to the observer effect, drawn from the Emerson, Fretz, and Shaw ethnographic tradition cited in the proposal’s references [1]. The range of observation settings (consortium meetings, advisory groups, conference calls, community and ethics advisory boards, and IRB meetings) is appropriate to the governance domains under study [1]. The shared coding framework enables the comparison of observational data with interview accounts and policy documents.

The five provisional cases provide systematic variation along maturity (mature, new, in development), configuration (study-based pooling, state registry-linked tissue, state-held newborn screening samples, cloud-based sharing, locally held specimens with planned cloud repository), and governance type (varying presence of community and ethics advisory boards, IRB observation) [1]. The proposal includes an appropriate contingency: findings from Aim 1 may justify replacing or adding cases within time and budget [1].

### Two limitations affecting the normative reach

**Exclusion of specimen donors.** The deliberate exclusion of donors—“direct investigation of donors’ experiences is reserved for subsequent work” [1]—constrains the normative reach of the recommendations. Evidence shows systematic divergence between professional stakeholders and research participants on key ethical questions: healthcare professionals and IRB members are “less positive and more cautious about returning results” than participants and the public [8], and community advisory boards of 5–10 members have inherent limitations in “representativeness and breadth of responses” [9]. Community advisory members are not reliable proxies for donor perspectives. The focus groups in Aim 3 recruit stakeholders with “practical experience in that domain” [1], which again privileges professional perspectives. The recommendations’ attention to donor interests therefore relies on inference rather than direct evidence.

**Under-specified normative integration pathway.** The empirical-to-normative reasoning pathway is under-specified. The proposal describes the components—coded empirical findings, a normative core examining “conceptual and legal literature,” evaluation of candidate solutions according to “compatibility with ethical/legal standards, costs and practical trade-offs, and usefulness across different network configurations” [1]—but does not commit to a specific integration methodology. Davies et al. (2015) catalogued 32 distinct approaches [3], and Wangmo et al. (2023) found that even experienced practitioners describe their methods with “hesitance and uncertainty,” characterizing the process as a “magic box” [4]. The proposal’s consultative approach—where stakeholders feed into analysis but the research team retains normative responsibility—is one recognized methodological orientation [3], but without specifying how disagreements within the normative core will be resolved or what kind of justification will support the recommendations, the warrant for the normative conclusions remains opaque. This under-specification reflects a genuine challenge in empirical bioethics rather than a failure of design, and it does not undermine the value of the empirical findings themselves, but it does limit confidence in the transparency and contestability of the resulting recommendations.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility: B (confidence: B).** The project is broadly feasible, with secured access to all five observation sites, a credible overlapping schedule, and a team whose prior large-scale survey and interview experience supports confidence in delivering the core empirical work. The main risks—a compressed final-year schedule and under-specified focus group recruitment—are manageable rather than fatal.

### Schedule: sound phasing with one compressed period

The four-year schedule in Table 7 [1] is a deliberately overlapping design with three recognizable phases: foundation (Q1–Q2: inventory search, final case selection), intensive data collection (Q3–Q10: interviews, observation, document collection, with coding from Q7), and validation and output (Q11–Q16: focus groups, symposium, finalization, dissemination). The overlap of interviews (Q3–Q10) with observation (Q3–Q14) is manageable because each observation site is assigned a dedicated dyad of one investigator and one research coordinator [1], while interviews are distributed across all co-investigators [1]. The biweekly team reflection sessions (Q3–Q14) serve as the integrating mechanism, ensuring that observations inform interview protocols and vice versa.

The credible concern is the Q11–Q16 period. Focus groups (Q11–Q14), the expert symposium (Q13–Q14), and finalization and dissemination (Q13–Q16) are compressed into the final year, with the symposium occupying the same two quarters as the focus groups. The proposal acknowledges that the team will meet “immediately afterward” the symposium to “synthesize feedback and finalize recommendations” [1]. If the symposium identifies fundamental problems with cross-domain coherence—which is precisely its purpose—the team has only Q15–Q16 to address them. However, the symposium is positioned as a validation and refinement step, not a primary consensus-development exercise; the focus groups have already tested domain-specific recommendations. The risk is that major revisions cannot be accommodated, not that the entire output is jeopardized.

### Secured access and recruitment capacity

Access to the five observation sites is the strongest feasibility element. Network leaders have “agreed to observation of relevant activities” [1], and the advisory committee formalizes these relationships with named representatives from each case: Janet Olson (Mayo), Rex Chisholm (eMERGE), Michael Watson (NBSTRN), Thomas Tucker (Kentucky Cancer Registry), and Megan Dorr (Sage Bionetworks) [1]. The observation settings table shows significant variation in what can be observed—Mayo offers the most comprehensive access including IRB meetings, while Kentucky Cancer Registry lacks advisory and community boards [1]—but this variation is scientifically valuable for comparing ethical deliberation across different governance structures.

Interview recruitment is plausible given the team’s track record. The prior national biobank survey achieved a 72% response rate (456 of 636 biobanks) for a 30-minute online survey, using persistent email and telephone follow-up [2]. The 2015 IRB study surveyed administrative directors at 60 CTSA institutions [12]. However, these were surveys, not 60-minute interviews, and the proposal notes that interviews will be “conducted without participant incentives” [1]. The jump from survey participation to interview participation—longer time commitment, more personal exposure—is significant but not insurmountable given the team’s established relationships in the biobanking community.

Focus group participation is the least well-supported dependency. Up to ten sessions, each devoted to one domain report, require purposive recruitment of “stakeholders with practical experience in that domain” [1]. The proposal does not specify target group size, the total number of participants needed, or the concrete recruitment mechanism. If each focus group requires 6–10 participants, the total recruitment target is 60–100 additional professional stakeholders, approaching the scale of the interview sample itself. It is reasonable to infer that participants will be drawn partly from the interview sample and partly from the advisory committee’s professional networks, but the proposal provides no evidence that domain-specific experts will commit to an additional session requiring advance reading of a draft report and group discussion. This dependency does not threaten the project’s core empirical contribution, which rests primarily on the inventory, interviews, and observation, but it could reduce the robustness of the recommendation-testing process.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety: A (confidence: B).** The ethics and data protection plan is well-developed, proportionate to the risks involved, and addresses the specific complexities raised by studying professional stakeholders within organizational settings.

### Appropriate risk framing

The study population consists of adult professionals and stakeholders: scientific and administrative leaders, operations managers, informaticists, ethics advisers, IRB members, community advisers, donor-study investigators, and client researchers [1]. This is a significant risk-limiting feature. Unlike research involving patients or specimen donors, where decisions about return of results, privacy, or consent directly implicate personal health interests, the professional participants are speaking about organizational policies and practices in their institutional roles. The risks are therefore primarily reputational and professional rather than clinical or psychological.

### Differentiated consent and the observation challenge

The consent approach is appropriately differentiated by activity. For interviews and focus groups, participants will receive information, opportunities for questions, and consent procedures, with the right to decline questions, stop recording, or withdraw [1]. The 60-minute interview format, with open-ended prompts and nondirective follow-up [1], allows participants to control the depth and direction of their disclosures. The absence of participant incentives [1] reduces the ethical complication of undue inducement for professional participants who may feel institutional pressure to cooperate.

The observation component presents the most complex ethical challenge, and the proposal addresses it thoughtfully. Observation occurs in dynamic group settings—consortium meetings, advisory groups, conference calls, governance bodies [1]—where participants change over time and individual consent from every transient attendee may not be practicable. The proposal commits to transparency, repeated reminders about the researchers’ presence and purpose, and appropriate authorization through coordination with network leadership and the study IRB [1]. This is a defensible approach that balances the practical constraints of organizational ethnography with the ethical obligation to avoid covert observation. The biweekly reflexive team process, in which investigators discuss “how their presence may have influenced deliberation” [1], adds a further safeguard by making the observer effect visible and subject to correction.

### Data protection and mitigation of power imbalances

Data protection measures are well-specified and appropriate. Transcripts and reports will use study identifiers or pseudonyms. Network names will generally be withheld in publications unless permission is obtained [1]. This matters because the small number of US networked biorepositories, and the distinctive configurations described in the proposal [1], could make networks identifiable even without naming them. The proposal acknowledges the risk of “indirect identification of individuals or networks” [1] and addresses it through withholding network names as the default. Access to recordings, transcripts, and linkage information is restricted to the research team; paper records are locked and computer files password-protected. Original recordings will be destroyed once transcription and coding are complete [1].

The focus group design incorporates an explicit ethical consideration: groups will be “composed with attention to power differences so that participants can speak freely” [1]. This is important because focus group participants may include individuals in hierarchical relationships—for example, a junior operations manager and a senior scientific director from the same network—and discussion of ethical challenges, governance failures, or tensions between local and network priorities could carry professional risk if attributed to identifiable individuals. Mitigating power imbalances in group composition is a concrete step beyond generic confidentiality assurances.

One implementation detail merits attention. The proposal does not specify how transparency and reminders will be operationalized in observation settings—for example, whether a standard verbal announcement will be made at the start of each observed meeting, whether written information will be provided to regular attendees, or how new participants joining an ongoing observed group will be informed. This is a matter of implementation detail rather than a design flaw; the commitment to transparency and repeated reminders provides a sufficient ethical framework, and the operational specifics can reasonably be developed with the study IRB.

## Management

**Management: A (confidence: B).** The project is organized around a well-designed co-PI structure with clear division of methodological and substantive leadership, supported by co-investigator expertise precisely matched to the demands of the work. The advisory committee embeds representatives from all five observation sites, formalizing access and providing both practical and theoretical oversight.

### Leadership structure and matched expertise

The co-PI structure divides operational leadership along methodological lines: Goldenberg leads the retrospective empirical components (inventory, interviews, focus groups), and Brothers leads the prospective and normative components (primary documents, observation, policy development) [1]. This creates clear accountability for each major work stream while maintaining integration through shared oversight and the biweekly team reflection process that runs across all three years of data collection.

The co-investigator expertise is well-matched to the project’s requirements. Jean Cadigan contributes ethnographic methods directly relevant to the participant observation in Aim 2, and brings experience from the prior 465-biobank inventory [1]. Richard Sharp contributes normative analysis and biobank experience. Susanne Rivera brings regulatory and IRB expertise, essential for a project examining how networks navigate IRB requirements across sites. Mark Rothstein contributes legal and privacy analysis. Heather Harrell takes primary responsibility for document analysis under Brothers’ lead [1].

The normative core includes “philosophical and legal/regulatory specialists and contributes throughout instrument development and interpretation” [1]. This integrated involvement of the normative team throughout instrument design, fieldwork, and interpretation is a structural strength that distinguishes the project from approaches where ethical analysis is appended after data collection is complete. It ensures that the conceptual framework informs data collection from the start.

### Advisory committee as a dual-purpose asset

The advisory committee (Table 6, [1]) serves both governance and practical functions. It embeds representatives from all five provisional observation cases: Janet Olson (Mayo Clinic Biobank), Rex Chisholm (Northwestern, eMERGE), Michael Watson (NBSTRN), Thomas Tucker (University of Kentucky, Kentucky Cancer Registry), and Megan Dorr (Sage Bionetworks). This creates formal governance relationships that complement the operational access agreements described in the observation plan [1]. Gail Henderson and Eric Juengst provide ELSI and philosophical research ethics expertise, while Jonathan Haines adds biorepository and genomic research perspective. The committee thus combines theoretical oversight with practical network representation, providing both scholarly guidance and a direct channel for translating findings into practice.

### A minor gap: resolving normative disagreements

One limitation in the management structure is that the proposal does not specify the decision-making process for resolving disagreements within the normative core. The integration approach involves “team deliberation” to “integrate the reports” [1], but when philosophical, legal, and regulatory specialists reach different conclusions about what a recommendation should be—for example, on the permissibility of broad consent for secondary data sharing or the threshold for returning incidental findings—the mechanism for resolution is unspecified. This is not unusual in interdisciplinary bioethics work, and the team’s prior collaborative history on the 465-biobank study and the 60-CTSA IRB survey provides a reasonable foundation for effective working relationships. Nonetheless, a more explicit account of how disciplinary disagreements will be adjudicated—whether through a designated decision-maker, majority voting, or consensus-seeking procedures—would strengthen confidence in the management of this component.

# Comprehensive Proposal Review

## Overall Assessment

This proposal addresses a genuinely important gap in vestibular science: understanding how the brain combines visual and inertial cues to perceive self-motion, and how this process breaks down after peripheral vestibular damage. The clinical motivation is credible and well-supported—dizziness and vertigo affect a large population, and clinical vestibular assessment has historically prioritized reflex measures that correlate poorly with perceptual symptoms. The project aims to produce the first systematic, full-horizontal-plane characterization of causal inference between visual and inertial heading signals, including both explicit unity judgments and implicit cross-modal biases. The goals extend to testing perceptual adaptation methods in healthy participants and in individuals with vestibular loss, with a longitudinal study tracking perceptual recovery after acute unilateral lesions.

The proposal demonstrates underlying feasibility through concrete pilot data spanning all three aims, an existing instrument suite, and a plausible recruitment base. The pilot data already show that inertial heading estimates are pulled toward a discrepant visual heading even when participants report the stimuli do not share a common cause—a dissociation that motivates the systematic investigation and represents a genuine empirical advance. The separation of explicit common-cause judgment from implicit cross-modal influence within the same stimulus set, measured across the full 360° of horizontal heading, is the most innovative feature of the work.

The critical threats to the project's scientific contribution lie in several intersecting methodological and feasibility limitations that could undermine the inferences drawn from specific aims. The block separation of visual and inertial reports, while pragmatically justified, requires explicit handling of order effects and criterion shifts to avoid spurious dissociations. The narrow motion parameters used for the initial causal-inference mapping (a single two-second translation profile) mean that the generalizability of the dissociation to other dynamics is deferred rather than established. The clinical aims are ambitious relative to the anticipated sample sizes, with longitudinal attrition in the acute-lesion cohort representing a critical feasibility risk for which no contingency plan is described. The rotating virtual-environment adaptation paradigm (Experiment IIB) introduces substantial unquantified variability through voluntary head-movement behavior, threatening reproducibility. Finally, safety monitoring and consent procedures are insufficiently specified for the intensive and prolonged exposure protocols, particularly for participants with acute vestibular loss who are vulnerable to provocation of their primary symptoms.

These issues do not render the project without merit—the core causal-inference characterization in healthy participants (Aim I) is well-designed and likely to yield valuable descriptive and methodological contributions regardless of the clinical outcomes. However, the ambition to extend findings to clinical populations and to test durable adaptation effects depends on premises that the current plan does not adequately support. The proposal would benefit from a more tightly scoped set of clinical aims, explicit contingency plans for recruitment and attrition, and substantive elaboration of safety procedures. A revised plan that addresses these foundational concerns could provide a supportable route to the stated goals.

## Detailed Assessment by Criterion

### Scientific Value

**Rating: B (good)**
**Confidence: B (high)**

The project addresses a well-motivated question with both basic-science and translational significance. The attainable knowledge contributions are substantial and clearly specified across descriptive, conceptual, methodological, clinical-mechanistic, and practical dimensions. The production of the first systematic full-horizontal-plane characterization of causal inference between visual and inertial heading signals, with independent measurement of explicit unity judgments and implicit cross-modal biases, would constitute a valuable empirical and methodological resource. The longitudinal study of acute unilateral lesions has the potential to dissect the origin of persistent heading errors in a way that current clinical literature has not resolved.

The scientific value is tempered by two considerations. First, the clinical adaptation component (Aim IIIB) remains at an early feasibility stage, with pilot data showing single-session effects and some retention in only four of five participants, but no evidence for durable correction. The proposal treats this appropriately as a question to be investigated, and the small DHI changes in the pilot do correctly not form the basis of a functional benefit claim. The clinical payoff is downstream, and the scientific value of testing whether sustained correction is possible remains real even if the immediate results are modest. Second, the generalizability of the core causal-inference findings depends on the motion parameters used. The initial mapping (Experiment IA) is performed under a single two-second translation profile; the investigation of parameter dependence is deferred to subsequent experiments. This is a legitimate scientific sequence, but it means the value of the IA findings is conditional on the later parameter investigations being completed as planned.

These are limitations of scope and sequence rather than of the question's importance. The descriptive and methodological contributions of Aim I alone, combined with the mechanistic hypotheses tested in Aim IIIA, justify a "good" scientific value rating.

### Innovation

**Rating: B (good)**
**Confidence: B (high)**

The proposal advances beyond relevant prior work in several respects, though some elements build incrementally on established paradigms. The most innovative feature is the systematic separation of explicit common-cause judgment from implicit cross-modal influence within the same stimulus set, measured across the full 360° of horizontal heading. The pilot data demonstrating that the discrepancy range for cross-modal influence exceeds that for explicit unity perception is not simply a replication of visual-vestibular integration—it targets the structure of the causal-inference process itself. The expansion beyond the straight-ahead constraint, with full-circle sampling, is a genuine methodological and conceptual innovation that can reveal directional dependencies invisible to conventional designs.

The application of perceptual adaptation paradigms to heading bias correction in vestibular patients is novel, and the explicit comparison of two adaptation methods (sustained visual-inertial offset and rotating virtual environment) represents a meaningful increment. The acute longitudinal design that compares heading error evolution with sway asymmetry and rotational bias is innovative in its explicit attempt to distinguish otolith-mediated from canal-mediated contributions.

Limitations on innovation are that reliability-based cue integration is a mature theoretical framework, the psychophysical methods are standard, and the general concept of perceptual adaptation to systematic sensory discrepancies is established. The proposal does not articulate how its causal-inference measurements would adjudicate between competing computational models (e.g., Bayesian causal inference with a fixed or malleable prior on common cause, versus continuous coupling models), which limits the conceptual innovation from being fully realized. The rotating virtual environment method extends the pilot investigator's own prior vection work, making it a direct extension rather than a departure. The innovation is in the scope, measurement design, and clinical translation rather than in proposing a fundamentally new theoretical mechanism. That is appropriate for a project at this characterization stage, and the empirical and methodological advances are sufficient to warrant a "good" rating.

### Rigor

**Rating: C (moderate)**
**Confidence: B (high)**

The proposal's core strength is its commitment to dissociating explicit common-cause judgments from cross-modal influence, and the preliminary data demonstrate that this dissociation is empirically observable. The apparatus provides controlled, repeatable stimulation, and the statistical framework (cumulative Gaussian fitting with Monte Carlo maximum-likelihood procedures, resampling-based confidence intervals, individual-level inference) is explicit and appropriate. The sample-size justifications are powered for the primary comparisons, which is feasible for the basic psychophysical aims.

Several methodological limitations affect the rigor of specific inferences:

1. **Block separation of visual and inertial reports:** Because visual and inertial estimates are obtained in separate blocks, the direct comparison between percepts on the same trial is inferred rather than observed. For the primary inference—that cross-modal influence outlasts explicit unity perception—this separation is not fatal, because the influence is measured as a within-modality shift. However, the design requires that block-level order effects, fatigue, or criterion shifts do not produce spurious dissociations. The analysis strategy can detect instability through repeated blocks, but the text does not describe how potential order effects will be explicitly modelled. For a proposal that rests on a dissociation, this is a methodological gap.

2. **Narrow motion parameters for the initial causal-inference mapping:** Experiment IA's dissociation is established under a single two-second translation with a single motion profile. The generalizability of the dissociation to other motion dynamics is deferred to Experiment IB, which is a reasonable scientific sequence. However, the proposal risks overattributing the IA results to causal inference generally without acknowledging that the parameter space is initially narrow.

3. **Underpowered for interaction tests in parameter manipulations:** Experiment IB systematically manipulates temporal offset, duration, acceleration, and visual field size, but the stated sample size of approximately 10 participants per condition will likely be insufficient to test interactions among these parameters. The proposal does not specify whether parameter manipulations will be tested between-subjects, within-subjects, or in a mixed design, which has implications for power, carryover, and feasibility. If the wider-field (200°) equipment is contingent and not secured, a key prediction cannot be tested.

4. **Clinical heterogeneity and statistical power:** The small sample sizes in Experiments IC (~10), IIIA (8 completers after attrition), and the stated ambition to compare etiologies exceed the feasible inferential scope. Subgroup analyses are unlikely to be informative, and the proposal should be clearer that the primary inference is at the group level. The mismatch between stated ambition and feasible scope affects the rigor of the clinical-mechanistic claims.

5. **Unstandardized movement during rotation adaptation:** Experiment IIB's dependence on voluntary head movement introduces substantial variability. The text mentions that "minimum movement requirements or adjusted stimulus parameters can standardize exposure" but does not specify the requirements or how adherence will be monitored. Individual differences in effective stimulus dose threaten reproducibility.

6. **Crossover design without explicit carryover controls:** Experiment IIIB's sequencing of adaptation methods by enrollment cohort, rather than formal randomization, introduces potential temporal confounds that the analysis plan does not address.

7. **Incomplete treatment of multiplicity:** The use of p ≤ .01 as a criterion is stated but not integrated into a broader strategy for inference across the project's many hypothesis tests.

These limitations collectively affect the strength of the inferences that can be drawn from the clinical aims and the broader generalizability claims. The basic psychophysical characterization in healthy participants (Aim I core) is more robust. A "moderate" rating reflects the uneven rigor: strong in the central dissociation logic and pilot-supported measurements, but weakened by underaddressed design dependencies in the adaptation and clinical components that affect the project's main goals.

### Feasibility

**Rating: C (moderate)**
**Confidence: B (high)**

The proposal demonstrates underlying feasibility through concrete pilot data from all three aims, an existing and operational instrument suite, and a plausible recruitment base. The PI's laboratory has evidently conducted the core tasks, and the schedule is logically structured with overlapping aims that make efficient use of shared equipment.

The principal threats to feasibility are:

1. **Recruitment and retention of vestibular patients:** The acute longitudinal study (Aim IIIA) requires 16 participants with 50% anticipated attrition, yielding an effective eight completers. The proposal notes the difficulty of serial visits for patients travelling long distances but does not describe active mitigation strategies. If attrition exceeds 50%, the study could end with fewer than the eight required for the power calculation. The chronic-lesion adaptation study targets 20+ participants and prefers larger baseline biases (>10°), narrowing the eligible pool. The pilot data show five chronic participants were tested, but scaling to 20+ within five years while running parallel healthy-control experiments depends on clinic throughput not quantified in the proposal. No contingency plans for lower enrollment or higher attrition are described.

2. **Equipment dependency for wide-field conditions:** The availability of wider visual fields (up to 200°) is explicitly described as "contingent on equipment availability and results." This means a key prediction of Experiment IB—that larger fields should increase visual influence—cannot be guaranteed with the current resources.

3. **Personnel workload and coordination:** The plan relies on a single full-time technician to manage recruitment, scheduling, and testing for healthy controls, acute serial follow-ups, and chronic repeated-session adaptation protocols concurrently. The scaling from pilot demonstrations to full production over five years adds a coordination burden that is not explicitly addressed. The proposal does not describe tools (database, scheduling system) to prevent conflicts or data loss.

4. **Analytic expertise:** The statistical methods are described but the person responsible for executing advanced modeling (including the unified causal-inference model planned for Year 5) is not named. This creates uncertainty about whether the team includes the necessary computational expertise.

These dependencies do not render the project infeasible, but they introduce material risk to the timely completion of the acute-cohort and wide-field components, and to the depth of the final modeling integration. The core causal-inference, healthy-participant adaptation, and chronic-lesion studies are more robustly supported. A "moderate" rating reflects the gap between the demonstrated pilot-level feasibility and the underaddressed risks in scaling to the full project scope, particularly for the clinical aims where recruitment and retention uncertainties are critical.

### Ethics and Safety

**Rating: D (weak)**
**Confidence: C (moderate)**

The proposal identifies the principal foreseeable risks—motion sickness, worsening dizziness, and loss of privacy—and includes some monitoring (DHI administration during adaptation visits, adverse-event documentation) and parameter adjustments (reduced exposure duration, rotational speed). The explicit acknowledgment of risks and the right to discontinue are appropriate starting points. The plan to adapt toward the direction that reduces baseline bias in the chronic-lesion adaptation study (IIIB) is a sensible precaution against inducing novel errors.

Critical gaps remain that affect the ethical appropriateness of the described activities:

1. **Absence of real-time symptom monitoring and explicit stopping rules for Aims I and II:** The adaptation sequence of 80 offset presentations over 20–25 minutes in Experiment IIA is a prolonged exposure that could provoke significant discomfort, yet no mid-session symptom check or stopping rule is described beyond the general right to discontinue. The rotating virtual-environment paradigm (IIB) mentions that rotation became unobtrusive in piloting but does not specify how motion sickness will be monitored in real time or what threshold would trigger session termination. DHI captures longer-term perceived handicap and is not a sufficient real-time safety instrument for acute symptom provocation.

2. **Heightened risk in acute vestibular-loss participants:** The longitudinal study (IIIA) proposes weekly testing during the first month after acute unilateral loss, a period when spontaneous vertigo, nausea, and postural instability are common. The proposal does not address the possibility that repeated motion-platform testing in the acute phase may exacerbate symptoms or delay natural compensation. The adaptation protocol for chronic lesions (IIIB) specifies stopping when bias disappears or reverses, but this provides a clinical endpoint only after additional exposure has occurred; it does not specify prospectively defined individual stopping criteria for intolerable symptoms unrelated to bias magnitude.

3. **Informed consent for clinical populations:** The consent process does not explicitly address how the distinction between research and clinical care will be communicated to patients recruited from otolaryngology clinics. These participants might conflate experimental heading-adaptation procedures with therapeutic intervention, a recognized ethical consideration in translational studies that recruit clinical populations for procedures that resemble rehabilitation but lack evidence of clinical benefit.

4. **Data management:** The summary-level description (identifying information maintained separately, access limited to designated personnel) satisfies basic confidentiality requirements but does not specify technical or administrative controls (encryption, access logging, data-use agreements).

These gaps are not necessarily evidence that the research would be unethical if conducted with appropriate oversight, but the supplied text alone does not provide adequate evidence that the safety and consent procedures are proportionate to the described activities. The absence of real-time monitoring and explicit stopping rules for prolonged provocative exposure, combined with heightened vulnerability in the acute patient population, constitutes a material ethical weakness in the current plan. A "weak" rating reflects that the safety framework requires substantive elaboration before the proposed activities can be considered ethically proportionate.

### Management

**Rating: C (moderate)**
**Confidence: C (moderate)**

The proposal demonstrates that the PI's laboratory has the technical capability to execute the core experimental paradigms, as evidenced by pilot data across all three aims. The existing instrument suite matches the needs of the experiments, and the schedule presents a logically structured five-year sequence with overlapping aims that makes efficient use of shared equipment and participant overlap.

The management plan has several underspecified elements:

1. **Personnel roles and workload:** The proposal identifies the PI and a single full-time technician but does not describe the division of responsibilities in sufficient detail to assess whether one technician can manage recruitment, scheduling, and testing for healthy controls, acute serial follow-ups, and chronic repeated-session protocols concurrently. The synchronization of acute lesion follow-up (weekly, then monthly visits) with the chronic adaptation protocol (weekly sessions with retention tracking) implies a demanding concurrent workload. The proposal does not describe coordination tools (database, scheduling system) to manage this complexity.

2. **Analytic expertise:** The sophisticated statistical modeling described (Monte Carlo methods, cumulative Gaussian fitting, resampling, unified causal-inference model) requires specialized knowledge. The proposal does not name who will execute these analyses, creating uncertainty about whether the team includes the necessary computational expertise or whether this expertise will need to be developed during the project.

3. **Contingency planning:** The proposal names critical risks—recruitment challenges, attrition in the acute cohort, contingent equipment—but does not describe active mitigation strategies. The absence of contingency plans for lower-than-expected enrollment, higher-than-anticipated attrition, or unavailability of the wide-field display means that the project lacks documented resilience to these identified threats.

4. **Participant tracking across experiments:** The plan to reuse participants across experiments reduces recruitment burden but introduces a scheduling dependency: participants in Aims IA, IIA, and IIB may need to be tracked to avoid carryover or fatigue effects. No explicit log or washout protocol is described.

These underspecifications do not indicate incapacity—the pilot data demonstrate that the work can be done at the individual-experiment level. However, the scaling to full production over five years with multiple concurrent protocols introduces a coordination burden that the current management description does not adequately address. A "moderate" rating reflects that the technical capability is established but the plan for managing the operational complexity of the full project scope is incomplete.

## Recommendation: Current proposal insufficient for support (revise)

The project pursues a worthwhile direction with a well-motivated scientific question, concrete pilot data demonstrating the feasibility of core methods, and the potential to produce valuable descriptive and methodological contributions. However, substantive problems in the current plan affect its main goals. The ethical and safety framework is insufficient for the prolonged and intensive exposure protocols described, particularly for participants with acute vestibular loss. The clinical aims are ambitious relative to the anticipated sample sizes, with no contingency plans for recruitment and attrition failures that could prevent the project from reaching its powered minimums. The rotating virtual-environment paradigm introduces unquantified variability through unstandardized voluntary movement, threatening the reproducibility of a key adaptation method. The block-separated measurement design requires explicit handling of order effects that the current analysis plan does not provide. These limitations are not individually fatal, but collectively they create unacceptable uncertainty about whether the clinical and adaptation aims—which are central to the project's translational contribution—can be supported by the current plan.

The core causal-inference characterization in healthy participants (Aim I) is the strongest component and could likely be supported with modest revisions to the analysis plan and safety procedures. The proposal would benefit from a tighter scope that focuses on establishing the basic dissociation and parameter dependence in healthy populations, with the clinical aims restructured as smaller-scale feasibility studies with explicit stopping rules, real-time symptom monitoring, and contingency plans for recruitment shortfalls. A revised proposal that addresses the identified ethical gaps, provides realistic clinical sample-size targets with documented mitigation strategies, and specifies the standardization procedures for the adaptation paradigms could provide a supportable route to the stated goals.

**Comprehensive Review**

**Proposal Synopsis and Overall Assessment**

This proposal targets a clinically significant and timely question: how does the presence of multiple chronic conditions (multimorbidity) and associated functional and pulmonary impairment alter the net benefit of low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) lung cancer screening in routine care? The work is motivated by the well-recognized gap between the efficacy demonstrated in the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST)—which enrolled a relatively healthy cohort—and the real-world application of screening in an older population with a higher burden of comorbidity and competing mortality. The project’s core attainable contribution is a quantitative characterization of screening outcomes across levels of multimorbidity, using a combination of observational data from three diverse U.S. health systems and long-term projections from an established microsimulation model. This is a descriptive and prognostic contribution with direct implications for personalizing shared decision-making and refining clinical guidelines.

The proposal’s architecture is logical: Aims 1 and 2 assemble a multi-site cohort to describe screening performance and short- to medium-term outcomes stratified by comorbidity, functional limitation, and pulmonary function; Aim 3 seeks to integrate these real-world estimates into the CISNET UM-LCS simulation model to project long-term life-year gains and losses and identify subgroups where the net benefit of screening is uncertain or potentially unfavorable. The assembled team has documented expertise in the relevant clinical, epidemiological, and modeling domains, and the data infrastructure at the lead sites is substantial.

However, the proposal, as described, contains material unresolved issues that affect the strength of its central, policy-facing promise. The most critical limitation is an under-specified pathway from the observational cohort results to the parameterization of the microsimulation model in Aim 3. The proposal does not describe the specific method by which the cohort-derived estimates—many of which will be confounded by the clinical selection processes that determine who receives screening, follow-up, and treatment—will be translated into the causal parameters required for simulation of counterfactual screening strategies. This inferential gap, combined with systematic missingness of a key predictor (functional limitations) at one of the three sites and an unaddressed dependency on unvalidated natural language processing (NLP) tools for primary outcome ascertainment, introduces significant uncertainty about the validity of the proposed individualized screening thresholds. The study will generate a valuable description of screening patterns and outcomes across comorbidity levels, but the current plan provides insufficient methodological assurance that the simulated long-term benefit–harm thresholds will be robust enough to support the clinical decision-making claims that are the project’s distinguishing contribution.

The overall recommendation is that the proposal presents a worthwhile direction with a supportable core descriptive scope, but the current plan for its most ambitious aim is insufficiently specified. A revised plan that resolves the critical analytical pathway for Aim 3 and addresses the key measurement dependencies would substantially strengthen the project’s potential to achieve its stated goals.

### **1. Scientific Value**

**Rating: B (Good). Confidence: B (High)**

The proposal addresses a well-defined and clinically consequential knowledge gap. The premise that the relationship between baseline lung cancer risk and net screening benefit is non-monotonic is clearly articulated and supported by the preliminary data, including the striking contrast in 90-day surgical mortality between NLST-ineligible and NLST-eligible SEER patients (5.90% vs. 2.37%) and the substantial difference in estimated life expectancy (14 vs. 21.2 years). The central scientific value lies in shifting the evidence base from a blanket eligibility criterion to a more granular, individualized understanding of the benefit–harm balance. The descriptive contribution—characterizing the observed prevalence and outcomes of screening according to comorbidity, functional limitation, and airflow obstruction in three real-world settings—is substantial and would provide a useful resource for clinicians and guideline developers.

The multi-site design, encompassing an integrated care system (KPSC), a safety-net hospital (MUSC), and a Veterans Affairs facility (VA Portland), enhances the external validity of the descriptive findings. The inclusion of claims-based functional limitation indicators and spirometry data goes beyond standard comorbidity counts and addresses a recognized limitation of indices that may not capture physiologic reserve. Linking these rich phenotyping data to the UM-LCS simulation model offers a plausible route to estimating long-term outcomes that cannot be directly observed.

However, the strength and scope of the attainable knowledge contribution are constrained by several design-inherent limitations. The observational nature of Aims 1 and 2 means that the estimated associations between comorbidity and screening outcomes will reflect a mixture of causal effects and selection processes. The decision to screen, to biopsy a detected nodule, and to pursue invasive treatment is heavily influenced by clinical judgment about a patient’s fitness—a judgment that is informed by the same comorbidity and functional status variables under study and by other unmeasured factors. The proposal acknowledges this and specifies propensity-score methods, but residual confounding by unmeasured clinical gestalt, frailty, smoking intensity, or patient preferences is a structural feature of the data. The resulting estimates will describe patterns of care and outcomes in a screened population, not an uncontaminated causal effect of comorbidity on harm probability. This limits the strength of the inferences that can be drawn for individualized clinical thresholds. The contribution is better characterized as prognostic and descriptive rather than as yielding the “quantitative evidence for individualized screening” implied by the proposal’s strongest claims.

A further limitation concerns the validation of the long-term simulation outputs. The proposed real-world cohort can directly observe short-term outcomes such as cancer detection rates, false positives, and procedural complications, but not lung cancer mortality or life-years gained. The microsimulation extrapolates these long-term outcomes using model structures and external parameters (e.g., preclinical sojourn times, stage distributions) that are not independently validated for the specific multimorbidity subgroups under study. The extent to which the projected benefit–harm thresholds are driven by new empirical findings versus structural modeling assumptions is thus an open question that is not addressed in the proposal text.

### **2. Innovation**

**Rating: B (Good). Confidence: B (High)**

The proposal’s primary innovation is the integration of detailed, real-world patient-level functional and pulmonary data with a validated microsimulation model to generate long-term, net-benefit projections for lung cancer screening. Prior work, including the investigative team’s own comorbidity profiling in lung cancer patients and the Caverly et al. study on the preference-sensitive nature of screening benefit, has addressed comorbidity or individual preferences in isolation. The stated novelty is the combination of observed outcomes from geographically diverse screened populations, including functional limitations and airflow obstruction, into a single modeling framework. This is a meaningful, incremental advance over existing model-based analyses that have relied more heavily on trial populations or SEER-Medicare data without detailed functional phenotyping.

The operationalization of functional limitation using claims-derived indicators, rather than self-report, is a practical innovation that leverages data already available in many health systems but not yet systematically applied to screening benefit–harm assessments. The plan to update comorbidity and functional status as time-varying covariates across screening rounds addresses a limitation of studies using only baseline measures and acknowledges the dynamic nature of chronic disease in an aging population.

However, the novelty claim of “assessing functional and pulmonary status beyond comorbidity counts” is incremental rather than fundamentally new. Studies in other cancer screening and treatment contexts have combined comorbidity indices with functional or frailty measures. The more distinctive planned contribution is the formal, quantitative linkage between these detailed patient characteristics and the net-benefit outputs of a simulation model. The proposal text, however, does not provide sufficient detail on this linkage. It states that cohort-derived measures will “refine” model inputs and that results will be “compared with the original trial-based model,” but it does not specify how functional limitation or FEV1/FVC will be mapped onto the model’s core parameters (e.g., competing mortality, treatment complications, or stage shift). This lack of specification limits the basis for concluding that the integration yields a substantive advance over the prior work that the team has conducted separately. The innovation, at present, is a well-motivated plan rather than a fully specified design element.

### **3. Rigor**

**Rating: C (Moderate). Confidence: B (High)**

The proposal demonstrates a generally methodologically sound approach to the descriptive components of the research. The multi-site cohort assembly, the detailed operationalization of multimorbidity across multiple domains (Charlson/Elixhauser, functional limitation indicators, spirometry), and the use of discrete-time survival models for the repeated-measures data structure are all appropriate and well-justified. The inclusion of power calculations, a pre-specified plan for propensity-score methods for non-binary exposures, and sensitivity analyses for missing data conveys an awareness of the major inferential threats in observational research.

However, the rigor of the project’s most ambitious and policy-relevant aim, Aim 3, is materially weakened by a critical analytical gap. The proposal does not describe the specific method by which the cohort-derived estimates from Aims 1 and 2 will be translated into the parameters of the microsimulation model. This translation is the central inferential step that distinguishes this project from a purely descriptive study. The simulation model’s findings are sensitive to how competing mortality, treatment complications, and cancer outcomes are jointly modeled. The proposal does not specify whether the hazard ratios for procedure complications derived in Aim 2 will be fed directly into the model as independent modifiers, or whether a more complex joint modeling approach will be used to account for correlations between comorbidity, cancer stage, treatment choice, and survival. Without this specification, the validity of the “thresholds where benefits and harms resemble those of a predefined average-health subgroup” cannot be assessed.

This gap interacts with the problem of confounding by clinical selection. The complication rates estimated in the cohort will reflect the fact that clinicians already select patients for biopsy and treatment based on perceived fitness. If these downwardly biased estimates are naively inserted into a simulation model that assumes they represent the causal effect of comorbidity on procedure-related harm, the model will overestimate the net benefit of screening in the high-comorbidity subgroups. The proposal does not describe a method to adjust the observational estimates for this selection process before they are used to parameterize the model.

A further concern for rigor is the unvalidated NLP algorithm for radiology report abstraction. The proposal states that the algorithm has “unpublished sensitivity and specificity above 95%” in prior work. As the primary tool for ascertaining nodule characteristics and outcomes such as false positives and Lung-RADS adherence, its accuracy across the three health systems with different reporting styles is a critical dependency. The text does not describe a prospective cross-site validation protocol against a gold-standard chart review. This introduces a risk of systematic measurement error that could bias the core estimates.

Finally, the systematic missingness of functional limitation data at MUSC (a site serving a socioeconomically disadvantaged population) is not addressed by an analytical strategy that credibly allows the findings to be generalized to that population or to other safety-net settings. Multiple imputation under a missing-at-random assumption is unlikely to be defensible when the variable is wholly unmeasured in a sub-cohort due to care-setting differences.

### **4. Feasibility**

**Rating: C (Moderate). Confidence: B (High)**

The project is feasible in its core descriptive components. The team has established access to the necessary data infrastructures at all three sites, and the plan to reuse and adapt the data harmonization tools from the prior “Watch the Spot” project is a practical strength that reduces technical risk. The five-year timeline is logically phased, with regulatory approvals, cohort development, and descriptive analyses occupying the first three years, while simulation work begins in parallel and continues through the end of the project.

However, several dependencies critical to the project’s most distinctive contribution are not secured by the described plan, which affects the feasibility of delivering the claimed individualized screening thresholds on schedule.

- **Data completeness across sites:** The feasibility of a unified analysis and simulation model depends on the availability of comparable functional limitation and pulmonary function data at all sites. The text states that functional limitation indicators will be available primarily at KPSC and the VA. The performance of NLP and text-mining methods for spirometry extraction at MUSC is not addressed. If these variables are systematically missing or measured differently at MUSC, the ability to build a single model applicable to the full population is compromised.
- **NLP validation for the screening cohort:** The proposal depends on NLP tools to identify screening-detected nodules and their characteristics. The absence of a prospective validation step on the specific screening cohort and across the three diverse report formats is a significant operational risk that could delay the delivery of reliable outcome data for Aims 2 and 3.
- **Unscheduled handoff between empirical and simulation teams:** The schedule is compressed at the critical junction where cohort estimates must be frozen and transferred to the simulation team. The proposal does not specify a data-freeze milestone or a go/no-go checkpoint to coordinate this handoff. A delay in finalizing the cohort—due, for example, to slower-than-expected NLP validation—would directly compress the simulation and manuscript phase and could prevent the completion of Aim 3 within the project period.
- **Dependence on external simulation parameters:** The UM-LCS model relies on external data sources (e.g., SEER, PLCO) for several foundational parameters. The proposal does not describe a plan for validating the updated model against independent data or for propagating uncertainty in these external inputs through to the final policy conclusions. The feasibility of producing robust thresholds is diminished without such a plan.

### **5. Ethics and Safety**

**Rating: B (Good). Confidence: C (Moderate)**

The proposed research involves no direct intervention, experimental manipulation, or prospective recruitment of participants for research procedures. The LDCT scans and all subsequent clinical care occur as part of routine practice. The ethical considerations therefore center on data governance, privacy protection, and the proportionate management of informational risks. The physical safety risks to participants are negligible.

The protocol describes several appropriate data-protection measures: separation of direct identifiers from the analytical dataset; use of a HIPAA-compliant structure for the central data repository; encrypted transfer via REDCap from MUSC and VA Portland to the coordinating center at KPSC; and authenticated, authorized access for the study team. These technical and procedural controls, if properly implemented, would address the principal operational risks of data breach and unauthorized access.

However, the proposal’s documentation of its ethics framework is incomplete in several respects, which limits confidence in the judgment.

- **Consent and waiver basis:** The text does not state whether informed consent will be obtained or whether a waiver of consent and HIPAA authorization will be sought. While waivers are standard for retrospective and prospective observational research of this type, the absence of any discussion leaves the ethical basis for proceeding without individual consent unspecified.
- **Re-identification risk:** The combination of detailed clinical variables, functional limitation scores, geocoded census data, and Medicare linkage creates a dataset with high granularity. The protocol does not discuss any measures for statistical disclosure control or cell-size suppression in the analysis of small subgroups (e.g., patients with rare multimorbidity profiles stratified by site, race, and ethnicity).
- **Cross-site data governance harmonization:** The three sites operate under different regulatory frameworks (particularly the VA). The proposal does not describe how data-use agreements will harmonize permitted uses, retention periods, or site-specific restrictions within the central repository.
- **Medicare linkage governance:** The addition of Medicare claims introduces another data source and transfer. The governance of this linkage (direct agreement with CMS, an intermediary, or a trusted third party) is not specified.
- **Community engagement:** The proposal does not mention any form of community or patient engagement, which is a limitation in the ethical framing for a study that explicitly examines variation by race/ethnicity and socioeconomic status and will generate evidence that could differentially affect screening recommendations for underserved groups.

These unspecified elements do not indicate that the research would be unsafe or unethical. They do mean that the ethical and safety case, as presented in the supplied text, is incomplete. The project’s fundamental ethical permissibility is likely to be established through the standard IRB and data-use agreement processes that are anticipated but not described.

### **6. Management**

**Rating: B (Good). Confidence: C (Moderate)**

The proposal assembles a multidisciplinary team with documented, directly relevant expertise. The PI and collaborators have published on comorbidity in lung cancer, NLP-based radiology report abstraction, and microsimulation modeling within the CISNET consortium. This track record provides credible evidence that the team possesses the technical skills to execute the core tasks of cohort assembly, exposure measurement, and simulation refinement. The inclusion of patient advocates for interpretation and dissemination is a strength for the translational aims.

The management structure is clearly outlined: a coordinating center at KPSC led by named investigators with statistical support; a common data dictionary; monthly team calls; and annual meetings. The reuse of the “Watch the Spot” common vocabulary and infrastructure is a practical advantage that should reduce the time required for initial data mapping and harmonization. The explicit assignment of responsibility for the data pipeline to Gould, Shen, and Fan provides accountability.

The five-year timeline maps the work into logical, phased dependencies. The acknowledgment that regulatory approvals will occupy the first half of Year 1 is realistic for a multi-site study using EHR and claims data.

However, the management plan has an important gap at its most critical juncture. The proposal does not specify a formal mechanism—such as a data-freeze milestone or a go/no-go checkpoint—to coordinate the handoff of finalized cohort estimates from the empirical team (Aims 1 and 2) to the simulation team (Aim 3). The schedule shows these activities overlapping in Years 3 and 4, but without a frozen dataset and a defined delivery date, the simulation work could proceed on preliminary data that is later revised, or be delayed by holdups in the descriptive analyses. A compressed simulation phase at the end of the project could prevent the rigorous exploration of sensitivity analyses and uncertainty that the stated aims demand.

Additionally, the plan for validating the NLP tools on the specific screening cohort across the three sites is not integrated into the management timeline or the resource allocation. This validation is a prerequisite for the reliability of the core outcomes and should be an early, explicitly scheduled activity. Its absence from the management plan is a coordination risk.

---

### **Overall Recommendation: Current proposal insufficient for support (revise)**

The proposal addresses a question of genuine clinical importance and is grounded in a well-reasoned conceptual framework. The team has the necessary expertise, and the core descriptive aims (Aims 1 and 2) are feasible and would produce a valuable characterization of real-world screening outcomes across levels of multimorbidity in three diverse care settings. The project’s fundamental direction is therefore a worthwhile one.

However, the proposal’s most ambitious and distinguishing aim—to generate validated, quantitative thresholds for individualized screening decisions using a microsimulation model—is not adequately supported by the current plan. The critical inferential pathway from confounded observational estimates to causal simulation parameters is unspecified. This gap, combined with a key predictor that is systematically missing at one site, an unvalidated NLP outcome-ascertainment tool, and an under-specified handoff between the empirical and simulation phases, introduces significant uncertainty about whether the projected long-term benefit–harm thresholds will be robust. These issues are structural to the design as described and affect the project’s main goals.

The project is not unsupportable in principle. A revised proposal that specifies (a) the exact method for translating cohort-derived estimates into simulation model parameters, (b) a strategy for handling systematic missingness in functional limitation data, (c) a prospective NLP validation plan, and (d) a formal milestone for the data freeze and handoff between Aims 2 and 3 would substantially strengthen it. The recommendation is thus to revise and resubmit, preserving the important core question and descriptive infrastructure while strengthening the inferential chain for the modeling aim.

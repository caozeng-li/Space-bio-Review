## Overall Assessment

This proposal describes a multisite, prospective, observational cohort study designed to characterize recovery trajectories across three outcome domains—linguistic impairment, cognitive-communicative participation, and quality of life—over 18 months following stroke-related aphasia. The project further aims to evaluate the incremental predictive value of clinical, treatment-exposure, genetic, and neuroimaging variables, and to develop an interactive tool for generating individualized expected trajectories. The leadership team, led by Heinemann and Cherney, brings directly relevant expertise in aphasia rehabilitation, psychometrics, rehabilitation outcomes measurement, and longitudinal statistical modeling, including prior successful application of individual growth curve (IGC) methods in related clinical populations. The study is grounded in a well-articulated rationale: the current literature on post-stroke aphasia recovery is fragmented across outcome domains, limited by short follow-up durations, and rarely examines the joint predictive contribution of multiple biomarker classes alongside detailed treatment-exposure data.

The proposal’s principal strengths are its ambitious integration of impairment, participation, and subjective well-being outcomes within a single longitudinal framework; its plan to collect granular, prospective therapy-content data rather than relying on coarse billing codes; and its systematic approach to testing whether resource-intensive biomarkers add meaningful prognostic information beyond routinely available clinical variables. These features, if successfully executed, would produce a uniquely detailed descriptive and prognostic resource for the aphasia research community and a clinically oriented tool with potential utility for shared decision-making and discharge planning.

However, the proposal’s capacity to deliver fully on its stated aims is constrained by several material limitations that are not resolvable within the current design as described. These limitations cluster around three themes: (1) the inherent inferential boundaries of an observational design in which treatment is neither assigned nor controlled, yet treatment-outcome associations are framed with causal language and are intended to inform the prediction tool; (2) unresolved feasibility dependencies, most critically an ambitious 75% retention target that lacks supporting evidence and a novel, labor-intensive therapist-logging protocol whose real-world sustainability is not fully de-risked; and (3) important ethical and procedural specifications—particularly around consent capacity assessment, genetic return-of-results policy, and MRI incidental-finding management—that are absent from the reviewed sections. These issues do not negate the project’s considerable scientific value or the soundness of its core descriptive aims, but they introduce substantive risks to the more ambitious prognostic and translational goals. The recommendation of conditional support reflects the judgement that a supportable scope is identifiable—centered on trajectory description, comparative outcome-domain time courses, and internally validated prognostic modeling with explicit acknowledgment of associational limits—provided specific factual premises are clarified and strengthened. The conditions identified below address the gaps that most directly affect the credibility and feasibility of the central inferences.

## Evaluation by Criteria

### 1. Scientific Value: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

**Supported contributions.** The question addressed is of genuine scientific and clinical importance. The proposal’s core ambition—to model recovery as individual growth curves across linguistic, participatory, and quality-of-life domains, and to test whether these trajectories diverge in clinically meaningful ways—represents a conceptually important extension beyond the impairment-focused endpoint analyses that characterize much of the existing aphasia recovery literature. The explicit hypothesis that linguistic improvement slows around six months while participation and QoL continue to improve out to 18 months operationalizes the ICF distinction between impairment and participation in a testable longitudinal form. If the study meets its retention target and the planned models satisfy their internal validation criteria, the resulting dataset would constitute one of the most comprehensive, multidimensional descriptions of early post-stroke aphasia recovery currently available.

The integration of three outcome domains within a single cohort, the prospective documentation of therapy content at a task-and-modality level, and the direct comparison of nested predictive models—with and without genetic and imaging predictor blocks—address open and practically relevant questions about the incremental value of biomarker assessment in clinical prognosis. The development of an interactive trajectory tool is a well-conceived translational output that could support shared decision-making, though its utility is contingent on adequate model performance and careful communication of its associational (not causal) basis.

**Material limitations and their consequences for attainable knowledge.** The most consequential limitation is the observational nature of the treatment-exposure data. Treatment is part of ordinary clinical care and is not assigned by the study; the proposal acknowledges this in its design statement but then employs language that implies stronger inferential goals, such as “earlier, more intensive, and longer treatment is expected to accompany better outcomes.” The attainable knowledge is strictly associational. Confounding by indication—patients with better recovery potential receiving more or different therapy—is a fundamental threat that statistical adjustment cannot fully resolve without randomization or strong, defensible instrumental-variable conditions, neither of which is proposed. The trajectory tool, if it accepts “therapy characteristics” as inputs to generate expected recovery paths, risks being misinterpreted as predicting the consequence of modifying therapy, which the design cannot support. This limitation does not negate the value of the treatment-exposure data for generating descriptive hypotheses about real-world service use and its correlation with outcomes, but it constrains the epistemic status of any treatment-related conclusions.

Additional limitations temper the depth of attainable knowledge from the genetic and imaging components. The candidate-gene approach, while biologically plausible, faces modest statistical power for detecting gene-gene interactions in a sample of fewer than 300 complete genetic observations. The resulting high-risk genotype indicator is likely to be sample-specific and difficult to replicate. The imaging subsample of 100 participants, while reasonable for feasibility, limits the precision and generalizability of imaging-related findings, particularly when multiple network measures are examined across density thresholds and parcellation schemes. The imaging component is best understood as an exploratory biomarker module that can identify broad patterns rather than fine-grained network mechanisms. The reliance on internal validation (bootstrap, Monte Carlo cross-validation) without an external holdout sample means that the generalizability of the final prognostic model and the associated tool to other settings or populations remains uncertain.

**Summary.** The proposal’s scientific value lies in its ambitious, integrated longitudinal framework and its capacity to produce a uniquely detailed descriptive and internally validated prognostic resource. The inherent limitations of the observational design, modest sample sizes for some predictor classes, and reliance on internal validation constrain the attainable knowledge to descriptive and associational dimensions, stopping short of causal, mechanistic, or externally generalizable conclusions. These constraints are consistent with a rating of Good rather than Excellent: the contribution is substantial and well-justified, but the most ambitious inferential claims are not fully supported by the design.

### 2. Innovation: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

**What the project adds beyond prior work.** The proposal’s innovation resides primarily in its integration and scope, rather than in the development of fundamentally new assays, imaging sequences, or computational algorithms. Several features represent genuine extensions of prior work as the proposal characterizes it. The simultaneous trajectory modeling of three outcome domains with formal comparison of their time courses—and the explicit hypothesis that they diverge after six months—is a conceptually important extension beyond studies that examine impairment-level outcomes alone or add single participation or QoL measures cross-sectionally. The granular, prospective documentation of therapy content (modality, task timing) using therapist logs, rather than retrospective billing codes, is a methodological innovation for observational aphasia research that could generate hypotheses about treatment intensity and modality that randomized trials rarely address at this scale or ecological validity. The systematic inclusion of candidate gene-gene interactions, resting-state connectivity measures, and clinical predictors in a single model, with explicit comparison of their incremental predictive value, contrasts with studies that examine biomarker classes in isolation and leave their added utility unclear. The application of individual growth curve methods to aphasia recovery—an adaptation of approaches the team has used in spinal cord injury and traumatic brain injury—is a non-trivial extension to a domain where recovery plateaus, rates, and shapes are debated.

**Distinguishing originality from scientific value.** Several points temper the degree of innovation. The overall observational cohort design with longitudinal behavioral assessments is not novel in itself; large-scale aphasia cohort studies exist, though they vary in scope. The genetic and imaging methods (TaqMan genotyping, resting-state fMRI with HCP-style pipelines, graph-theoretic network measures, FreeSurfer volumetry) are well-established and drawn from standard protocols. The computational approach to generating individualized predictions from fitted model coefficients follows a known translational model in prognostic research. The innovation is therefore best characterized as residing in the specific combination and integration of these elements for aphasia recovery, and in the comparative prognostic evidence this integration can generate. This is a meaningful and well-supported contribution, but it does not constitute a paradigm-shifting methodological breakthrough.

The preliminary imaging pilot (n=8), while useful for demonstrating feasibility and motivating the larger study, does not itself constitute an independent novel discovery; its finding that language and attention changes were not correlated and associated with different connectivity patterns is suggestive but cannot be considered established on the basis of eight participants. The proposal appropriately treats this as motivation rather than as confirmed prior work.

**Summary.** The project adds meaningfully to prior work through its integrated framework, its prospective treatment-content measurement, and its comparative evaluation of predictor classes. The claims to originality are well supported, provided they are understood as residing in the integration and scope rather than in novel assays or algorithms. A rating of Good reflects a substantial and clearly articulated increment over prior work that does not, however, represent a transformative departure from established approaches.

### 3. Rigor: C (Moderate) — Confidence: B (High)

**Design elements that support the intended inferences.** The proposal’s core methodological choices are well aligned with its descriptive and predictive aims. Individual growth curve modeling within a mixed-model framework is an established and appropriate method for longitudinal data with unequally spaced measurements and intermittent missingness. The plan to compare linear, quadratic, cubic, and negative-exponential functional forms using AIC and BIC, with residual diagnostics and a sensitivity comparison against categorical-time models, provides reasonable protection against model misspecification. The team’s prior experience applying IGC methods to spinal cord injury and traumatic brain injury strengthens confidence in their capacity to execute these analyses. The target of 300 retained participants with up to five time points is large by the standards of aphasia recovery research and supports estimation of random slopes and intercepts with reasonable precision for the principal descriptive aims.

The treatment-exposure measurement strategy is unusually detailed: triangulating facility billing records, clinician-completed task logs with modality coding, progress-note abstraction, and post-discharge participant checklists supported by regular research-assistant contact. The explicit reliability target (clinicians trained to ≥90% agreement) and the plan to video-record 10% of sessions for independent log verification address a known measurement gap in rehabilitation research. The bootstrap stability assessment and Monte Carlo cross-validation for Aim 3 provide honest internal performance estimates, and the use of the minimally clinically important difference (MCID) as the prediction accuracy threshold is a principled and clinically meaningful criterion.

The imaging and genetic analysis plans employ widely used, well-documented pipelines (HCP-style preprocessing, FSL, SPM, FreeSurfer, lesion-aware registration using virtual brain transplant, TaqMan genotyping with k-means clustering for quality control). The dual resting-state scan for the imaging subsample allows estimation of scan-to-scan reliability, which is a thoughtful addition.

**Limitations that temper methodological support.** The central inferential limitation—that treatment is neither assigned nor controlled—is acknowledged in the design statement but is not addressed with any formal sensitivity analysis. The proposal states that treatment-outcome associations will be adjusted for patient characteristics, but confounding by indication cannot be adequately managed by covariate adjustment alone in the absence of randomization or strong quasi-experimental methods. Inverse-probability-of-treatment weighting, propensity-score methods, or instrumental-variable approaches are not proposed. For a study that explicitly frames treatment characteristics as predictors and intends to incorporate them into a clinical tool, the absence of any formal attention to the magnitude or direction of treatment-selection bias is a material gap in methodological rigor.

The sample-size rationale, while defensible for the primary descriptive trajectory estimation, leaves important questions unanswered for the predictive aims. The number of candidate predictors—spanning demographics, clinical variables, therapy characteristics, genetic polymorphisms with interaction terms, and multiple imaging measures—is large relative to the sample of 300 (or fewer for the imaging and genetic subsets). The proposal describes a purposeful model selection strategy but does not commit to a sample-size-sensitive penalty (e.g., BIC, a prespecified shrinkage method such as ridge regression or LASSO, or a degrees-of-freedom budget) that would constrain model complexity during fitting. The bootstrap and cross-validation in Aim 3 can diagnose over-optimism retrospectively but cannot increase effective sample size or prevent overfitting during the selection process itself. Without a clearer specification of how model complexity will be managed—for example, by limiting the number of candidate predictors per outcome, using a penalized approach, or prespecifying a maximum number of parameters per block—the risk that the reported model will be more optimistic than cross-validation fully corrects is elevated, particularly for secondary outcomes and the fully integrated models.

The genetic interaction analysis faces a related challenge. With ten candidate variants, a two-way or three-way epistasis screen using multifactor dimensionality reduction explores a large combinatorial space. The proposal does not provide a power approximation for these interaction tests, and the resulting high-risk indicator is likely to be sample-specific and difficult to replicate. The proposal appropriately frames these analyses as exploratory, but the absence of a power assessment makes it difficult to judge whether non-significant interaction findings will be interpretable as evidence of absence or simply as insufficient information.

Measurement considerations also bear on rigor. The assessment battery is lengthy—well over two hours of direct testing before rest breaks—and the operational feasibility of administering it with fidelity to participants with moderate-to-severe aphasia across three sites and multiple examiners is not fully developed. The proposal notes that aphasia-supportive communication will be used but does not detail how measurement error arising from comprehension or expression difficulties will be handled in the growth models, beyond a mention that Rasch analysis may be used for ordinal outcomes. The psychometric properties of the primary instruments (SAQoL-39, CPIB) have been established in aphasia populations, which provides some external support, but the proposal does not discuss how aphasia severity might systematically affect self-report measurement quality over time.

**Summary.** The design can credibly deliver on its primary descriptive aims—estimating unconditional recovery trajectories and comparing their time courses across outcome domains—with appropriate sensitivity to model form. For the predictive aims and the integrative models combining all predictor classes, the rigor is weakened by the absence of a confounding sensitivity analysis for treatment-exposure associations, by a predictor-to-sample-size ratio that is not adequately addressed through prespecified complexity constraints, and by underpowered interaction analyses. These limitations do not undermine the core descriptive contribution but materially affect the strength of the prognostic and translational inferences. A rating of Moderate reflects a design that is adequate for its most central descriptive goals but has substantive gaps in its capacity to support the more ambitious predictive and associative claims.

### 4. Feasibility: C (Moderate) — Confidence: B (High)

**Strengths supporting feasibility.** The investigative team is well matched to the project’s demands. The co-PIs bring complementary expertise in aphasia treatment and psychometrics (Cherney) and in rehabilitation outcomes, health services research, and longitudinal statistical modeling (Heinemann). The addition of specialists in genomics and neuroimaging within the management structure provides credible oversight for the multi-domain data collection. The plan to recruit from three established inpatient rehabilitation facilities demonstrates existing clinical access to the target population, and the team’s prior success in extracting therapy billing data from these settings reduces implementation risk for the billing-record component. The use of REDCap for centralized data management, with explicit time allocated for database setup, site approvals, and staff training before recruitment commences, reflects sound operational planning. The five-year schedule mapping specific activities to quarters is clear and includes built-in contingencies for extending the accrual period or adding a fourth site if recruitment or retention falters.

The quality-control infrastructure is well specified for the domains it addresses. Certified NIH Stroke Scale examiners, standardized training across sites, explicit reliability targets for treatment logs (clinicians to ≥90% agreement), video-recording of 10% of treatment and assessment sessions for independent rescoring, and a 10% recheck of case-report forms and data entry provide a robust system for monitoring fidelity and data integrity.

**Critical dependencies and unresolved risks.** The feasibility of the central analytical aims is critically dependent on retaining 300 of the 400 enrolled participants (75%) through the 18-month follow-up. The proposal states an assumed loss of 15% by six months and an additional 10% by 18 months. No evidence—from the team’s own prior work in comparable populations or from the aphasia literature—is provided to support the feasibility of achieving this low dropout rate. The population is medically acute, transitioning from intensive inpatient rehabilitation to community settings, and faces significant communication, physical, and socioeconomic barriers to continued research participation. The management plan describes weekly research-assistant calls transitioning to monthly calls, which aids contact maintenance but is a relatively light-touch retention strategy. A failure to meet the retention target would not simply reduce statistical power; it would systematically bias the trajectory estimates, as dropout is explicitly identified as a threat to the missing-at-random assumption on which the IGC models depend. The proposal’s fallback—extending accrual or adding a fourth site—addresses recruitment volume but does not directly address retention.

The novel therapist-logging protocol introduces a critical implementation dependency. Clinicians under clinical productivity pressures are asked to record start-stop times and modality codes for each task during treatment sessions. The training-and-reliability protocol is well specified, and the survey of 56 speech-language pathologists provides some local evidence of willingness. However, sustained compliance over the multi-year accrual period—with staff turnover and varying clinical workflows across three facilities—is not fully de-risked. The escalation procedure (video-recording sessions for later research-assistant coding if a clinician cannot reach the 90% reliability criterion) creates a potential bottleneck: it adds an unstaffed, lagged data-collection task that could delay data cleaning and analysis. The management of this escalation—tracking which clinicians require it, ensuring timely coding, and staffing the coding effort—is not specified. Given that the therapy-content variables are a core scientific contribution of the project, any systematic degradation of logging compliance or delay in processing would directly affect the quality of a central predictor set.

The schedule concentrates data cleaning, final IGC model development, and the creation of the prediction tool in the final year (Year 5). This terminal analytic phase is entirely contingent on the successful and timely completion of all preceding activities: complete enrollment, high retention, reliable extraction and cleaning of therapy logs from all sites, and completion of genetic and neuroimaging data processing. The schedule does not describe early analytic checkpoints—such as a mid-project integration of a subset of data streams to test the full pipeline—that would de-risk the final synthesis. A delay or quality failure in any one stream could cascade into the final year with limited time for remediation.

The imaging protocol specifies that the subsample will receive two resting-state scans to estimate reliability, but the schedule lists MRI acquisition only at admission. How two scans will be accommodated within a single session, the impact on the stated 30-minute protocol timeline, and the management of participant burden for an extended scanning session are not clarified. This ambiguity could affect the feasibility of obtaining usable reliability data from a population that may find prolonged scanning difficult.

**Summary.** The investigative team and the existing clinical infrastructure provide a strong foundation for feasibility. The quality-control plan is thorough. However, the feasibility of delivering the full set of aims is materially constrained by three unresolved dependencies: an ambitious retention target that lacks supporting evidence; a novel, labor-intensive therapist-logging protocol whose real-world sustainability is not fully de-risked; and a back-loaded analytic timeline that concentrates critical integration work in the final year without early integration checkpoints. A rating of Moderate reflects a project that is feasible in its core descriptive components but carries substantive execution risk for its more ambitious predictive and integrative goals.

### 5. Ethics and Safety: C (Moderate) — Confidence: C (Moderate)

The proposal identifies the principal risks—fatigue, frustration, MRI discomfort, and loss of confidentiality—and describes proportionate mitigation measures. The use of supported conversation techniques for consent, the option to split assessment visits across consecutive days, the availability of home-based follow-up assessments, and the inclusion of patient-reported outcomes alongside performance-based measures all reflect an appropriate sensitivity to the needs of participants with aphasia. The confidentiality and data-governance framework—research records separated from medical records, code-based identification, password-protected electronic files, secured physical storage, and a data-use agreement for sharing that prohibits reidentification—follows standard practice for clinical research. The establishment of a central Data Monitoring and Oversight Committee with clinical, imaging, genetic, epidemiologic, and patient/family representation provides appropriate oversight for an observational study, and the annual review of safety, burden, progress, and data integrity is well calibrated to the study’s risk profile.

However, several material gaps in the supplied sections limit confidence that the ethical and safety arrangements are fully proportionate. First, the proposal requires participants to have “sufficient capacity to consent and participate in testing” but does not describe the instrument, assessor qualifications, or standard that will be used to determine capacity. Aphasia is a language disorder, and capacity assessment in this population is methodologically challenging; without a specified procedure, the protection of potentially vulnerable individuals rests on a criterion whose application is undocumented. The proposal also does not discuss the ethical implications of excluding people with more severe aphasia from an observational study that aims to describe recovery trajectories. This exclusion may protect individuals from research burden but also introduces selection bias and potentially excludes those whose participation and quality-of-life outcomes are most clinically informative. The trade-off between protection and representativeness warrants explicit ethical justification.

Second, the proposal does not address the return of individual genetic results or the management of incidental findings from genetic or imaging data. For a study collecting genetic material—even with a candidate-gene focus that reduces the likelihood of medically actionable incidental findings—a return-of-results policy is a standard ethical expectation. The absence of any statement on this matter, including whether genetic counseling would be available, is a limitation. Similarly, the MRI safety protocol mentions screening for implants, claustrophobia, and pregnancy but does not describe who conducts the screening, whether a standardized checklist is used, how incidental findings on research-acquired structural scans will be managed (e.g., radiologist review, communication to the participant and treating physician), or the procedure for managing participant distress or withdrawal during scanning. These are standard elements of research MRI governance, and their absence from the reviewed material limits confidence that the imaging substudy is fully specified from a safety perspective.

The proposal’s data-sharing plan appropriately prohibits reidentification, but the risk of reidentification from the combination of genetic, imaging, and demographic variables—a recognized concern in contemporary biomedical data sharing—is not explicitly discussed. A brief acknowledgment of this risk and the rationale for judging it acceptable would strengthen the ethical framework.

**Summary.** The proposal demonstrates appropriate general awareness of the risks associated with research involving participants with aphasia and includes proportionate mitigation strategies. Strengths include the communication-supportive consent approach, the oversight committee’s composition, and the attention to assessment burden. The main limitations are the absence of a specified capacity-assessment procedure, the lack of a genetic return-of-results or incidental-findings policy, and incomplete specification of MRI safety governance including incidental-finding management. These are material to ethical appropriateness and safety management. The confidence rating of Moderate reflects the fact that these gaps are identified primarily from omissions in the supplied text rather than from demonstrated deficiencies; a more complete application may address some or all of them. The ethical and safety framework, as documented here, is adequate to proceed only if these specifications are resolved.

### 6. Management: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

**Strengths.** The management structure integrates the necessary domain expertise. The co-PI arrangement pairs leadership in aphasia clinical research and psychometrics with leadership in rehabilitation outcomes measurement, health services research, and longitudinal statistical modeling. The explicit inclusion of genomics and neuroimaging specialists within the oversight structure ensures that these technically demanding components have dedicated expert supervision. The delineation of a five-year schedule with activities mapped to specific quarters provides a clear and plausible timeline, with explicit time allocated for pre-recruitment activities (site approvals, database setup, staff training) that are often compressed in project plans. The built-in contingencies—extending the accrual period or adding a fourth site if recruitment or retention lags—demonstrate proactive risk awareness.

The data-management plan is sound. Centralized REDCap case-report forms, common data checks, coordination through research assistants who maintain post-discharge contact, and training of site staff before recruitment begins represent a scalable operational framework. The quality-control procedures—certified examiners, standardized training, explicit reliability targets, video-recording for independent rescoring, and systematic data-entry verification—are well specified and provide a credible system for monitoring fidelity and data integrity.

**Limitations.** Three management-related limitations temper confidence in delivery. First, the absence of early analytic integration checkpoints is a concern. The schedule concentrates final IGC model development and tool creation in Year 5, with all data cleaning and processing as immediate precursors. A mid-project milestone—for example, a complete pipeline test using data from the first 100 participants with at least 6-month follow-up—would de-risk the final synthesis by identifying data-integration problems, processing bottlenecks, or measurement issues while there is still time to adjust. The current schedule does not build in such a checkpoint.

Second, the management of the therapist-logging escalation pathway is underspecified. The plan states that a clinician who cannot reach the 90% reliability criterion will have sessions recorded for later coding by a research assistant. This creates a new data-collection task—video coding of therapy sessions—that is not explicitly staffed or scheduled. Tracking which clinicians need this support, ensuring timely coding, and managing the associated workload across three sites over multiple years requires a more detailed operational plan than the proposal provides.

Third, the coordination between the imaging subsample and the main cohort is partially specified. The schedule indicates MRI acquisition at admission only, but the proposal’s scientific description mentions two resting-state scans for reliability estimation. How this additional scan time is accommodated within the protocol, how it is scheduled alongside the already lengthy assessment battery, and how the imaging data will be integrated with the main dataset for the final analytic phase are not fully articulated.

**Summary.** The management plan is fundamentally sound, with appropriate expertise, a clear timeline, a strong data-management infrastructure, and well-specified quality-control procedures. The limitations—absence of early integration checkpoints, underspecified management of the therapist-logging escalation pathway, and incomplete articulation of imaging-main cohort coordination—are practical gaps that can be addressed through operational planning and do not reflect fundamental flaws in the management structure. A rating of Good reflects a management framework that is adequate to support the project’s core aims, with specific operational details that warrant further specification.

## Synthesis of Strengths and Limitations

### Cross-Cutting Strengths

1. **Integrated, multidimensional framework.** The project’s most distinctive strength is its simultaneous attention to impairment, participation, and quality of life within a single longitudinal design, using individual growth curve methods that respect heterogeneity in recovery shape. This integration addresses a genuine fragmentation in the current literature and promises a more holistic picture of aphasia recovery than studies focused on a single outcome domain.

2. **Prospective, granular treatment measurement.** The commitment to moving beyond billing codes to therapist-logged, task-level treatment content is a methodological contribution with implications beyond this project. It addresses a recognized limitation in rehabilitation outcomes research and could generate hypotheses about treatment intensity and modality that inform future intervention studies.

3. **Comparative evaluation of predictor classes.** The explicit plan to test nested models—comparing the predictive performance of models with and without genetic, imaging, and therapy-exposure predictor blocks—directly addresses the practical question of whether resource-intensive biomarkers improve prognosis enough to justify their clinical use. This comparative framework is a strength of the analytical design.

4. **Strong team and clinical infrastructure.** The co-PIs’ complementary expertise, their prior experience with IGC methods in related populations, and the existing clinical access through three inpatient rehabilitation facilities provide a credible foundation for execution.

5. **Well-specified quality control.** The reliability targets, video-recording and rescoring plans, and systematic data-entry verification provide a robust system for monitoring fidelity and data integrity.

### Cross-Cutting Limitations

1. **Observational treatment data and the absence of confounding sensitivity analysis.** This limitation runs through the scientific value, rigor, and feasibility criteria. The proposal’s treatment-related claims are associational, yet the language occasionally implies causal expectations, and no formal sensitivity analysis is proposed to assess the potential magnitude of confounding by indication. The trajectory tool, if it incorporates therapy characteristics, risks clinical misinterpretation. This is the single most important inferential constraint on the project.

2. **Predictor-to-sample-size ratio and model complexity management.** The ambition to integrate demographic, clinical, therapy, genetic, and imaging predictors within a single model is scientifically appealing but strains the statistical capacity of a sample of 300 (with smaller subsets for imaging and complete genetic data). The absence of prespecified complexity constraints—such as a penalized regression approach, a degrees-of-freedom budget, or a limited number of candidate predictors per outcome—increases the risk of overfitting and limits the interpretability of the resulting model. The bootstrap and cross-validation provide retrospective diagnostics but do not substitute for prospective parsimony.

3. **Retention target without supporting evidence.** The 75% retention rate at 18 months is critical to the study’s central aims but is asserted rather than supported. In a medically acute population with significant communication disability, this rate is ambitious, and the retained sample is likely to be biased toward better outcomes, threatening the validity of trajectory estimates for the most impaired patients.

4. **Ethical and safety specifications incomplete.** The absence of a documented capacity-assessment procedure, a genetic return-of-results policy, and MRI incidental-finding management protocols are material gaps that affect confidence in the ethical and safety framework. These are remediable through operational specification but are not resolved in the reviewed material.

5. **Terminal analytic integration without early checkpoints.** The concentration of final model development and tool creation in Year 5, without mid-project integration milestones, creates a schedule risk that could be mitigated through earlier pipeline testing.

## Recommendation: Conditional Support (conditional_support)

**Rationale.** This proposal describes a project with substantial scientific merit. The integrated longitudinal framework, the systematic comparison of outcome-domain trajectories, the prospective therapy-content measurement, and the comparative evaluation of predictor classes would, if successfully executed, produce a valuable descriptive and prognostic resource for aphasia research and a clinically relevant tool. The investigative team is well qualified, the clinical infrastructure is established, and the quality-control plan is thorough. These strengths support the judgement that the project addresses a worthwhile set of questions and that the core descriptive aims are within reach.

However, the proposal in its current form has substantive problems that affect its capacity to deliver on its more ambitious predictive and translational goals. The most important of these is the absence of any formal attention to treatment-selection confounding, which limits the attainable knowledge from the treatment-exposure data to associational description and creates a risk of clinical misinterpretation of the trajectory tool. The predictor-to-sample-size ratio and the lack of prespecified model-complexity constraints elevate the risk that the reported prognostic model will be overfitted and poorly generalizable. The ambitious retention target is not supported by evidence, and its failure would systematically bias the trajectory estimates. The therapist-logging protocol, while scientifically appealing, carries unresolved feasibility risks related to sustained clinician compliance and the management of the escalation pathway. The ethical and safety framework is incomplete in several material respects.

These limitations are not fatal to the project’s core scientific value, but they must be addressed for the proposed plan to be judged sufficient. A supportable scope is already identifiable: a project centered on trajectory description across outcome domains, comparative time-course analysis, and internally validated prognostic modeling that is explicitly and consistently framed as associational, with appropriate caution about the non-causal nature of treatment-outcome associations. The conditions below identify the factual premises that must be clarified and the design elements that must be strengthened to align the proposal with this supportable scope.

### Conditions for Support

1. **Treatment-exposure associations must be explicitly and consistently framed as associational throughout all project communications, including the trajectory tool.** The proposal should include a formal plan for sensitivity analyses that assess the potential magnitude of confounding by indication (e.g., inverse-probability-of-treatment weighting using measured covariates, or a stated rationale for why such analyses are infeasible). The trajectory tool’s user interface and documentation must clearly communicate that therapy characteristics are descriptive covariates, not causal levers, and that predicted trajectories conditional on therapy characteristics do not represent causal effects of modifying therapy.

2. **Model complexity must be managed through prespecified constraints.** The proposal should specify a strategy for limiting the number of candidate predictors or for applying a penalized estimation approach (e.g., LASSO, elastic net, or Bayesian shrinkage priors) that is appropriate to the sample size. A prespecified degrees-of-freedom budget per outcome domain, or a commitment to a limited set of primary predictor comparisons, would strengthen confidence that the final model is not substantially overfitted.

3. **The retention target must be supported by evidence or a more detailed, evidence-informed retention plan.** The proposal should provide data from the team’s prior longitudinal studies in comparable populations (e.g., spinal cord injury, traumatic brain injury) demonstrating achievable retention rates, or should describe proactive retention strategies beyond telephone contact (e.g., community partnerships, transportation support, flexible assessment modalities) that have been shown to improve retention in similar populations. A plan for assessing and reporting the sensitivity of trajectory estimates to nonignorable dropout (e.g., pattern-mixture models) should be included.

4. **The therapist-logging protocol should include a detailed operational plan for managing the escalation pathway and ensuring sustained compliance.** This plan should specify staffing for video coding of therapy sessions, a tracking system for clinician reliability status, a schedule for reliability reassessment, and a contingency plan for sites or periods with low compliance.

5. **The ethical and safety framework should be completed.** Specifically, the proposal should describe: (a) the instrument and procedure for assessing consent capacity, including the qualifications of assessors; (b) the policy on return of individual genetic results and management of incidental findings from genetic and imaging data, including communication pathways; (c) the MRI incidental-finding management protocol, including radiologist review arrangements; and (d) the ethical rationale for excluding individuals who lack consent capacity, with consideration of whether an assent-plus-proxy-consent pathway could be made available.

6. **An early integration checkpoint should be added to the project schedule.** This checkpoint—for example, a complete pipeline test using data from the first 100 participants with at least 6-month follow-up—should be scheduled no later than the end of Year 3 and should include data extraction, cleaning, integration across streams, and a preliminary IGC model fit to identify any data-format, processing, or measurement issues.

These conditions are directed at the limitations that most directly affect the credibility and feasibility of the central inferences. They do not require redesigning the study as a randomized trial, expanding the sample beyond what is feasible, or developing new methods. They ask that the proposal strengthen its alignment between its stated goals and its documented capacity to achieve them, and that it provide the operational and ethical specifications necessary for a project of this scope and complexity.

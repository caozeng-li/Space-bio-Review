## Overall Assessment

This proposal addresses a pressing and clinically significant question: why children who wear hearing aids continue to struggle with speech understanding in noisy environments even when amplification restores audibility. The investigators propose to develop a comprehensive developmental account that differentiates sensory, cognitive, and linguistic contributions to complex listening in school-age children with hearing loss (CHL). The project is ambitious in scope, integrating improved psychoacoustic measurements with cognitive assessments in a longitudinal design across three interconnected Aims. The primary scientific contribution lies in simultaneously characterizing multiple pathways—spectral resolution, perceptual weighting, and selective attention—within the same children over time, and in refining measurement tools to make them clinically feasible.

The proposal's strengths are substantial. The research questions are important and well-motivated by gaps in the current cross-sectional literature. The methodological refinements to the spectral-ripple task and the band-importance weighting procedure represent tangible advances directly responsive to limitations in the team's own prior work. The longitudinal design with planned mediation and growth models allows for the testing of developmental predictions that cross-sectional studies cannot address. The management plan is realistic, with a well-sequenced schedule and thoughtful contingency planning. The ethical framework is sound, with appropriate protections for a pediatric clinical population.

However, the proposal also has limitations that warrant careful consideration. The most significant concerns involve the construct validity of the core psychoacoustic tasks when administered to children with hearing loss at reduced sensation levels, the psychometric properties of the cognitive measures in this specific population, and the observational nature of the longitudinal design, which constrains causal inferences. These issues, discussed in detail below, create some uncertainty about whether the planned analyses can fully disentangle the proposed sensory, cognitive, and linguistic pathways. These are not fatal flaws—the project can still produce valuable descriptive and associational knowledge—but they bound the strength of the mechanistic claims the investigators aim to support.

The synthesis of section-specific reviews reveals a consistent pattern: the proposal demonstrates sophisticated awareness of methodological challenges and includes thoughtful safeguards, but in several places the inferential ambitions outpace what the specified measurements can reliably deliver. The overall recommendation reflects a weighing of the project's clear scientific value and feasibility against the unresolved psychometric and inferential vulnerabilities.

## Criterion-by-Criterion Evaluation

### 1. Scientific Value

**Rating: B (Good) | Confidence: B (High)**

The scientific value of this project is substantial. The central question—why aided audibility does not fully restore speech-in-noise performance in children with hearing loss—is both theoretically important and clinically urgent, given that classroom and social settings are predominantly noisy environments. The project aims to provide a developmental account that distinguishes sensory limitations from cognitive demands and language knowledge across multiple tasks and over time. This goes beyond the predominantly cross-sectional evidence currently available and addresses the pressing need for models that can explain changing listening skills during childhood.

The three complementary Aims collectively target a well-structured knowledge gap. Aim 1 examines spectral resolution and its cognitive demands, with the interstimulus-interval manipulation in Experiment 1b providing a direct test of whether poor ripple thresholds reflect sensory or memory-related processes. Aim 2 develops a clinically feasible method for measuring individual perceptual weights and tests developmental predictions. Aim 3 investigates selective listening in competing speech using a well-chosen contrast between energetic and informational masking. The deliberate inclusion of a normal-hearing adult reference group in Aim 2 and the planned comparisons between children with hearing loss (CHL) and children with normal hearing (CNH) strengthen the developmental inferences.

A particularly commendable feature is the proposal's explicit identification of alternative outcomes that would themselves constitute meaningful scientific contributions. If the predicted mediation patterns do not emerge, the data will still characterize sensory-cognitive relations in this population. If perceptual weights prove stable over two years, this finding would support the current assumption of temporal stability rather than a developmental shift. This self-critical framing demonstrates that the design does not depend on confirming the investigators' preferred model to produce interpretable evidence.

The project will also generate valuable descriptive resources: aided audibility distributions, individual importance functions, and two-year trajectories on cognitive and speech-recognition measures in a well-characterized sample of children with mild-to-severe hearing loss. With the planned preregistration and sharing of de-identified data and analysis code, these resources will support secondary analyses and model testing beyond the immediate hypotheses.

**Limitations on scientific value:** The proposal's translational ambition—to inform individualized amplification and habilitation—is stated as a goal, but the text does not specify how the resulting knowledge will be translated into clinical recommendations. This is legitimate for hypothesis-driven observational research, but it bounds the immediate practical contribution. The knowledge attained is likely to identify explanatory pathways rather than deliver a validated clinical decision rule. Additionally, the exclusion of children with secondary disabilities and nonverbal cognitive scores more than 1.5 SD below age norms enhances internal validity but limits generalizability to the full range of children seen in audiology clinics. The proposal does not discuss whether these excluded subgroups represent populations for whom the proposed cognitive mechanisms might operate differently, leaving a gap in the population to which the findings can be generalized. Finally, although the Speech Intelligibility Index (SII) is used as an index of aided audibility, the proposal does not explicitly plan to test whether individually measured perceptual weights predict speech recognition better than the SII's default importance function, a comparison that is available within the design and would strengthen translational relevance.

### 2. Innovation

**Rating: B (Good) | Confidence: B (High)**

The proposal offers genuine methodological and integrative innovation. The team's prior work is used effectively to motivate each enhancement.

First, the refinement of the perceptual-weighting method is a clear methodological advance. Reducing measurement time from approximately four hours to under 30 minutes while retaining sensitivity to individual differences is a concrete achievement that makes developmental and clinical applications feasible. The use of logistic regression with multiple band combinations and the inclusion of both nonwords and real words are well-justified design choices. Comparing CNH, CHL, and normal-hearing adults adds a developmental dimension absent from existing band-importance literature for this population.

Second, the spectral-ripple task design directly addresses confounds identified in the team's pilot work. The shift to narrowband carriers (500 and 2000 Hz center frequencies), multiple ripple densities, trial-level amplitude roving, and the systematic manipulation of interstimulus interval represent substantive methodological improvements that isolate spectral resolution more cleanly than previous combined spectrotemporal measures.

Third, the systematic comparison of speech-shaped noise and a two-talker masker within the same children, with cognitive mediators measured, adds a mechanistic dimension to the common clinical observation that children with hearing loss are particularly susceptible to competing speech. The planned path models testing immediate audibility, language-mediated, and attention-mediated routes across both types of masking extend beyond existing cross-sectional studies.

Fourth, the integration of these components into a single longitudinal project with preregistered mediation and growth models is itself an innovative contribution. No existing study has simultaneously characterized spectral resolution, perceptual weighting, and selective attention in children with hearing loss over multiple time points.

**Limitations on innovation:** The individual components—spectral ripple, band-importance measurement, cognitive tests, and speech-in-noise paradigms—are each established in other populations or smaller samples. The innovation lies primarily in their integration, developmental application, and improved measurement efficiency, rather than in proposing previously undescribed mechanisms or theoretical constructs. The theoretical framework (Figure 2) separates selective attention, working memory, and language as contributors to spectral resolution, perceptual weighting, and informational masking, but these constructs overlap substantially. The proposal does not clearly articulate how its model differs from or improves upon existing cognitive-hearing frameworks such as the Ease of Language Understanding (ELU) model or the Framework for Understanding Effortful Listening (FUEL). The degree of conceptual novelty relative to these established models is not fully established in the supplied text. The absence of a true within-subject developmental manipulation—CNH and CHL are separate groups—also constrains causal inferences about the interplay of maturation and hearing-loss experience.

### 3. Rigor

**Rating: B (Good) | Confidence: C (Moderate)**

The design is largely well-matched to the research questions, with several thoughtful elements that address known limitations of prior work. The research team demonstrates methodological sophistication across all three Aims, and the stated commitment to preregistration, independent scoring of a subset of speech responses, and sharing of code and data supports reproducibility.

**Aim 1:** The shift to narrowband spectral-ripple stimuli with octave-wide carriers, two ripple densities, and trial-level amplitude roving is well-justified. The interstimulus-interval manipulation (0.4, 1.2, 3.0 s) in Experiment 1b is a strong within-experiment test of the cognitive-demand hypothesis. The recognition that weak interval effects would serve as useful evidence against a memory-intensive account shows methodological self-awareness. The longitudinal component (Experiment 1c) appropriately compares models with and without baseline spectral resolution and considers bidirectional relationships.

**Aim 2:** The logistic-regression approach to estimating individual perceptual weights from 300–350 trials is internally coherent. The inclusion of both nonwords (to reduce lexical support) and real words (to connect to everyday listening) is well-grounded. The plan to validate CNH weights against published adult importance functions provides an external reference. The explicit treatment of stable weights as a meaningful alternative outcome is a valuable precommitment.

**Aim 3:** The two-masker comparison (speech-shaped noise versus two-talker masker) is a well-chosen contrast for separating energetic from informational masking. The interleaved adaptive tracks avoid order confounds. The use of the AZ-Bio pediatric sentence corpus is appropriate for the age range, and the contingency to switch to a 71%-correct target if 50%-correct tracks show poor reliability is prudent.

**Limitations on rigor, with implications for intended inferences:**

A central vulnerability is the construct validity of the spectral-ripple task as a measure of sensory spectral resolution in children with hearing loss, tested at reduced sensation levels through headphones that do not replicate the aided listening experience of everyday life. The proposal acknowledges that poor thresholds may reflect sensory resolution, cognitive demands, or both. The interval-ISI manipulation tests this indirectly but does not parse the relative contributions of attention, working memory, and true spectral resolving power in a way that cleanly attributes variance to each source. The cross-sectional associational design of Experiments 1a and 1b—and the observational longitudinal design of 1c—cannot fully satisfy the strong assumptions required for causal interpretation of the planned path analyses. The proposal's statement that failure to find predicted mediation would "still characterize the sensory-cognitive relation" is accurate, but the characterization will remain associational.

For Aim 2, the hearing-aid simulator is described as permitting precise control of band levels, but the proposal does not explain how the simulator will handle individual audiometric configurations, nonlinear signal processing typical of modern hearing aids (compression, noise management, frequency lowering), or verification that the simulated output corresponds to a plausible aided percept. For a sample spanning mild-to-severe losses of mixed etiology, the mapping from prescribed sensation level to actual band-by-band audibility at the cochlea is nontrivial. If certain bands remain inaudible or uncomfortably loud, the resulting weight estimates will conflate audibility and perceptual importance in ways the logistic regression cannot automatically separate. The proposal acknowledges that poorly audible bands are predicted to contribute little, but this prediction itself makes the interpretation of between-child weight differences ambiguous.

For Aim 3, the exclusive reliance on PEBL Eriksen Flanker and Switcher tasks to operationalize selective attention is a significant vulnerability. The pilot correlations (r ≈ 0.51–0.52) with two-talker SNR50 in 40 CNH children are encouraging but preliminary. The reliability and construct validity of these computer-based inhibition and switching tasks in children with hearing loss who may have language delays is less securely documented than for CNH. The proposal notes the possibility of using other PEBL tasks as a contingency but does not specify which tasks or how they would be calibrated across the 6–14-year age span of the longitudinal sample. If the attention measure changes in its psychometric properties or conceptual referent across development, inferences about the growth of attention may be confounded by measurement non-invariance.

A cross-cutting concern is that the same cognitive measures (Flanker, Switcher, AWMA subtests, PPVT/WASI vocabulary) serve as predictors, mediators, and covariates across all three Aims. If these measures share substantial method variance, or if their factor structure differs systematically between CNH and CHL, the same interpretive ambiguity will propagate across Aims, potentially producing a coherent but misleading pattern of results that survives the planned internal checks.

The longitudinal analyses in Experiment 3c face a small-cell-size challenge. Following 80 CHL over 24 months with three measurement waves and estimating individual slopes for multiple mediating variables simultaneously places heavy demands on the data. Random slopes for cognitive and language trajectories, measurement error in the mediators, and collinearity among repeated cognitive assessments may produce unstable parameter estimates or boundary solutions. The proposal does not describe sensitivity analyses for model convergence failures or specification checks.

The power analyses state detectable effect sizes (d > 0.2 for group differences in Aim 1; d > 0.10–0.15 for Aim 2) but do not report the fixed-effect and variance specifications used in the simulations, which limits independent evaluation of whether these targets are realistic given the expected between-participant and residual variability in a pediatric clinical sample. The absence of pilot test-retest reliability estimates for the band-importance method in children compounds this uncertainty.

### 4. Feasibility

**Rating: B (Good) | Confidence: B (High)**

The proposal demonstrates a high level of operational planning. The five-year schedule thoughtfully sequences the Aims, allowing core personnel to gain proficiency with shared assessments and the spectral-ripple paradigm (Aim 1) before layering on the more technically novel band-importance procedure (Aim 2) and the two-talker masking paradigm (Aim 3). The use of shared participants and core cognitive-linguistic assessments across Aims is an efficient design that substantially reduces the screening and recruitment burden.

The institutional expertise at Boys Town National Research Hospital and the University of Iowa is strong, with established participant databases, experience in pediatric audiology and cognitive testing, and a track record of longitudinal analyses. The total number of subject visits (955) and data-collection hours (2,100) appear realistic for a two-site study over five years, and the per-subject visit duration of 2–3 hours is credible for this type of protocol.

The contingency planning is mature. The proposal identifies critical dependencies—such as the usability of cognitive data in hearing-impaired children and potential developmental inappropriateness of attention tasks—and provides concrete alternatives. The explicit treatment of stable perceptual weights as a meaningful alternative outcome in Aim 2 is a well-considered hedge against the primary hypothesis.

**Limitations on feasibility:** The management section does not describe a formal mechanism for ensuring procedural fidelity across the two sites. While both institutions are highly experienced and the tasks are largely computer-administered, systematic cross-site procedural drift is a recognized risk in multi-site behavioral research. The absence of a named cross-site coordinator or a joint training plan is a minor but real dependency for data consistency that is not explicitly managed. Additionally, the final project phase (Years 4–5) concentrates substantial effort, with longitudinal follow-up for Aims 2c and 3c occurring concurrently with cross-sectional collection for Aims 2a/2b and 3a/3b. The longitudinal cohorts require precise scheduling to complete third visits before children age out of the 6–12-year window, and the oldest children recruited later in the project are at risk of exceeding the age limit. Without a detailed tracking system, this could lead to selective loss of longitudinal data from the most linguistically mature participants, subtly biasing the developmental analyses.

### 5. Ethics and Safety

**Rating: B (Good) | Confidence: B (High)**

The proposal establishes an ethically sound framework for behavioral and psychoacoustic research with pediatric participants. The described activities—listening tasks, cognitive assessments, and probe-microphone measurements—are appropriately characterized as minimal risk. Three concrete procedural safeguards are specified: limiting auditory signal levels below damaging thresholds via hardware and software controls, and managing probe-microphone discomfort through removal and repositioning. These address the primary physical discomforts inherent to the study.

The plan for informed consent and assent is age-appropriate. The stated procedure that children and caregivers complete applicable consent and assent with questions addressed at visits indicates an active communication process beyond mere documentation. The single-IRB arrangement for participating institutions is a recognized and efficient oversight mechanism for multi-site research.

Data management and privacy protections are clearly delineated, including access controls, password-protected storage, and a concrete commitment not to identify children in publications. The data-sharing plan includes responsible-use provisions: de-identified data shared via the Open Science Framework under agreements that restrict re-identification and require secure use and destruction after analysis. The sharing of analysis code via GitHub supports transparency without compromising participant identity. Audio and video recordings are acknowledged, which is necessary for managing these sensitive data types.

The plan for handling clinically relevant incidental findings is ethically commendable. The provision that "clinically relevant audiological, language, or cognitive results may be shared with the child's care or education team" with caregiver permission establishes a pre-planned pathway to return potentially beneficial information to participants.

**Limitations:** The ethics section does not address the cumulative burden on children who participate in multiple experiments across the longitudinal design. A child with hearing loss in the longitudinal cohorts could complete up to nine separate visits and over 17 hours of testing across two years. While total hours are moderate, the number of visits and aggregate cognitive demand on a child with hearing loss merit explicit consideration in the consent and assent materials. The decision process for sharing results with care teams—who determines clinical relevance, the criteria for that determination, and how results will be communicated to avoid misinterpretation—lacks operational detail. These are planning details that would feasibly be refined during IRB review and do not signal an unethical project, but they represent areas where the ethical framing is currently less complete than ideal.

### 6. Management

**Rating: B (Good) | Confidence: B (High)**

The management plan is logically structured with clearly defined responsibilities, resources, and schedule. The five-year timeline is realistic, with Year 1 dedicated to personnel hiring, IRB approvals, and software finalization before data collection begins. The staggered launch of Aims is a deliberate and practical choice that allows learning and refinement.

The table of experiments provides clear accounting of total visits (955) and data-collection hours (2,100). Personnel allocation is appropriately scaled to these demands. The commitment to preregistration, shared data, and code release is embedded in the management structure rather than treated as an afterthought. The plan for independent scoring of a subset of speech responses is a proportionate quality-assurance measure for the primary outcome.

The two-site recruitment strategy—approximately half the sample from each institution—balances the load and draws on complementary participant databases. The single-IRB arrangement streamlines regulatory oversight.

**Limitations:** As noted under Feasibility, the proposal does not describe a formal mechanism for cross-site protocol coordination, such as a named coordinator, joint training sessions, or periodic fidelity monitoring. The concentration of longitudinal follow-up in Years 4–5, concurrently with active cross-sectional data collection and with strict age-window constraints, creates an operational pinch point that is acknowledged but not fully detailed. The plan for tracking children approaching the age limit and for replacing those who age out before completing follow-up visits is stated in principle but lacks procedural specificity.

## Cross-Cutting Observations

Several patterns emerge across the criteria. First, the proposal consistently shows methodological sophistication and awareness of limitations in prior work, but the inferential ambitions of the path models and growth models sometimes exceed what the specified measurements can reliably support. The observational longitudinal design is appropriate for the developmental questions, but the causal language in places implies stronger inferences than the design warrants. Second, the integrative nature of the project—using shared measures across Aims—is efficient, but it creates a vulnerability: if the cognitive battery has unrecognized psychometric limitations in children with hearing loss, this limitation propagates across all Aims. Third, the proposal's treatment of alternative outcomes as informative is a genuine strength that protects the scientific value even if the preferred hypotheses are not confirmed.

---

## Dimensions Requiring Attention Before Implementation

The following areas merit attention before data collection begins, as they affect the strength of the inferences the project can support:

1. **Pilot test-retest reliability for the band-importance procedure in children.** The claimed sensitivity depends on the precision of individual weight estimates. Without a reliability coefficient or standard error of measurement from the target age range, the power calculations rest on untested assumptions.

2. **Psychometric evaluation of the attention battery in children with hearing loss.** Explicit pilot data on the reliability (test-retest, internal consistency) and convergent-discriminant validity of the Flanker and Switcher tasks in CHL, and a documented plan for age-appropriate alternatives if these measures perform poorly, would reduce the risk of measurement non-invariance confounding developmental inferences.

3. **Specification of hearing-aid simulator parameters and verification.** A protocol for mapping individual audiograms to the simulator, verifying aided audibility per band, and handling cases where prescribed sensation levels cannot be achieved without discomfort, would clarify the interpretation of perceptual-weight differences between children.

4. **Longitudinal tracking protocol for aging-out participants.** A detailed system for scheduling follow-up visits within the age window and for documenting reasons for attrition would protect against systematic loss of older, more linguistically mature children from the longitudinal analyses.

---

## Recommendation

**Conditional support.** This is a well-designed project addressing an important question with largely appropriate methods and a realistic management plan. The scientific value and innovation are solid, and the feasibility is high given the institutional resources and expertise. The conditions for support concern the psychometric groundwork needed to ensure that the planned path models and growth models can support the intended mechanistic inferences. Specifically, before scaling to the full longitudinal data collection, the investigators should provide pilot evidence on the test-retest reliability of the band-importance procedure in children and the psychometric properties of the attention tasks in CHL. These data can be collected during the initial project phase without redesigning the study. With this additional foundation, the project is well-positioned to deliver the developmental characterization it promises, even if the specific predictive pathways require revision in light of the data. The proposal's own commitment to treating alternative outcomes as informative provides insurance that the investment will yield valuable knowledge under a range of empirical scenarios.

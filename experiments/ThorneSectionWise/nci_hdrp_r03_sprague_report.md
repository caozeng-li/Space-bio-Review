# Comprehensive Proposal Review

## Overall Assessment

This is a proposal for an observational retrospective study comparing the clinical performance of screening with combined digital mammography and tomosynthesis (DM/DBT) against tomosynthesis with synthesized 2D views (s2D/DBT) among women screened at seven facilities in Vermont and Pennsylvania. The central question is of high practical importance: as breast imaging services transition from acquiring conventional digital mammograms alongside DBT to synthesizing the 2D image from the DBT acquisition, does screening performance—recall rates, cancer detection, sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative intervals—change in clinically consequential ways? The investigators exploit a natural experiment in which six community facilities and one academic medical center uniformly adopted s2D/DBT, creating consecutive before-and-after cohorts totaling over 185,000 screening examinations.

The proposal's primary strength is its direct engagement with a question that matters for patients, radiologists, health systems, and payers. The technology change is motivated by dose reduction, but the clinical trade-offs remain incompletely characterized. By linking screening records to pathology reports and state cancer registries with twelve-month follow-up, the study can assess not merely the number of recalls and detected cancers but also sensitivity and false-negative rates—metrics that require complete outcome ascertainment and are rarely available in single-institution reader studies or clinical series. The focus on lesion-specific detection (invasive cancer versus DCIS, benign biopsy subtypes) and on s2D-induced shifts in BI-RADS density classification adds nuance directly relevant to the benefit-harm balance of screening and to density-notification policies.

The principal limitation is the before-and-after observational design itself. The comparison of DM/DBT examinations from an earlier period with s2D/DBT examinations from a later period at the same facilities cannot separate the effect of the imaging technology from concurrent temporal trends in the screened population, radiologist interpretive thresholds, biopsy practices, or underlying disease incidence. This limitation is inherent in the natural-experiment approach and is not a design flaw per se, but it constrains the strength of causal attribution. The proposal acknowledges the observational nature of the study but does not plan to model time trends, examine radiologist learning trajectories, or test the stability assumptions that the before-and-after comparison requires. These omissions are most consequential for the analyses of DCIS detection, benign biopsy rates, and density classification, where small secular shifts could be mistaken for modality effects. The absence of a concurrent control group also means that the density analysis confronts a circularity problem: if s2D/DBT alters density classification, and density is simultaneously used as a covariate in performance models, the interpretation of adjusted analyses becomes ambiguous.

These trade-offs shape the overall recommendation. The question is important, the opportunity to study it in a multicenter real-world setting is valuable, and the outcome ascertainment infrastructure is strong. The analytical framework is appropriate for the data structure. However, the current plan does not adequately address the inferential threats posed by the single-arm before-and-after design. With additional analytical provisions—explicit modeling of temporal trends, tests of population stability, sensitivity analyses for density mediation, and a clearly documented management plan—the proposal could support its stated inferences more convincingly. In its present form, the design limitations create substantive uncertainty about whether observed performance differences should be attributed to the imaging modality or to background change.

## Criterion-by-Criterion Evaluation

### Scientific Value

**Rating: A (excellent)**

**Confidence: A (very high)**

The importance of the question is well-established and requires little defense. Breast tomosynthesis has been adopted rapidly in the United States, and many facilities have transitioned from acquiring a conventional DM view alongside DBT to generating a synthesized 2D image from the DBT data. The dose reduction is clear; the clinical consequences are not. The proposal identifies a genuine evidence gap: the existing literature is dominated by reader studies in enriched or laboratory settings, small single-institution clinical series, and comparisons of DBT against DM alone rather than DM/DBT against s2D/DBT. A multicenter, population-based evaluation with cancer registry linkage addresses that gap directly.

The study design adds value on several dimensions that go beyond the simple head-to-head comparison:

- **Complete outcome ascertainment.** Linkage to state cancer registries and pathology records, with a minimum twelve-month follow-up window, permits valid estimation of sensitivity and false-negative rates—metrics that are central to understanding screening effectiveness and harm but are unavailable in most published series. The ability to identify interval cancers diagnosed after a negative screen is a distinguishing strength of the PROSPR infrastructure and represents a material advance over work that reports only recall rates and cancer detection rates without denominator follow-up.

- **Multicenter, demographically diverse setting.** The inclusion of six community facilities and one academic medical center serving rural and urban populations, with different demographic compositions, enhances the real-world generalizability of the findings. The Vermont data include essentially complete capture of screening within a defined geographic region; the Pennsylvania data add a more racially diverse population. This heterogeneity is a scientific asset rather than a nuisance.

- **Clinically nuanced endpoints.** The explicit hypothesis that s2D/DBT may differentially affect detection of invasive cancer versus DCIS, and may alter benign biopsy rates and breast density classifications, connects the technology evaluation to the downstream clinical consequences that determine the net benefit of screening. A modality that reduces recall without missing cancers is desirable; one that reduces recall by missing subtle lesions or by systematically downgrading density classification—potentially delaying supplemental screening recommendations—may not be.

A limitation worth noting is that the attainable knowledge is descriptive and comparative rather than mechanistic. The study will tell us whether s2D/DBT performs differently from DM/DBT in the studied settings but not why—is it the different appearance of the synthesized 2D image, the reader's adaptation to the new image type, or the elimination of the DM acquisition step? This does not diminish the practical value of the comparison, but it bounds the scope of the contribution.

### Innovation

**Rating: B (good)**

**Confidence: A (very high)**

The proposal does not introduce a novel technology, experimental design, or analytical method. Its originality lies in the rigor, scale, and clinical specificity with which it evaluates an existing practice change. The increments beyond the prior work described in the proposal are clearly identifiable:

- **Scale and setting.** Prior work on s2D/DBT is characterized as coming from reader studies and small single-institution clinical series. The proposed study size—over 185,000 examinations, approximately 1,100 cancers, 22 radiologists across seven facilities—is substantially larger and includes community practice, where most screening occurs.

- **Outcome completeness.** As described under scientific value, the ability to calculate sensitivity and specificity with complete follow-up distinguishes this work from most prior evaluations.

- **Natural-experiment design with facility-level uniform adoption.** The before-and-after comparison avoids the confounding by indication that would plague a contemporaneous comparison in which clinicians selected patients for one modality or another based on perceived risk. This design choice, while introducing temporal confounding, strengthens the internal comparability of the two exposure groups in ways that cross-sectional observational studies cannot.

- **Focused density hypothesis.** The objective to quantify how s2D/DBT alters BI-RADS density classification is a practically oriented contribution that prior work at this scale has not addressed. It connects the technology evaluation to clinical workflow (density notification, supplemental screening) in a way that matters for policy.

These are meaningful increments. The proposal contributes a necessary and timely evidence base for a technology already in clinical use. The originality is proportional to the identified evidence gap, and the stated novelty claims are appropriate rather than oversold.

The limitation is that the increments are extensions of established methods applied to a new comparison, rather than conceptual, methodological, or technological breakthroughs. This is the norm for pragmatic clinical effectiveness research and is not a weakness; it simply calibrates the innovation rating.

### Rigor

**Rating: C (moderate)**

**Confidence: B (high)**

The proposal's design has substantial methodological strengths, but the central inferential threat—temporal confounding in a before-and-after comparison—is acknowledged only in passing and is not addressed in the analytical strategy. The result is a plan that can describe performance differences between periods but has limited ability to attribute those differences to the imaging modality.

#### Strengths

**Common facilities, radiologists, and equipment constrain important confounders.** The design uses the same seven facilities, 22 radiologists, and Hologic Selenia Dimensions equipment across the two periods. Radiologist skill, reading style, equipment resolution, and facility-level referral and follow-up practices are held constant in ways that a cross-sectional comparison of different sites could not achieve. This is a genuine strength of the before-and-after approach.

**Prospectively collected registry data with standardized elements.** The PROSPR network has collected standardized screening and outcomes information since 2011, with common data elements and established quality-control procedures. This reduces measurement error and ensures that the variables needed for the analysis are available in comparable form across sites and periods.

**Cancer registry linkage for outcome ascertainment.** This is the design feature that most distinguishes the proposal from typical clinical series. By capturing cancers diagnosed outside the screening facility within the twelve-month follow-up window, the study can compute sensitivity and false-negative rates rather than only recall and detection rates.

**Explicit cohort definitions.** Transitional examinations combining DM, DBT, and s2D views are excluded, as are women with personal breast cancer history. These criteria reduce contamination between exposure groups and remove a subgroup with distinct imaging protocols.

**Appropriate clustered analysis plan.** The use of generalized estimating equations with the Miglioretti-Heagerty approach for non-nested clustering within women, radiologists, and facilities is methodologically sound and reflects the group's established expertise.

**Empirically grounded power calculations.** The detectable differences are based on DM/DBT performance estimates from the same PROSPR data that will supply the study sample, lending credibility to the projections.

#### Limitations

**Before-and-after design without a concurrent control group.** This is the single most important methodological limitation. The DM/DBT examinations are drawn from an earlier calendar period and the s2D/DBT examinations from a later period. Any difference observed could reflect the imaging modality, temporal trends in the screened population (age, risk profile, prior screening history), secular changes in biopsy thresholds or pathologic diagnostic criteria, or radiologists' accumulating experience with DBT interpretation.

The proposal acknowledges the observational design but treats the stability of the served populations as a reasonable assumption and proposes statistical adjustment for measured patient-level confounders (age, race, density, family history, screening interval). This strategy addresses case-mix shifts in measured variables but cannot control for unmeasured temporal drift in disease prevalence, biopsy practice, or interpretive criteria. The assumption that these factors remained stable over a period spanning up to six years (2011-2017) is a strong one and is not tested or defended with evidence about the specific facilities and populations.

**Radiologist experience confounded with the modality change.** The same 22 radiologists interpret examinations in both periods, but their experience with DBT accumulates over time. The DM/DBT period includes their earlier DBT experience; the s2D/DBT period includes both accumulated DBT experience and any specific learning curve with the synthesized 2D image. The proposal does not plan to model time trends, learning effects, or radiologist-specific temporal trajectories. Any improvement in recall or detection over time could be attributed to the modality when it partly reflects radiologist learning or secular improvement in interpretive performance. This is especially problematic for Aim 2, where shifts in DCIS versus invasive cancer detection and benign biopsy rates could reflect evolving interpretive thresholds rather than image characteristics.

**No plan to test the stability assumption.** The analytical strategy as described treats the before-and-after comparison as a simple group contrast with covariate adjustment. It does not include:
- An analysis of temporal trends in key outcomes before and after the transition to check for pre-existing drift.
- Sensitivity analyses that restrict the comparison to narrower time windows around the transition date.
- Explicit modeling of radiologist-level time effects or modality-by-experience interactions.
- Comparison of patient characteristics across the two periods to verify the assumption of population stability.

These omissions are material because they leave the study unable to distinguish modality effects from background change, even probabilistically.

**Density circularity.** The proposal hypothesizes that s2D/DBT may produce fewer dense classifications (i.e., a systematic downgrading of BI-RADS density categories) and frames this as a potential artifact of the synthesized image appearance. At the same time, breast density is used as a covariate in the Aim 1 performance models. If s2D/DBT does alter density classification, density is an intermediate variable on the pathway from modality to recall or cancer detection, not a confounder. Adjusting for density in this setting would answer a different question—the direct effect of modality not mediated through density—rather than the total effect, which is arguably what clinicians and policymakers want to know. The proposal does not discuss this mediation problem or specify whether density-adjusted and unadjusted models will be interpreted differently.

**Power for subgroup analyses not established.** The main-effect power calculations appear adequate for recall and overall cancer detection. For the lesion-specific and molecular-subtype analyses in Aim 2, and for interaction tests by density or age, no power is reported. With approximately 1,100 cancers total distributed across multiple subtypes and strata, the study may be underpowered for the more granular comparisons that give the proposal its clinical nuance.

**Unspecified treatment of transition periods.** The exact adoption dates and any washout window around the transition are not specified. A facility might phase in s2D over weeks or months, and screening examinations during that transition could be classified inconsistently if handling rules are not explicit.

### Feasibility

**Rating: B (good)**

**Confidence: B (high)**

The core data-assembly activities are low-risk because they draw directly on the established PROSPR infrastructure and the same team's prior pooled analyses. The study requires no new patient recruitment, no new imaging, and no new equipment. The eligibility criteria are clearly operationalized, making cohort assembly from existing electronic records straightforward.

**Strengths:**
- **Immediate data access.** The Vermont and Pennsylvania centers have collected standardized screening and outcomes data since 2011, including the variables needed for the proposed analyses. The planned sample sizes are directly derived from annual screening volumes and documented adoption dates, so their availability is realistic.
- **Uniform equipment.** All facilities use Hologic Selenia Dimensions systems and C-View reconstruction, removing vendor-level technology confounding.
- **Established collaboration.** The team has previously pooled PROSPR data for publications with overlapping methodology, demonstrating that multi-site harmonization and secure data transfer are achievable.
- **Near-complete population capture.** The passive-consent model in Vermont yields approximately 95% participation, and the Pennsylvania waiver supports similar completeness, making selection bias within the eligible population unlikely to be severe.

**Limitations and critical dependencies:**

- **Temporal confounding as a feasibility constraint.** The before-and-after design's reliance on population and practice stability is the most important dependency affecting whether the Aims can be delivered as stated. If the screened population, referral patterns, or biopsy practices changed materially over the study window, the observed performance differences will conflate modality effects with secular trends, and the study's contribution will be more descriptive than evaluative.

- **Follow-up completeness across modality periods.** The sensitivity and false-negative analyses require that cancers diagnosed within twelve months of a negative screen are captured equally well in both the DM/DBT and s2D/DBT periods. The proposal describes the registry linkage infrastructure but does not discuss whether linkage completeness or timeliness varied over the study period. Differential outcome ascertainment—for example, if registry reporting improved over time—could bias sensitivity estimates in favor of the later period.

- **Pathology harmonization.** The Vermont and Pennsylvania centers use different pathology data pathways (standardized abstraction forms versus direct electronic pathology access). Harmonizing benign lesion classifications across these sources requires shared coding rules and quality checks. The proposal references College of American Pathologists standards and prior collaboration but does not describe a specific harmonization protocol or inter-site validation step. The risk of misclassification is modest but could affect benign-lesion rate comparisons.

- **Statistical power for secondary aims.** As noted under rigor, the power calculations address main effects but not the subgroup and interaction analyses that give the proposal its clinical nuance. The event counts for rare subtypes may be insufficient for stable inference.

### Ethics and Safety

**Rating: B (good)**

**Confidence: C (moderate)**

The study design avoids most physical research risks by using data generated during routine clinical care. No experimental imaging is performed, no radiation exposure is added, and no study-specific recruitment interactions occur. The passive-consent and waiver arrangements are common for retrospective record-based studies of this type and, if properly implemented, are likely to meet regulatory standards for minimal-risk research.

**Strengths:**
- **No incremental physical risk.** Women receive whichever screening modality their facility offers at the time of examination. The research does not alter clinical care.
- **Low psychological burden.** The absence of individual randomization and study-specific recruitment reduces the risk of research-induced anxiety or coercion compared with a prospective intervention trial.
- **Near-complete population inclusion reduces selection bias.** The 95% participation rate in Vermont and the Pennsylvania waiver support validity of the findings.

**Limitations:**
- **Underspecified consent justification.** The proposal states two different arrangements—passive consent with opt-out in Vermont and a waiver of informed consent in Pennsylvania—but provides no rationale for the waiver or description of the institutional review board criteria applied. The text does not indicate whether Pennsylvania patients were notified that their records would be included in this multi-center pooled analysis or whether any opportunity to object existed. For a site serving a high proportion of Black women, a population with justified historical concerns about research conducted without explicit consent, the absence of documented justification is a substantive gap.

- **Data governance underspecified.** The proposal mentions secure transfer of exam-level Pennsylvania data to Vermont for pooling but provides no detail on the data-sharing agreement, de-identification standards, security protections, or provisions for data retention and destruction. The combination of detailed imaging data, pathology results, and registry outcomes linked at the individual level warrants clear privacy protections that are not described.

- **Downstream safety implications not addressed as ethical considerations.** Changes in recall rates, cancer-detection patterns, or density classification could affect the number of women undergoing additional imaging, biopsy, or supplemental screening recommendations. The proposal treats these as scientific outcomes but does not discuss them as potential risks requiring ethical consideration or monitoring.

These limitations do not indicate unethical conduct, but they prevent a confident judgment about the ethical adequacy of the consent and data-governance arrangements as documented in this proposal. The confidence rating reflects this evidentiary gap rather than a judgment that the practices are deficient.

### Management

**Rating: C (moderate)**

**Confidence: C (moderate)**

The management assessment is constrained by substantial omissions in the supplied proposal text. The scientific team's expertise is clearly aligned with the proposed work—the PI has led prior PROSPR analyses using the same data and methods—but the documentation of how that expertise will be deployed, coordinated, and scheduled is minimal.

**Strengths:**
- **Relevant team expertise.** The PI and co-investigators have published the preliminary work that motivates this proposal and have demonstrated the ability to pool PROSPR data, execute clustered statistical models, and link screening records to pathology and registry outcomes. The R03 mechanism implies a small, focused team, and the alignment between the team's record and the proposed tasks supports delivery.
- **Leveraged infrastructure.** The project builds on existing PROSPR data-collection and linkage systems rather than requiring new infrastructure development, reducing management complexity.

**Limitations:**
- **No personnel assignments specified.** The proposal does not identify who will handle secure data transfer, pathology linkage harmonization, registry matching, statistical programming, or the complex GEE modeling with the Miglioretti-Heagerty correlation structure. These are specialized tasks requiring distinct skills, and the absence of named responsibilities makes it impossible to judge whether the team's composition matches the work.

- **No schedule or milestones.** No timeline is provided for data extraction, harmonization, quality checks, analysis, or dissemination. The tasks are logically sequential and likely feasible within the R03 timeframe given that data already exist, but without a schedule the management plan cannot be evaluated for realism.

- **No coordination protocol described.** The pooling of data from two centers requires coordination of data-transfer timing, variable harmonization, quality checks, and iterative analysis. The proposal mentions secure transfer but provides no detail on these coordination steps, leaving the practical management of the multi-site work unspecified.

- **No harmonization protocol for pathology or registry data.** As noted under feasibility, the two centers use different pathology data pathways, and the proposal does not describe a specific plan for standardizing lesion classifications or verifying follow-up completeness across sites and periods.

These omissions do not demonstrate incapacity—the team's track record and the leveraged infrastructure strongly imply that the work can be done—but they leave the management arrangements substantially undocumented. The confidence rating reflects the limited evidence available in the supplied text.

## Recommendation

**Current proposal insufficient for support (revise).**

The proposal addresses an important question with high scientific value and genuine innovation in the scale, setting, and outcome completeness of its evaluation. The multicenter natural-experiment design with uniform facility-level adoption and cancer registry linkage is a strong platform for assessing the real-world performance of s2D/DBT. However, the before-and-after design without a concurrent control group creates a central inferential threat—temporal confounding—that the current analytical plan does not adequately address. This limitation is most consequential for the lesion-specific and density-classification analyses that give the proposal its clinical distinctiveness.

A revised proposal could substantially strengthen its inferential position by incorporating some or all of the following:

1. **Model temporal trends explicitly.** Include time as a variable in the analytical models, examine outcome trajectories before and after the transition, and conduct sensitivity analyses restricting comparisons to narrower time windows around the adoption dates.

2. **Test population and practice stability.** Compare patient demographics, risk profiles, biopsy rates, and supplemental screening use across the two periods to verify the assumptions underlying the before-and-after comparison.

3. **Address density mediation.** Specify whether the performance models will treat density as a confounder or an intermediate variable, present both adjusted and unadjusted estimates with clear interpretation, and discuss the implications of any s2D-induced density shift for the performance comparisons.

4. **Document management arrangements.** Provide personnel assignments, a timeline, a pathology-harmonization protocol, and a plan for verifying follow-up completeness across modality periods.

5. **Justify consent and data-governance arrangements.** Supply the rationale for the Pennsylvania waiver, describe privacy protections, and address the proportionality of the consent models in the populations served.

The scientific question remains worth answering, and the investigators are well positioned to answer it. With a revised analytical strategy that confronts temporal confounding more directly, the proposal would be supportable.

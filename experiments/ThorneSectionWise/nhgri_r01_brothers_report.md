# Comprehensive Research Proposal Review

**Proposal Under Review:** A multi-site, mixed-methods investigation into the ethical and regulatory challenges of networked biorepositories and the development of evidence-informed governance recommendations.

**Review Date:** 2026-10-28

---

## Overall Assessment

This proposal addresses a timely and practically urgent question: how the shift from single-institution biobanks to multi-party, networked sharing arrangements creates distinctive ethical and governance challenges, and what responses those challenges require. The project is ambitious in scope, combining a systematic inventory of existing networks, stakeholder interviews, longitudinal participant observation of five contrasting networks, and an integrative normative phase that develops and tests governance recommendations with practitioners. The central contribution would be a richly textured, empirically grounded account of how ethical tensions arise and are managed in networked governance, together with a set of recommendations that are both normatively reasoned and stakeholder-vetted.

The proposal demonstrates substantial strengths across multiple criteria. The research question is important, the multi-method design is well-justified, the case selection promises instructive variation, and the translational architecture linking observation to recommendation-testing is a model of integrated bioethics research. The investigative team possesses the requisite expertise, and the management structure assigns clear responsibilities. Critical access to the five observation sites has been secured, providing a solid foundation for the longitudinal fieldwork.

However, material limitations exist that affect the scope and force of the attainable contribution. The most significant is the deliberate exclusion of specimen donor perspectives from the empirical work. The study relies on community advisory board members and institutional informants as proxies for donor interests. This is an acknowledged boundary, but it means the governance recommendations—however well-reasoned and practitioner-vetted—will rest on an incomplete empirical foundation regarding what donors actually value, understand, or prefer. A secondary concern is that the operational details for managing ethically sensitive aspects of the research itself, particularly consent in transient observational settings and protection of dissenting participants in potentially identifiable professional contexts, remain under-specified in the supplied materials. The normative framework guiding the ethical analysis in Aim 3 is also described in general terms, leaving the grounds for conceptual innovation less transparent than they could be.

These limitations do not invalidate the project. The descriptive and comparative aims are well-supported, and the translational deliverables—a typology of network configurations, detailed case narratives of governance in action, and practitioner-informed recommendations—represent a genuine and useful contribution. The study would advance scholarship on research governance and provide practical guidance to institutions navigating the complexities of network-based precision medicine. The recommendation below reflects this balance: the project is supportable in its current scope, but the strength and generality of its normative claims are contingent on premises that warrant explicit acknowledgment and management.

## Criterion-by-Criterion Evaluation

### 1. Scientific Value: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

**Importance of the question and nature of the attainable contribution**

The question is both scientifically and practically important. Precision medicine depends on the ability to aggregate and share biospecimens and associated data across institutional boundaries, yet the ethical frameworks, consent models, and oversight mechanisms that govern individual biobanks were not designed for multi-site, networked arrangements. As the proposal notes, these networks create distinctive tensions around consent, donor control, data access, privacy, return of results, and accountability that existing single-institution governance cannot readily resolve. A systematic, comparative empirical investigation of how these tensions actually manifest—and how they are deliberated and addressed in practice—promises knowledge that is currently lacking.

The attainable contribution is multi-layered. At the descriptive level, the project would produce an inventory and typology of network configurations (Aim 1a), a coded corpus of stakeholder perspectives and artefacts (Aim 1b), and detailed case narratives of governance deliberation in contrasting settings (Aim 2). These descriptive products alone would constitute a valuable resource, given the acknowledged inconsistency of terminology and the absence of comparable empirical syntheses. At the explanatory level, the prospective comparative design—including networks at different maturity stages and with different architectures—promises to reveal how organizational context shapes ethical problem-framing and problem-solving. At the translational level, the integration of empirical findings with normative analysis and stakeholder refinement (Aim 3) would yield recommendations that are both ethically reasoned and tested for practical acceptability.

**Strengths that support the value judgement**

Several design features strengthen the value of the attainable knowledge. The deliberate inclusion of developing networks and an abandoned networking effort (Sections 2, 5.3) is a significant sampling strength: it captures failures, trade-offs, and adaptive responses that studies limited to mature, successful networks would miss. This extends the inferential scope beyond a single maturity stage and can reveal conditions under which ethical tensions escalate or become manageable—knowledge of direct practical relevance to institutions contemplating or building network arrangements.

The prospective participant observation component (Aim 2) offers a rare window into real-time governance deliberation. Rather than relying solely on retrospective accounts or formal policies—both of which may smooth over conflict or rationalize outcomes post-hoc—the embedded observation can capture how actors frame problems, weigh competing values, negotiate disagreements, and adopt specific solutions in context. This process knowledge is a distinctive form of evidence that interviews alone cannot reliably supply.

The translational architecture of Aim 3 is well-conceived. Recommendations are not merely abstract ethical principles derived from armchair analysis; they are to be grounded in the empirical findings of Aims 1 and 2 and then tested for acceptability and implementability through focus groups and an expert symposium with the very stakeholders who would use them. This structured feedback loop increases the likelihood that the outputs will address real-world constraints and be usable by practitioners, not just publishable by scholars.

**Limitations affecting scientific value**

The most significant limitation is the exclusion of specimen donor perspectives from the empirical work. The proposal states explicitly that direct investigation of donors' experiences is reserved for subsequent research and that the study relies instead on community advisory board members and others tasked with representing donor priorities (Section 5.2). While community representatives may articulate important viewpoints, they are not a substitute for donors' own accounts of their expectations, misunderstandings, trust, or preferences regarding network sharing. This is not a peripheral concern. The proposal's own rationale emphasizes that ethically permissible local practices may differ across communities and that donor commitments should not be erased when specimens and data travel across institutional boundaries (Section 2). Governance recommendations about consent models, control mechanisms, and return of results that are to be "responsive to what donors actually value and understand" (as the ethical framing implies) would ideally be grounded in evidence about those values and understandings, not solely in institutional actors' representations of them.

The consequence is a limitation on the normative force of the recommendations. The project will generate a systematic account of how institutional and professional stakeholders reason about donor interests and how they build governance to reflect those interests as they understand them. That is a legitimate and valuable contribution—a necessary foundation for donor-centred governance. But it is an incomplete foundation. The study risks projecting stakeholder assumptions onto donors rather than grounding recommendations in donors' lived perspectives. This limitation is acknowledged in the proposal as a scope boundary, and within that boundary the knowledge goal remains valuable and achievable. The limitation bears on the reach of the normative outputs, not on the validity of the descriptive and comparative aims.

A second limitation is that the project is not designed to evaluate the downstream effects of different governance arrangements on research participation, trust, or health outcomes. The proposal states this scope clearly, and it is not a flaw for a study focused on ethical problem-characterization and normative solution-development. It does, however, mean that the practical contribution stops at the level of well-grounded, stakeholder-vetted recommendations rather than demonstrated effectiveness. The knowledge product is a normative and procedural guide, not an empirically validated governance intervention.

**Judgement**

The scientific value is good. The question is important, the descriptive and comparative knowledge attainable is substantial, and the translational product addresses a genuine practical need. The absence of donor perspectives tempers the completeness and normative authority of the recommendations but does not negate the value of the institutional-level account the project will deliver.

### 2. Innovation: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

**Nature of the innovation**

The project's innovation is primarily one of object, integration, and comparative design, rather than the invention of wholly new ethical concepts or analytic techniques. The central move is to make the *network*—the relationships, coordination mechanisms, and governance tensions among multiple institutions—the unit of analysis, rather than treating the network as one descriptive category among others in a survey of individual biobanks. Prior work cited by the investigators (Henderson et al. 2013, Goldenberg et al. 2015) inventoried biobanks and examined IRB policies; those studies identified networks as a configuration but did not investigate them as the core phenomenon to be explained. The current proposal explicitly builds on that work, and its inventory method is a deliberate extension of earlier search and case-study approaches. The increment is a targeted, in-depth investigation of a previously identified but under-analysed organizational form.

The second axis of innovation is methodological: the combination of prospective participant observation with concurrent normative analysis and stakeholder validation in one integrated design. Systematic qualitative observation of bioethics deliberation exists, and the investigators cite relevant experience. However, the application of embedded, longitudinal observation to the day-to-day governance of multiple heterogeneous networks, and its direct linkage to the formulation and focus-group testing of normative recommendations, is a distinctive design choice. The project does not merely describe practice and then separately reflect on norms; it builds an iterative feedback loop in which observed tensions and stakeholder reactions inform the normative products. This processual integration is a methodological contribution for translational bioethics research.

The comparative case selection introduces further originality. The five provisional cases span different development stages (mature, new, in-development) and different architectures (centralized data holdings vs. locally stored specimens, academic vs. registry-based, cloud-based sharing, state-held dried blood spots). This is not a convenience sample; it is justified by the analytic goal of understanding how context shapes governance. The explicit provision for replacing or adding cases based on Aim 1 findings (Section 5.3) indicates a design oriented toward theoretical sampling, which strengthens the potential for conceptual innovation over a fixed-description approach.

**Limitations affecting innovation**

The principal limitation is that the normative framework guiding the ethical analysis in Aim 3 is not specified in the supplied text. The proposal states that a "normative core" will examine conceptual and legal literature and evaluate candidate solutions, and that recommendations will be assessed against compatibility, cost, and trade-offs (Section 5.4). However, the particular ethical principles, theories, or legal standards that will structure this assessment are not described. This matters for the innovation judgement because the degree of conceptual novelty depends on what happens when established norms encounter the messy realities of networked governance. The empirical work is well-positioned to surface tensions—between respect for donor autonomy and collective research benefit, between local control and network efficiency, between privacy and data utility—that may resist easy resolution within familiar frameworks. Whether the project will produce novel normative resolutions (e.g., a revised conception of consent or stewardship specific to network contexts) or primarily offer practically refined articulations of familiar principles in a new setting remains to be demonstrated.

Applying existing norms to a new context can be valuable and original in its own right; the proposal does not overclaim theoretical novelty. But the unspecified framework tempers the expectation of a major conceptual breakthrough and leaves the depth of normative innovation as an open variable rather than a demonstrated feature of the design.

**Distinction from scientific value**

The importance of the project lies in the urgency of the question and the usable knowledge it could produce. Its originality lies in the shift of analytic focus to the network, the prospective comparative design, and the integration of empirical observation with iterative normative refinement. Both qualities are present, but the innovation is incremental—a well-justified extension and deepening of existing lines of inquiry—rather than transformative.

**Judgement**

The innovation is good. The combination of a novel unit of analysis, a methodologically integrated design, and a theoretically motivated case selection constitutes a genuine advance over prior work. The unspecified normative framework limits confidence specifically in the depth of conceptual innovation, but does not undermine the originality of the overall research approach.

### 3. Rigor: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

**Intended inferences and stage**

The project is mixed-methods and largely exploratory-descriptive in Aims 1 and 2, with a translational-prescriptive component in Aim 3. The three aims are sequential in logic but overlapping in execution: (1) a retrospective inventory and stakeholder interviews characterize existing networks and their challenges; (2) prospective participant observation examines how those challenges arise and are deliberated in everyday practice; (3) an integrative normative phase produces recommendations tested through focus groups and an expert symposium. The appropriate standards are those of qualitative description, cross-case comparison, and normative analysis—not those of a pilot trial or confirmatory study.

**Strengths supporting rigor**

The design contains multiple features that strengthen the credibility and trustworthiness of the intended inferences.

*Inventory and sampling.* The Aim 1a inventory builds on a prior national biobank survey and supplements it with multi-source searches (PubMed, NIH RePORTER, Google, AAMC member institutions, CTSA sites). The search strategy accommodates inconsistent terminology and includes verification through direct contact—an appropriate design for identifying an organizational form that lacks a standard label. The inventory yields both a descriptive map and a sampling frame for the interview and observation components, and the explicit provision that Aim 1 findings may justify replacing or adding cases in Aim 2 supports internal coherence.

*Interview design.* Up to 100 semi-structured interviews with professionals across multiple roles per network produces a substantial corpus. The sampling logic recognizes the risk of leadership-selected accounts and includes deliberate recruitment of less powerful or dissenting stakeholders. Professionally transcribed, 60-minute interviews with open-ended prompts and non-directive follow-up, conducted by experienced investigators, are a standard and dependable method for capturing stakeholder experiences. The boundary excluding specimen donors is acknowledged, and the inferences are appropriately limited to organizational and governance perspectives.

*Triangulation.* The explicit plan to compare stated policies (consent documents, data-use agreements, IRB protocols), reported experiences (interviews), and observed deliberations (participant observation) provides a built-in triangulation strategy. This is well-suited to the project's theoretical interest in discrepancies between formal rules and practice, and it strengthens descriptive claims by requiring convergence across evidence types.

*Qualitative analysis.* Framework Analysis with an initial codebook, double-coding on ten transcripts with a 90-percent agreement threshold, and a shared ATLAS.ti project across all aims provides a structured, auditable analytical procedure. Placing interviews, documents, and field notes in a common coding environment supports the cross-source comparative analysis the research questions demand.

*Participant observation.* The five provisional cases exhibit deliberate variation in maturity (mature, new, in-development) and configuration (study-based pooling, registry-linked tissue, state-held dried blood spots, cloud-based sharing, planned system-wide repository). The observation plan specifies settings (collaborator meetings, advisory boards, conference calls, IRB or ethics advisory boards) and frequency (in-person visits twice annually per network, with ongoing remote participation). Two-person site teams, immediate field-note recording in a secure workspace, and a bi-weekly team reflection process in which colleagues contribute interpretations before coding are recognized practices that make observer influence more visible rather than ignored—a strength for the credibility of the observational data.

*Normative integration.* The three-step translational sequence (empirical synthesis, normative analysis, stakeholder testing) is logically sound. The focus-group design specifies purposive recruitment by domain expertise, attention to power differences, advance distribution of draft recommendations, and systematic coding of discussions. The expert symposium adds cross-configuration review, and the immediate post-symposium investigator synthesis is a feasible mechanism for incorporating last-round feedback.

*Team and analytic integration.* The leadership assignments map investigators' documented expertise to relevant components. The normative core contributes throughout instrument development and interpretation, embedding ethical and legal reasoning from the outset rather than treating it as a post-hoc overlay. The shared ATLAS.ti project and common codes across aims create a technical pathway for the promised integration.

**Limitations affecting rigor**

*Absence of donor-level data.* The exclusion of donor perspectives is an acknowledged scope boundary. The descriptive and comparative aims focused on institutional governance do not require donor data. However, to the extent that the normative recommendations in Aim 3 bear on donor interests—consent, control, return of results—the empirical grounding for those claims rests entirely on institutional and advisory-body accounts, without independent corroboration from the affected population. The proposal does not claim donor validation, but the limitation is material to the completeness of the normative evidence base.

*Case generalizability and observation window.* The five cases are well-described by stage and configuration, but the rationale for expecting findings to be transferable across the heterogeneous universe of networked biorepositories is implicit rather than systematically argued. The observation window of approximately three years (quarters 3–14) can capture routine governance and emerging challenges, but major structural changes, long-term sustainability dynamics, or regulatory shifts that unfold slowly may not fully mature within the study period. This delimits the temporal phenomena the project can observe: deliberative processes and mid-term problem-solving, rather than the full lifecycle of network evolution. The adaptive case-selection mechanism (modifying cases based on Aim 1 findings) partially addresses generalizability but is constrained by the four-year schedule.

*Interview saturation and prioritization.* The inventory may identify many more than the roughly 20 networks implied by the five-informants-per-network planning figure. The supplied text does not specify how networks are prioritized for interview recruitment within time and budget, nor the criteria determining whether a network receives only document review and a key-contact interview versus deeper multi-stakeholder interviewing. Without this, some network types may remain thinly described, or data collection may cease before thematic saturation is reached for subtypes. The use of Framework Analysis with early analytical work beginning after ten interviews is typical, and the purposive sampling for variation partially mitigates the concern; the risk is constrained depth for some configurations, not a fundamental inability to produce meaningful description.

*Observer effects and access.* The bi-weekly reflexivity sessions address observer influence, but the presence of investigators in governance meetings may nonetheless affect deliberation, particularly where sensitive conflicts are at play. The proposal acknowledges this and aims to make effects "visible" rather than eliminate them—an appropriate stance for ethnographic work. The ongoing terms of access (which sessions are closed, whether informants later restrict field-note use) are not specified beyond the statement that network leaders have agreed to observation in principle. This introduces an uncertainty common in fieldwork; it is not a design flaw, but it is a dependency that cannot be settled in advance.

*Normative reasoning procedure.* The specific analytic method for moving from empirical patterns to normative conclusions—how conflicting values will be weighed, what ethical framework(s) will structure the analysis—is not detailed. The inclusion of philosophy and legal specialists, the plan for team deliberation on integrated reports, and the structured stakeholder testing provide a plausible multi-method foundation. The limitation is one of transparency in the proposal rather than an obvious analytical flaw, but it constrains independent assessment of the normative rigour from the supplied materials alone.

*Operational details for qualitative data management.* The volume of qualitative data (transcripts, field notes, documents) will be large. The schedule concentrates transcription and analysis in quarters 7–12, which demands substantial analyst time and close coordination across two lead sites. The text does not specify the number of qualitative analysts, procedures for maintaining coding consistency over a long field period, or how disagreements beyond the initial inter-coder agreement phase are resolved. These are practical issues that experienced qualitative teams can manage, but their absence from the supplied materials leaves some operational uncertainty.

**Synthesis**

The design provides a solid methodological foundation for the descriptive and exploratory aims and a reasonable pathway toward the translational aim. The triangulation strategy, Framework Analysis with inter-coder checks, reflexive observation practices, and structured stakeholder testing are well-matched to the intended inferences. The principal limitations—the donor-data boundary, the implicit generalizability assumptions, and the under-specified operational details—do not threaten the capacity of the design to support its central descriptive and comparative claims. They do, however, circumscribe the strength and reach of the normative outputs and introduce some manageable risk to timely delivery.

**Judgement**

Rigor is good. The multi-source, mixed-methods design with built-in triangulation and systematic qualitative analysis is appropriate for the research stage and questions. The acknowledged scope boundaries and unspecified operational details constrain confidence in some specific aspects—particularly the empirical grounding of normative claims about donor interests and the management of large-volume qualitative analysis—but do not undermine the overall inferential capacity of the design.

### 4. Feasibility: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

**Strengths supporting feasibility**

The feasibility of the proposed work rests on several well-supported foundations.

*Secured access.* A critical dependency for Aim 2 is access to the five contrasting networks for longitudinal observation. The proposal states that "Network leaders have agreed to observation of relevant activities," and this is substantiated by the inclusion of representatives from four of the five case-study networks on the advisory committee. This indicates that access has been negotiated at a high level and is not merely aspirational. For ethnographic fieldwork, such pre-negotiated access is a significant feasibility asset.

*Integrated schedule.* The four-year schedule (Table 7) shows a logical workflow with appropriate overlap. The inventory and interviews begin early and feed into the identification of key challenges by mid-project. The prospective observation (Q3–Q14) runs concurrently with analysis and the development of preliminary recommendations (Q9–Q12), enabling empirical findings to inform the normative work iteratively. This integrated design is a management strength.

*Proven methods.* The core methods—systematic multi-source inventory, semi-structured interviewing, Framework Analysis, participant observation, focus groups—are well-established in qualitative health research and bioethics. The investigators have documented experience with these methods, reducing the risk of methodological learning curves disrupting the schedule.

*Adaptive design.* The provision for modifying or adding cases based on Aim 1 findings (Section 5.3) introduces flexibility that can strengthen feasibility by allowing the team to adjust the observation set if the initial cases prove less instructive than anticipated or if unanticipated network types emerge from the inventory.

**Limitations affecting feasibility**

*External stakeholder recruitment.* The plan for 100 interviews (Aim 1b) and 10 focus groups (Aim 3) depends on successful recruitment of stakeholders outside the research team's direct control. The proposal relies on snowball sampling and public information to identify participants including IRB members, community advisers, and client researchers. It deliberately seeks "less powerful or dissenting stakeholders," which is methodologically sound but introduces a recruitment dependency. Dissenting members of a network or client investigators with negative experiences may be less accessible or less willing to participate, potentially biasing the sample toward institutionally aligned accounts. The proposal does not specify contingency plans for low recruitment from specific stakeholder categories, leaving uncertainty about how representational balance will be ensured if targeted groups prove hard to reach.

*Data management infrastructure.* The collaborative workflow requires both lead sites to analyze data, with two coders (one at each site) independently coding the same interviews, and all three aims sharing a common ATLAS.ti project. This creates a dependency on robust shared data management infrastructure for secure, real-time collaboration across the University of Louisville and Case Western Reserve University. The proposal names the analysis software but does not describe systems for securely sharing, storing, and synchronizing large volumes of sensitive qualitative data (audio recordings, transcripts, field notes) between the two sites. Delays in data synchronization or version-control ambiguities could threaten the planned bi-weekly reflection process and the ability to compare results between coders on schedule.

*Resourcing for normative analysis.* The translational aim involves a "normative core" that will examine conceptual and legal literature and evaluate candidate solutions. The investigators assigned to this core have appropriate legal and philosophical expertise. However, the extent of their dedicated effort for a substantial, year-long task (preparing and deliberating on full reports across multiple domains) is not detailed. The risk is that the normative analysis—a cornerstone of the project's final contribution—may be constrained not by lack of expertise but by insufficient protected time, given the concurrent demands of empirical data collection and observation.

*Observer access conditions.* While initial access has been secured, the ongoing terms of that access are not specified. Ethnographic fieldwork depends on sustained relationships and trust; if networks later restrict access to sensitive meetings or if informants limit field-note use, the observational data may be thinner than anticipated. This is a common uncertainty in fieldwork and not a design defect, but it is a dependency the proposal cannot fully control.

**Judgement**

Feasibility is good. The core dependencies—secured site access, appropriate methods, an integrated schedule, and an experienced team—are well-supported. The principal feasibility risks concern external stakeholder recruitment, cross-site data management infrastructure, protected time for normative analysis, and the ongoing conditions of observational access. These are manageable risks of a type that experienced research teams routinely navigate, but they are not fully addressed in the supplied materials and warrant attention during implementation.

### 5. Ethics and Safety: B (Good) — Confidence: C (Moderate)

**What the proposal addresses**

The project examines ethical and regulatory challenges in networked biorepositories. The research activities generating primary data are: a systematic inventory and up to 100 interviews with adult professional stakeholders (Aim 1); longitudinal participant observation of governance activities in five contrasting networks (Aim 2); focus groups with purposively selected stakeholders to test draft recommendations (Aim 3); and an expert symposium. The proposal does not recruit specimen donors as research participants. Participants are investigators, administrators, informaticists, ethics advisers, IRB members, community advisory board members, and client researchers. The study is a mixed-methods descriptive, observational, and normative project with translational outputs.

**Strengths in ethics and safety planning**

*Risk identification and mitigation.* The proposal correctly identifies the principal participant risks as discomfort, professional repercussions, and indirect identification of individuals or specific networks. Planned mitigations include study identifiers or pseudonyms in transcripts and reports, withholding network names in publications unless permission is obtained, restricting access to recordings and linkage information to the research team, locking paper records, and password-protecting computer files. These are standard and generally proportionate measures for professional qualitative research.

*Attention to internal confidentiality and power disparities.* In the interview component, the team states it will "deliberately seek less powerful or dissenting stakeholders to reduce reliance on leadership-selected accounts." For focus groups, the proposal promises "purposive recruitment" and composition "with attention to power differences so that participants can speak freely." These are explicit procedural commitments to reduce the risk that hierarchical or professional pressure silences critical or dissenting views—appropriate and important for a study of governance power dynamics.

*Observer reflexivity.* The participant observation plan incorporates a structured bi-weekly team reflexivity process in which investigators discuss observations and reflect on how their presence may have influenced deliberation. Rather than assuming neutrality, the proposal treats observer influence as an object of explicit professional attention and makes it part of the record. This is a strength for the ethical quality of the research process and for the trustworthiness of normative inferences drawing on observational data.

*Separation of practical and ethical concerns.* The interview design plans "prompts [that] distinguish practical problems from ethical responsibilities: for example, logistical difficulty in sharing is different from a perceived duty of stewardship." This reduces the risk of conflating operational inconvenience with normative obligation in stakeholder language and subsequent analysis—an ethically relevant analytic discipline.

*Consent, withdrawal, and data destruction.* Interview and focus-group participants will receive information, have opportunities for questions, provide consent, and may decline questions, stop recording, or withdraw. Original recordings will be destroyed once transcription and coding are complete. These are expected protections, well-matched to the activities described.

**Limitations affecting ethics and safety**

*Consent in transient observational settings.* The proposal acknowledges that "explicit individual consent may not be practicable for every transient participant" in observed settings and promises "transparency, repeated reminders, and appropriate authorization" coordinated with network leadership and the study IRB. This is a legitimate challenge in ethnographic and organizational research. However, the proposal does not specify what this authorization and transparency will consist of in practice: a blanket information sheet posted for recurring meetings, a verbal announcement at the start of sessions with an opportunity to opt out, periodic written notifications to mailing lists of observed bodies, or some other mechanism. The lack of operational detail does not demonstrate that the practice is unethical, but it prevents an independent assessment of whether the measures are proportionate to the settings. Some observed bodies (e.g., IRB meetings, community advisory boards) may include members with distinct professional or community relationships to the networks for whom observation by external researchers could create unease or reputation risk, even with identifiers removed. This is a condition that the IRB of record will need to evaluate against the specific contexts in Table 4; the proposal's description remains thin for a reader assessing ethical proportionality.

*Indirect identification of networks.* The project intends to "withhold network names in publications unless permission is obtained." However, the networks under study include distinctive arrangements—the Kentucky Cancer Registry, the Newborn Screening Translational Research Network, Sage Bionetworks–Synapse—whose organizational details and governance challenges may be sufficiently unique that rich description alone makes them identifiable to knowledgeable readers in genomics, bioethics, and public health. The proposal does not discuss how it will handle the tension between the institutional detail necessary for its normative and translational aims and the residual risk of indirect identification, or whether that risk is judged acceptable given the public, institutional nature of the activities studied. This is an unresolved dimension that should be addressed in IRB review and in agreements with network leadership.

*Protection of dissenting participants.* The commitment to seek out less powerful or dissenting voices is a methodological and ethical strength, but it also creates an obligation to protect those voices in dissemination when network identity can be inferred. The proposal does not describe procedures—such as member-checking with attention to potential harm, offering affected participants an opportunity to review representations of their network before publication, or a protocol for handling requests to excise sensitive material—that would operationalize protection of those who provide critical accounts within a small and potentially identifiable professional milieu. The absence of such procedures is a gap in the ethical risk-management plan as currently described; it does not indicate that the team would be insensitive, but it leaves the reader unable to confirm that risks to dissenting participants have been thoroughly addressed.

*Donor voices and proxy claims.* The exclusion of donor perspectives is a scope choice, not an ethical violation. However, the translational aim promises "generalizable recommendations" affecting donor interests. These recommendations will be based on institutional and advisory-body proxy accounts whose alignment with actual donor views is unverified. This is a limitation of the evidence base supporting claims about donor interests, and it carries an ethical dimension to the extent that governance recommendations are formulated and disseminated as responsive to donor needs without direct empirical grounding in donor perspectives. The proposal appropriately does not claim such grounding; the limitation concerns the scope of warranted inference, not participant protection.

*Participant incentives.* The proposal states interviews will be conducted "without participant incentives." For busy professionals whose organizations are already cooperating with the study, the absence of direct compensation is not inherently problematic. However, the choice may systematically affect who can participate—favoring those with institutional time and authority and potentially excluding the very dissenting or less powerful stakeholders the team wishes to include. The proposal does not discuss this trade-off or describe alternative accommodations (e.g., scheduling flexibility, modest honoraria to offset time away from other duties). This is a minor but practical ethical-design consideration left unspecified.

**Overall character of the ethics plan**

The ethics and safety planning shows awareness of the relevant professional and reputational risks in observational governance research and includes several thoughtful features—particularly the reflexivity practice around observer influence and the explicit attention to power disparities in sampling and focus-group composition. The principal limitations concern the level of operational detail for managing consent in transient observational settings, protecting dissenting participants in potentially identifiable professional contexts, and addressing indirect network identification. These are issues that the institutional review board for the study would need to resolve, and they do not, on the evidence supplied, appear to make the study disproportionate or unsafe, provided they are adequately specified in the protocol submitted for ethical review.

**Judgement**

Ethics and safety are good in their conceptual orientation and identification of principal risks. Confidence is moderate because the supplied materials leave several operationally important protective measures unspecified. The plan is not deficient; it is incomplete at the level of detail needed to confirm that risks in observational settings and to dissenting participants have been fully addressed. These gaps are of a type that IRB review would normally require to be filled.

### 6. Management: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

**Strengths in management**

*Coordinated leadership and defined responsibilities.* The management structure is clearly delineated. The co-PIs (Brothers and Goldenberg) share oversight but have distinct, non-overlapping leadership areas—Goldenberg leading the inventory and interviews, Brothers leading observation and policy development. This division of labour is practical and reduces potential for conflict. Table 5 assigns a lead and specific contributions for each major component, extending responsibilities to other expert co-investigators (Cadigan for ethnographic methods, Harrell for document analysis, Rothwell for legal analysis). This demonstrates a well-coordinated team with appropriate expertise distributed across the work packages.

*Integrated schedule and workflow.* The four-year schedule in Table 7 illustrates a logical and integrated workflow. Aims are appropriately overlapped: the inventory and interviews begin early and feed into challenge identification by mid-project. The prospective observation (Q3–Q14) runs concurrently with analysis and the development of preliminary recommendations (Q9–Q12), enabling empirical findings to inform the normative work directly. This integrated design ensures the translational aim is grounded in ongoing data collection, not treated as a separate downstream activity.

*Advisory committee.* The inclusion of representatives from four of the five case-study networks on the advisory committee serves dual management functions: it formalizes the access relationships and creates a governance structure for ongoing engagement and practical guidance. This is a sensible mechanism for maintaining network relationships and obtaining real-time feedback on emerging challenges.

*Shared technical infrastructure.* The commitment to a common ATLAS.ti project and shared codes across all aims creates a technical pathway for data integration and cross-aim analysis. Placing interviews, documents, and field notes in a single coding environment supports the comparative work the project requires.

**Limitations affecting management**

*Cross-site data coordination.* The collaborative workflow requires both lead sites to analyze data, with two coders (one at each site) independently coding the same interviews. This creates a dependency on robust shared data management infrastructure for secure data sharing, storage, and synchronization between the University of Louisville and Case Western Reserve University. The proposal does not describe this infrastructure. The volume of qualitative data will be large, and delays or version-control problems could affect the bi-weekly reflection schedule and the ability to compare coder results on time. This is a practical implementation issue that experienced teams can address, but it is unspecified in the supplied materials.

*Qualitative analysis resourcing.* The schedule concentrates transcription and qualitative analysis in quarters 7–12, a period of high demand for analyst time across two sites. The proposal does not specify the number of qualitative analysts, procedures for maintaining coding consistency over a long field period (beyond the initial inter-coder agreement phase on ten transcripts), or how coding disagreements that emerge later will be resolved. The volume of data—transcripts, field notes, documents—suggests a substantial analytical workload. The investigators' documented experience provides indirect reassurance, but the operational planning is not detailed in the supplied text.

*Resourcing for normative work.* The normative core's task—examining conceptual and legal literature, evaluating candidate solutions, preparing full reports, and participating in team deliberation—is central to the project's contribution. The investigators assigned to this core have appropriate expertise, but the extent of their dedicated effort for this year-long analytical and writing task is not specified. The management plan primarily describes an investigative lead for each report from within the team; the risk is that this work may be constrained by insufficient protected time when balanced against concurrent empirical data collection and observation demands.

**Judgement**

Management is good. The leadership structure, assignment of responsibilities, integrated schedule, and advisory committee provide a coherent framework for delivering the stated aims. The principal management limitations concern the unspecified operational details for cross-site data coordination, qualitative analysis resourcing, and protected time for normative work. These are practical implementation issues that an experienced team with a track record in multi-site qualitative research can typically navigate, but their absence from the supplied materials leaves some residual uncertainty about on-time delivery of all components.

## Recommendation: Conditional Support

The project addresses an important and timely question with a well-justified, multi-method design that integrates empirical observation with normative analysis and stakeholder validation. The descriptive and comparative aims are well-supported, and the translational deliverables—including a typology of network configurations, detailed case narratives of governance deliberation, and practitioner-informed recommendations—would constitute a genuine and useful contribution to scholarship and institutional practice. The investigative team possesses the requisite expertise, the management structure is clear, and critical access to observation sites has been secured.

The recommendation of conditional support reflects the balance of these strengths against two identifiable conditions that affect the scope and force of the project's central contributions.

**Condition 1: Donor perspectives as a boundary on normative claims.** The study excludes direct investigation of specimen donor perspectives and relies on community advisory board members and institutional informants as proxies for donor interests. The project can produce a valuable account of how institutional and professional stakeholders reason about donor interests and how they design governance to reflect those interests as they understand them. However, the governance recommendations will rest on an empirical foundation that does not include donors' own accounts of their expectations, values, or preferences regarding network sharing. The normative force and generalizability of recommendations about consent, control, and return of results are therefore contingent on the unverified premise that proxy accounts adequately represent donor interests. The project should make this boundary explicit in its outputs, framing its recommendations as grounded in institutional and professional reasoning about donor interests, with direct investigation of donor perspectives identified as a necessary next step. This does not require redesigning the study; it requires disciplined scope-claiming in dissemination.

**Condition 2: Specification of protective measures in observational settings.** The ethics and safety planning identifies the principal risks and includes several thoughtful features, but the operational details for managing consent in transient observational settings, protecting dissenting participants in potentially identifiable professional contexts, and addressing indirect network identification are not yet specified at a level that allows independent confirmation of their adequacy. The study is not unsafe or disproportionate on the evidence supplied, but these protective measures must be elaborated in the protocol submitted for IRB review. Support is conditional on the team specifying: (a) the practical mechanisms for providing transparency and an opportunity to opt out in observed meetings with changing participants; (b) procedures for protecting dissenting voices when network identity may be inferable from published description; and (c) the approach to managing the tension between rich institutional detail (necessary for the normative aims) and residual identifiability. These are matters that institutional review would normally require; they do not require redesign of the research, but they must be resolved before fieldwork commences.

The project is supportable within its stated scope. The conditions identified do not alter the fundamental design or the nature of the contribution; they delineate its limits and require operational specification of ethical protections. With these conditions addressed, the project is well-positioned to deliver a systematic, practice-informed account of networked biorepository governance that advances scholarship and provides actionable guidance to the institutions navigating this complex terrain.

**Recommendation:** Conditional Support

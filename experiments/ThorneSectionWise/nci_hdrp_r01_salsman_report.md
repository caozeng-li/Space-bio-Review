# Comprehensive Review

## Overall Assessment

This proposal describes a methodologically sophisticated optimization trial (EMPOWER) that applies the Multiphase Optimization Strategy (MOST) framework to a five-component positive psychology intervention for young adult cancer survivors after treatment. The project addresses a genuine gap by seeking to identify which specific skills contribute to psychological well-being in a population whose post-treatment developmental needs remain underserved. The factorial design is an efficient and appropriate choice for the component-selection question, and the expertise assembled for the work is well-matched to the planned activities.

The central trade-off in this proposal is between a strong methodological architecture and a set of interdependent execution uncertainties that the submitted documentation does not fully resolve. The factorial design promises actionable knowledge about component efficacy that prior single-package trials have not provided, and the analytic framework is correctly specified for the optimization stage. However, the plan rests on optimistic assumptions about recruitment yield, retention, and the inertness of control content—assumptions that are partially supported by limited pilot data but lack formal contingency planning. More critically, the ethical framework as documented contains substantive gaps, particularly an undefined exclusion of participants with significant psychiatric history and no specified protocol for monitoring or responding to participant distress during a study that repeatedly collects mental health data and delivers emotion-focused content. These gaps are not merely documentation deficiencies; they affect core participant protections and equitable access.

The contribution is appropriately scoped as an optimization phase that will yield component efficacy signals and an assembled package suitable for subsequent confirmatory evaluation. This is a legitimate and valuable stage, but the downstream utility of the assembled package depends on the study producing interpretable component contrasts and reliable retention. The current plan provides a plausible route to that knowledge, but only under conditions that are not yet fully established. The strengths across scientific value, innovation, and the core design logic justify a recommendation of conditional support, subject to documentation that resolves the material dependencies identified below.

## Criterion-Specific Evaluations

### Scientific Value

**Rating: B | Confidence: B**

The proposal addresses a high-value question at the intersection of positive psychology, cancer survivorship, and young adult development. The rationale, grounded in Broaden-and-Build Theory and Revised Stress and Coping Theory, frames positive affect as a resource for coping rather than merely an outcome. The attainable knowledge is clearly specified: a data-driven identification of which intervention components contribute meaningfully to positive affect, life satisfaction, and reduced depression and anxiety over a one-month follow-up.

The secondary aims add mechanistic and differential value by examining coping self-efficacy and social support as mediators and age and gender as moderators. This moves the contribution beyond a simple efficacy question toward understanding mechanisms and heterogeneity. The project also produces an assembled, refined intervention ready for confirmatory testing, which represents a tangible practical output.

The principal limitation on scientific value concerns the breadth of contribution that this single optimization trial can reliably support. The study is powered to detect moderate-or-larger main effects (d=0.31 with 256 retained participants at alpha=0.10), which is appropriate for a screening phase. However, the proposal also commits to mediation analyses and moderation exploration within the same design. The mediation plan conditions on components judged effective, which introduces post-selection inference challenges, and the moderation analyses will have substantially lower power than the main-effect contrasts. Unless the mediation strategy is causally identified (e.g., through instrumental variable approaches using randomization) and the moderation expectations are explicitly qualified as exploratory, the secondary knowledge contribution may be less definitive than the proposal narrative suggests. This does not diminish the primary value of the component screen, but it tempers expectations for the mechanism-oriented aims.

The value is also contingent on the assembled package being demonstrably superior to the original multicomponent intervention. If several components show small effects below the retention criterion, or if interactions complicate selection, the path to a single optimized package becomes less straightforward than the timeline implies. This is a genuine scientific uncertainty and an acceptable one for an optimization trial, but it affects the certainty with which the downstream value can be asserted at this stage.

### Innovation

**Rating: B | Confidence: B**

The core innovation is methodological: applying a full factorial experiment within the MOST framework to a positive psychology intervention for young adult cancer survivors. The proposal correctly positions this approach against prior work that has evaluated multicomponent packages as whole entities, citing LILAC, DAHLIA, and LEAF. The critical limitation of those prior studies is that they cannot identify which specific skills contribute to outcomes—a limitation that this proposal directly addresses through simultaneous testing of five components in all 32 combinations.

The factorial design is not novel in itself; MOST is an established framework with published guidance and exemplars. However, its application to this specific intervention and population represents a genuine advance for this line of research. The plan to test two-way interactions, though power-limited, acknowledges the possibility of synergistic or antagonistic component effects and adds nuance to the component-selection strategy.

The innovation should be distinguished from breakthrough originality. The individual intervention components (gratitude, mindfulness, reappraisal, strengths, kindness) are well-established in positive psychology, and web-based delivery is a mature modality in psychosocial oncology. The proposal does not claim to develop new component content or a novel delivery mechanism. The contribution is the efficient, simultaneous empirical evaluation of these components as building blocks, which yields actionable knowledge for refining the intervention rather than advancing the theoretical frontiers of emotion regulation or survivorship care.

### Rigor

**Rating: B | Confidence: B**

The factorial design is well-matched to the component-selection question. Using a 2^5 design with 352 randomized participants provides efficient main-effect contrasts (n=176 per component on/off) and appropriately powered tests of selected interactions. The primary endpoint (one-month follow-up) and measurement instruments (PROMIS and NIH Toolbox CATs) are defensible and well-validated. The power analysis is clearly specified, with an explicit acknowledgment that the detectable main effect (d=0.31) is smaller than the retention criterion (d=0.50), and that smaller interactions will not be detectable. Setting alpha at 0.10 is appropriate for an optimization phase aimed at screening rather than definitive inference.

The analytic plan addresses key threats to inference by adjusting for baseline scores, examining baseline characteristics for imbalance, and using longitudinal mixed-effects models to improve precision. These are standard and sound choices.

Several material limitations affect the strength of the intended inferences:

**Retention uncertainty and missing data.** The power analysis assumes 72% retention at one month, yielding 256 completers. The proposal's pilot data show variability (66.7% to 72%) and indicate that those who dropped out had poorer baseline well-being. Retention falling below 60% would shift the detectable effect size for main effects noticeably. The proposal does not specify a missing-data strategy—multiple imputation, pattern-mixture models, or tipping-point analyses—that would assess sensitivity to non-random attrition. Without this, the one-month findings could be compromised in the plausible scenario that retention is lower than assumed.

**Control content inertness.** The design replaces absent components with factual journaling, nutrition reading, and stress psychoeducation. The proposal acknowledges that if these activities improve well-being, component effect estimates will be attenuated. This is a known design limitation, but the study provides no independent estimate of the control content's activity (e.g., a no-treatment condition) and no plan to measure control credibility or engagement. A null main effect could reflect either component ineffectiveness or a control condition that is not truly inert, and the design cannot distinguish these interpretations.

**Mediation and moderation inference.** The mediation plan conditions on components judged effective, creating post-selection concerns. The proposal does not specify whether randomized assignment will serve as an instrument, which is the standard approach for causally identified mediation in factorial designs. Without this specification, the inferential strength of the mediation claims cannot be evaluated. Moderation analyses by age and gender are underpowered relative to the main-effect contrasts, and the proposal does not report detectable subgroup differences or clarify whether these analyses are strictly exploratory.

**Completion timing.** The primary analysis starts from intervention completion, not randomization, but the protocol allows up to eight weeks for a five-week program. If completion time varies systematically with component assignment or participant characteristics, the outcome comparison no longer reflects the same elapsed time since randomization. The analytic plan does not address how differential completion timing will be handled.

These limitations interact: optimistic retention combined with an unvalidated control condition and no formal missing-data plan creates a compound risk to the interpretability of component-effect estimates. The individual issues are manageable, but their combination warrants strengthened documentation and pre-registered analytic commitments.

### Feasibility

**Rating: B | Confidence: B**

The project can likely be carried out with the stated methods, access, and resources. The expertise is well-matched: the PIs contribute positive-emotion intervention experience and young-adult oncology recruitment infrastructure, and the broader team covers MOST methodology, psychosocial oncology, quantitative methods, and eHealth. No critical skill gap is evident.

The recruitment infrastructure is substantive. Two comprehensive cancer centers provide documented sources of potentially eligible survivors (approximately 950 post-treatment survivors seen annually across sites), yielding an estimated 2,850 potentially eligible individuals over three years. The target of 352 enrolled is derived from a conservative 13% assumption and is consistent with the 16.5% eligible-and-interested figure from pilot experience. The multi-channel recruitment plan (registry review, EHR, letters, calls, community partnerships, flyers) is layered and provides contingencies, including the Cancer Services community partnership.

Technical resources are institutionalized and appropriate: the Wake Forest Research Information Systems Unit hosts the web-based intervention, REDCap manages data collection, and adaptive testing keeps assessment burden manageable. The four-year timeline sequences startup, recruitment, analysis, and dissemination coherently.

The primary feasibility dependencies that require ongoing management are:

**Recruitment conversion rates.** The 13% assumption is partially supported by pilot data, but those pilots enrolled small numbers (18 and 23) from limited denominators. The Wake Forest pilot's 23.2% yield came from 99 contacted, making that estimate unstable. If registry-identified survivors convert materially below 13%—due to lower provider permission rates, higher ineligibility, or lower interest—the recruitment window could extend beyond the planned 36 months. The Cancer Services backup is mentioned but not quantified in scale or expected yield.

**Retention.** The power analysis assumes 72% one-month retention. Pilot retention ranged from 66.7% to 72%, with the lower figure drawn from a wave still in progress. Sustaining 72% retention across 352 participants and 32 conditions over a multi-year enrollment period is within the range of behavioral trial experience but is not assured. The factorial design's requirement that all 32 cells contribute to the marginal contrasts means retention shortfalls affect the entire analytic sample, not just single arms.

**Factorial delivery fidelity.** The proposal does not document a quality-assurance plan for verifying that participants receive the correct combination of active and inert components, or for monitoring randomization fidelity across two sites. The Wake Forest Research Information Systems Unit centralizes the platform, which provides some control, but the coordination procedures for the Northwestern site are not specified. Given that the entire optimization inference depends on factorial assignment integrity, this gap in documented management procedures is notable, though it does not indicate a demonstrated problem.

### Ethics and Safety

**Rating: C | Confidence: B**

The proposed research activities are low-risk in nature: a web-based skills-training program involving daily practice of emotion regulation techniques, with weekly surveys measuring psychological outcomes. The described consent process, use of unique study IDs and PINs, and reliance on REDCap for data collection provide a baseline of participant protection. The intervention's remote, web-based format reduces geographic and mobility barriers to participation, which is ethically favorable for young adult survivors.

However, the ethical framework as documented contains substantive gaps that affect participant protection and equitable access:

**Undefined psychiatric exclusion.** The exclusion of individuals with a "significant psychiatric history" is stated without definition, clinical threshold, or scientific justification. This is a material ethical concern because it could systematically exclude young adult survivors with the greatest psychological need—precisely those for whom a well-being intervention might be most relevant. The proposal provides no safety rationale for this exclusion (e.g., risk of adverse reactions to specific components, interference with ongoing psychiatric treatment) and no connection to study validity or feasibility. As specified, this criterion risks unjust discrimination and reduces the generalizability and justice of the research.

**Absence of distress monitoring and response protocol.** The intervention engages participants with emotional content (mindfulness, reappraisal in the context of cancer-related trauma) and collects repeated measures of depression and anxiety. Even in a low-risk, self-guided intervention, some participants may experience temporary increases in distress. The proposal does not describe any plan for monitoring such events, establishing trigger-based alerts from survey data, providing duty-of-care pathways, or referring participants to clinical services. The statement that the online format "preserves participants' ongoing clinical care" does not constitute a plan for managing research-related distress; it simply notes that the study is not replacing clinical care. This is a genuine gap in proportionate risk management.

**Digital equity.** The requirement for a home computer or smartphone with reliable internet access creates a structural barrier that may disproportionately exclude survivors with fewer financial resources. The proposal acknowledges multiple recruitment channels, including community partnerships, but does not discuss this equity dimension or describe any mitigation (e.g., providing devices, data plans, or alternative access methods).

**Data safety and privacy.** The proposal mentions REDCap, unique IDs, and PINs, which suggest a reasonable security posture. However, specifics on personally identifiable information collected, encryption, access controls, retention and destruction timelines, and breach response are absent. This limits confidence in the comprehensiveness of the data protection plan, particularly given the volume and sensitivity of the longitudinal psychological data.

**Community engagement.** No evidence is supplied regarding survivor involvement in the design of the intervention, selection of outcomes, or recruitment strategies. Meaningful stakeholder engagement is an ethical strength in research with underserved populations, and its absence is a limitation in demonstrating alignment with community values.

These limitations are moderate in severity. The psychiatric exclusion and distress-monitoring gaps are the most consequential: the former affects justice and equitable access; the latter affects participant welfare during study conduct. They do not suggest the research is inherently unethical, but they indicate that the ethical framework requires substantive elaboration—including operationalized exclusion criteria with clear justification and a defined safety-monitoring protocol—before the protocol adequately protects participants.

### Management

**Rating: B | Confidence: B**

The management arrangements documented in the proposal are generally coherent and support delivery of the stated aims. The expertise matches the core tasks, the timeline sequences activities reasonably, and the technical infrastructure is institutionalized.

**Strengths:**

The team composition directly covers intervention design, MOST factorial methodology, young-adult survivorship recruitment, quantitative analysis, and eHealth delivery. PI Salsman contributes site access and recruitment experience at the two cancer centers; PI Moskowitz contributes the EMPOWER content and prior positive-emotion trial experience; collaborator Wagner contributes specific MOST trial expertise. The schedule (Table 5) integrates startup, Aim 1 optimization across three years of recruitment and follow-up, overlapping analysis, and Year 4 preparation of manuscripts and a confirmatory trial grant.

**Limitations:**

The factorial delivery coordination across two sites, with the Wake Forest unit hosting the platform and Northwestern enrolling participants, is not documented with a specific quality-assurance plan for verifying randomization fidelity and component delivery integrity. This is an unspecified arrangement that would benefit from explicit procedures.

Adherence and fidelity monitoring during the five-week intervention period is not described. If engagement varies substantially across components, the intention-to-treat comparison of assignment remains valid, but the interpretability of the component selection decision for assembling a refined package depends on understanding whether null effects reflect ineffectiveness or low engagement. This is an analytical concern but also a management one, as it affects the confidence with which the team can proceed to the confirmatory trial planning phase.

The deferral of confirmatory evaluation to a future trial is appropriate staging, but the current project's management assumes that the optimization phase will produce a clear component selection. If several components show modest effects below the retention criterion, or if interactions complicate the selection, the path to a single optimized package may require additional decision-making that the timeline does not accommodate. This is a scientific dependency rather than a management failure, but it affects the coordination of the final-year planning for the future confirmatory grant.

## Recommendation: Conditional Support

The proposal describes a worthwhile optimization study that addresses a genuine need and employs a methodologically strong design. The factorial experiment is well-suited to the component-selection question, the expertise is appropriate, and the recruitment infrastructure is substantive. The scientific value and innovation are clear, and the core design logic supports the primary inference.

However, the current plan contains material dependencies that affect the main goals and that are not resolved in the submitted documentation. These are:

1.  **Ethical framework gaps.** The undefined psychiatric exclusion and absent distress-monitoring protocol represent genuine participant protection concerns that must be addressed before study conduct. These are not merely documentation deficiencies; they affect who can participate and how the study manages foreseeable psychological risks.

2.  **Retention and missing-data strategy.** The power analysis depends on 72% retention, yet pilot retention varies and those who drop out differ systematically on baseline well-being. Without a pre-registered missing-data strategy and sensitivity analyses, the primary one-month findings remain vulnerable to attrition bias that could compromise component selection.

3.  **Control content validation.** The design cannot distinguish a null component effect from an active control condition. If this limitation cannot be resolved by a design change at this stage, the proposal should commit to measuring control credibility and engagement and to interpreting null results with appropriate caution in the component-selection algorithm.

4.  **Mediation causal identification.** The mediation analyses should specify whether randomized assignment serves as an instrument, which is required for causally interpretable indirect effects. Without this, the inferential claims from Aim 2 will remain associational and exploratory.

These conditions are supportable in scope: they require elaboration and pre-registration of specific analytic and ethical protocols rather than a redesign of the study. If the investigators can document operationalized exclusion criteria with clear justification, a distress-monitoring and response protocol, a pre-registered missing-data strategy with sensitivity analyses, a plan for assessing control content activity, and a causally identified mediation analytic plan, the project would provide a sufficient route to its stated optimization goals.

The recommendation is therefore conditional support: a supportable project is identifiable, subject to the factual premises listed above being satisfied and documented.

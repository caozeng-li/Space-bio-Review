## Overall Assessment

This proposal aims to produce the first systematic, population-based analysis of eugenic sterilization in California, drawing on approximately 15,000 sterilization recommendation records spanning nine institutions and a 31-year period. The planned work integrates quantitative epidemiological methods with qualitative analysis of administrative documents and patient correspondence to characterise who was recommended for sterilization, estimate demographic associations with sterilization risk, and recover evidence of patient and family refusal and resistance. The project represents the analytical core of a larger research programme; a companion digital archive is being pursued as a separate, parallel effort. The scientific value of the question is considerable, and the assembled dataset offers an empirical foundation that substantially exceeds prior descriptive and narrative accounts. The principal investigator brings strong historical expertise and demonstrated familiarity with the records, and the quantitative collaborators provide relevant epidemiological resources.

At the same time, the proposal’s capacity to deliver its most distinctive promises—population-based rate estimation using census denominators, formal hypothesis testing with a case-cohort Poisson regression design, and integrated interpretation of quantitative and qualitative findings—depends on methodological specifications that are not yet documented in the proposal text. The construction of comparable population denominators across discontinuous census years and heterogeneous institutional reports, the identification of institutional residents in historical census microdata, the analytical details of the regression models, and the volume and completeness of qualitative materials across all nine institutions remain insufficiently specified to permit a fully confident judgement of feasibility. Furthermore, while the ethical framing is appropriate and concrete data-protection measures are described, the proposal does not provide a sufficient plan for managing disclosure risk in the combination of rich demographic variables with qualitative narratives, nor does it engage with descendant communities or familial-level risks.

These limitations are resolvable in principle and do not indicate that the work is impossible or lacking in value. However, they affect central components of the proposed plan—the population-based comparisons of Aim 2 and the public-facing dissemination of patient narratives—such that the current proposal does not provide sufficient evidence that those components can be executed as described. The direction of research is demonstrably worthwhile and the preparatory work is substantial; what is needed is a more fully specified plan for the elements that distinguish this project from a purely descriptive or single-institution account.

## Criterion-by-Criterion Evaluation

### Scientific Value

**Rating:** A | **Confidence:** B

The scientific value of this project is high. The question—who was subjected to eugenic sterilization in California, what institutional and demographic patterns shaped that exposure, and how did patients and families respond—is historically and ethically important. The project will produce a detailed empirical account that goes well beyond existing descriptive statistics and narrative histories. Specifically, it will provide:

- **Descriptive knowledge:** The first systematic documentation of demographic patterns across approximately three-quarters of California’s recorded sterilization procedures and one-quarter of the national total. The dataset’s 212 discrete variables—including demographic, diagnostic, institutional, and family-context information—will support a more precise and comprehensive description than is currently available.
- **Methodological knowledge:** The study advances beyond simple tabulations by linking recommendation records to census microdata and institutional population data through a case-cohort design with Poisson regression. The distinction between the risk of institutionalisation and the risk of sterilization recommendation within institutions is essential for interpreting observed demographic disproportions, and the Pacific Colony pilot demonstrates that this distinction can be operationalised.
- **Conceptual knowledge:** The proposal treats historical categories of diagnosis, delinquency, and consent not as neutral descriptors but as objects of analysis. The qualitative component will examine how classifications changed over time, how consent was constructed in a setting where sterilization could be a condition of release, and how patients and families resisted. The analytical distinction between “signature, acquiescence, refusal, and meaningful choice” (Section 2) and the commitment to preserving the difference between institutional claims and patients’ own statements (Section 5.5) provide a conceptual approach with broader applicability to other studies of institutional coercion.
- **Practical knowledge:** The findings are intended to inform a companion digital archive under parallel development, contributing to contemporary ethical, legal, and social discourse on the history and implications of human genetics.

The attainable knowledge is primarily descriptive and interpretive rather than causal, and this is appropriate for the research type. The project does not promise to identify causal mechanisms; it will characterise who was recommended for sterilization, estimate demographic associations, document institutional and temporal variation, and recover acts of refusal. The main limitations affecting scientific value concern the precision and breadth of the attainable contribution: the incomplete denominator data for institutional populations, the narrower focus on Spanish-surnamed ethnicity relative to the fuller range of racialised classifications in the historical record, and the possible selectivity of surviving qualitative documents. These bound the contribution but do not negate its importance. The project remains a substantial increment beyond the prior literature.

### Innovation

**Rating:** B | **Confidence:** B

The project adds three principal innovations beyond prior work:

1. **Scale and systematic character of the quantitative analysis.** Prior scholarship has produced descriptive statistics and narratives from individual institutions or selected periods. This project will assemble data from nine institutions over 31 years, link them to population denominators, and apply formal epidemiological methods. This is a substantial expansion of an existing research programme, not a fundamentally new type of investigation—the Pacific Colony pilot already applies the case-cohort logic and the Spanish-surname method—but the extension to the full dataset represents a material increment in empirical reach.
2. **Integration of quantitative and qualitative analysis within a single analytical framework.** The proposal does not treat qualitative documents as mere illustration; it proposes systematic coding of diagnostic language, analysis of the complete set of refusal and protest letters, and examination of consent practices as institutional routines. The combination of population-level patterns with close analysis of the full corpus of administrative documents and patient letters goes beyond much prior mixed-methods historical work.
3. **Methodological attention to comparability of historical racial and ethnic classifications.** The systematic approach to identifying Spanish-surnamed individuals using standardised Census Bureau surname lists with manual review for misspellings, combined with explicit acknowledgement of cross-census differences in racial and ethnic categories, represents an improvement over analyses that treat original administrative categories as given.

The innovation of the qualitative–quantitative integration depends critically on execution. The proposal states that the two streams will “jointly support interpretation” (Section 5.5) but does not specify formal procedures for triangulation, for using emerging quantitative patterns to guide qualitative sampling, or for using qualitative findings to refine quantitative hypotheses. As described, the integration is a juxtaposition of complementary methods rather than a formal mixed-methods design with specified integration points. This limits the strength of the innovation claim as it stands, though the potential remains substantial.

### Rigor

**Rating:** B | **Confidence:** C

The proposal provides a defensible overall research strategy for a historical-epidemiological investigation. The Pacific Colony pilot demonstrates that basic comparisons can be executed and that the necessary variables exist in the recommendation records. The plan for identifying Spanish surnames is methodologically transparent. The qualitative component defines a clear analytical object and commits to a sound analytical distinction between institutional claims and patient responses.

However, the proposal contains several documentation gaps that directly affect the strength of support for the intended population-based inferences. These gaps lower confidence in the judgement rather than driving the rating lower, because the underlying direction of the work is well-conceived and the deficiencies are in specification rather than in demonstrated impossibility.

**Population denominator construction and comparability over time.** The proposal intends to use census microdata from 1920, 1930, and 1940 to identify residents of the nine institutions. The text does not describe how institutional residents will be identified in these microdata—for example, whether the census recorded institutional residence in a standardised field, whether the institutions are enumerable by name or address, or whether the records permit reliable linkage. If a substantial proportion of residents cannot be reliably identified, the denominators will be incomplete and rate estimates will be biased in unknown directions. This is a specific, material documentation gap.

**Temporal mismatch between numerator and denominator data.** Sterilization recommendations span 1921–1952, but individual-level census microdata cover only 1920, 1930, and 1940. Intercensal years—the majority of the study period—will rely on institutional reports whose variable coverage, completeness, and definitions may differ from the census and from the recommendation records. The pilot addresses only one institution during 1935–1944 using a single census year (1940) for its denominator; it does not demonstrate that the approach extrapolates to the full multi-institution, multi-decade design. Rate comparisons across the full period may conflate genuine demographic differences with changes in recording practices and denominator quality, and the proposal does not yet detail how commensurate variables will be constructed across these heterogeneous sources.

**Incomplete specification of the case-cohort design and Poisson models.** The proposal does not describe how the case-cohort sampling will be structured, how it will account for the fact that census denominators are point-in-time snapshots while recommendations accumulate over years, which covariates will be included in the Poisson models, or how clustering by institution will be handled. These details are material to whether the regression analyses will yield interpretable risk estimates rather than confounded associations. Some methodological elaboration is expected during the work for an analytical-phase project, but the absence of these specifications means the strength of support for the central hypothesis tests cannot be fully assessed from the current text.

**Linkage between recommendation records and qualitative documents.** The proposal does not state the proportion of the 15,000 records for which supplementary qualitative documents exist, whether they are systematically available across institutions and periods, or whether consistent identifiers permit linkage to quantitative variables. The qualitative corpus is described only as “approximately 50 handwritten letters from patients at Patton,” leaving the scope across all nine institutions unspecified.

These gaps do not make the proposed work impossible, but they mean that the elements distinguishing this project from a purely descriptive or single-institution account—the population-based rate estimation and hypothesis testing—lack the documentary support that would warrant high confidence at this stage.

### Feasibility

**Rating:** B | **Confidence:** C

The core data, infrastructure, and expertise are credible. Stern has published related historical work (Stern 2005, Lira & Stern 2014), demonstrating familiarity with the records and the analytical terrain. IRB approvals from California and the University of Michigan are in place. The digitised REDCap dataset with 212 variables is described as having data entry planned for completion before the analytical phase. Access to census microdata through the University of Michigan’s Institute for Social Research is a plausible and well-supported arrangement. The data-protection infrastructure (HIPAA-compliant REDCap, password-protected server, secure physical storage) is appropriate and described as already developed.

The companion digital archive is explicitly scoped as a parallel project with separate funding, so the analytical project’s deliverables are contingent only on producing findings, not on building the archive. This is a realistic and appropriate scoping.

Feasibility is materially affected, however, by several unspecified dependencies:

- **Completion of data entry** is a stated precondition for the analytical phase. The proposal states this is “planned for completion” but does not identify a contingency if the preparatory phase overruns. A delay in data entry would directly compress the 24-month analytical timeline.
- **The qualitative corpus volume** is not quantified for the full dataset. Whether the volume is manageable within two years depends on the actual quantity of forms, correspondence, and patient communications across all nine institutions, which is not stated.
- **Comparability of variables** across heterogeneous historical sources (recommendation records, three census enumerations, and institution-specific reports) will require interpretive decisions during execution. The success of this construction is a methodological dependency that cannot be fully assessed from the current proposal text.
- **Coordination across mixed-methods streams** is not described. The schedule states that quantitative and qualitative work proceed in parallel so that findings can inform each other, but no mechanism is specified for how emerging quantitative patterns will feed into qualitative sampling or how qualitative findings will inform quantitative hypothesis refinement. The presence of a project manager is mentioned only in the resource-sharing plan, not in day-to-day research management.

The two-year schedule is logically sequenced—data entry completes before analysis, census datasets and descriptive statistics precede hypothesis testing, and qualitative coding overlaps with quantitative work—but it assumes smooth execution of all preparatory steps and of the variable-construction work. The unspecified volume of qualitative materials and the absence of described coordination mechanisms introduce uncertainty that prevents a high-confidence feasibility judgement, although the work does not appear obviously unachievable on the information provided.

### Ethics and Safety

**Rating:** B | **Confidence:** B

The proposal has a strong ethical foundation in its aim to document historical injustice and recover evidence of resistance. The data-protection measures are appropriate in type and layered appropriately for archival records: password-protected server access, HIPAA-compliant REDCap instruments, exclusion of the linking identifier from exported data, physical security for digitised source copies, and secure carriers for transport. Team training requirements (IRB protocol inclusion, research-responsibility training, confidentiality training, REDCap training) provide administrative safeguards. The qualitative analysis explicitly treats consent as a contested category, distinguishing a signature from meaningful choice and analysing all refusal cases, which respects the agency of the people represented and avoids reproducing the original institutional dehumanisation.

Several ethical limitations require attention:

- **Disclosure risk management is insufficiently specified for this unusually rich and sensitive dataset.** The records contain detailed individual-level variables (age, gender, nativity, surname, diagnosis, institution, family structure) combined with qualitative correspondence. Even after removing explicit identifiers, rare combinations of characteristics can make some records uniquely identifiable. The resource-sharing plan mentions minimising disclosure risk but does not describe the methods to be used (e.g., k-anonymity thresholds, generalisation of rare variables, suppression rules). This is a material gap given the sensitivity of the records and the vulnerable status of the population.
- **Tension between narrative richness and de-identification in the companion archive.** Aim 4 plans to incorporate patient narratives into the companion digital archive. Combined with the demographic detail that quantitative aims will produce, narrative excerpts could render individuals identifiable to family members or community historians. The proposal does not specify procedures for reviewing qualitative excerpts for indirect identification risk—for example, whether names, dates, or unique biographical details will be altered.
- **Absence of engagement with descendant communities or familial-level risks.** People represented in the records may have living children, grandchildren, or other relatives who could experience distress or stigmatisation. The proposal focuses on individual privacy but does not consider familial or community-level risks, nor does it discuss consultation with descendant communities or culturally appropriate dissemination for groups disproportionately affected. This is a gap in the ethical analysis.
- **Downstream data use.** The resource-sharing plan provides for release of de-identified data after publication, but does not describe restrictions on secondary use to prevent re-identification attempts or uses inconsistent with the ethical aims of the original project. Given the coercive origins of the records, stronger conditions on downstream use would be appropriate.

These limitations do not indicate that the research is fundamentally unethical or unsafe, but the current documentation leaves practical risks insufficiently specified. They are resolvable through more detailed procedures, but they affect the current plan’s readiness.

### Management

**Rating:** B | **Confidence:** C

The team composition combines Stern (historical expertise, demonstrated familiarity with the records), Kardia and Harlow (epidemiological and quantitative expertise), and the institutional resource of the University of Michigan’s Institute for Social Research for census microdata work. This is a plausible configuration for the proposed mixed-methods design. The schedule is logically sequenced: data entry completion before analysis, census dataset creation and descriptive statistics before hypothesis testing, parallel quantitative and qualitative work with dissemination across both years.

Management limitations affect confidence in the adequacy of the plan as documented:

- **Division of labour for qualitative analysis is unspecified.** No specific qualifications for Kardia or Harlow in qualitative coding are described, and the proposal does not state who will code letters, ensure interpretive consistency, or manage the qualitative workflow. This places the management of Aim 3 on an assumption that Stern or an unspecified team member will carry that work—plausible given Stern’s qualitative historical expertise, but not documented in the proposal.
- **Coordination mechanism across streams is not described.** The proposal states that quantitative and qualitative work will proceed in parallel so that findings inform each other, but no operational detail is provided for how this integration will be managed. The presence of a project manager is mentioned only in the resource-sharing plan.
- **Contingency for data-entry delays is absent.** The analytical timeline depends on completion of data entry before the 24-month phase begins, but no contingency plan is described.
- **Scope of qualitative materials is unquantified.** Without knowing the volume of documents across all nine institutions, the resource and time demands of qualitative coding cannot be assessed.

These are unspecified arrangements rather than demonstrated defects. The underlying resources and expertise are credible, but the management plan as documented lacks the detail needed to establish that the mixed-methods coordination and the qualitative workload are realistically resourced within the two-year timeline.

## Recommendation

**Current proposal insufficient for support** (revise)

The research direction is demonstrably worthwhile, the preparatory work is substantial, and the Pacific Colony pilot provides credible evidence of feasibility for the core analytical approach. The question addresses a historically and ethically important topic, and the proposed dataset would support a descriptive and interpretive contribution of real value.

However, the proposal’s capacity to deliver its most distinctive components—population-based rate estimation using census denominators, formal hypothesis testing with a case-cohort Poisson regression design, and integrated interpretation of quantitative and qualitative findings—depends on methodological specifications that are not yet documented. The construction of comparable population denominators across discontinuous census years and heterogeneous institutional reports, the identification of institutional residents in historical census microdata, the analytical details of the regression models, the volume and completeness of qualitative materials across all nine institutions, and the mechanisms for integrating the mixed-methods streams are all unspecified to a degree that prevents confident assessment of whether the central aims can be achieved as described. Furthermore, the ethical protections require additional specification to address disclosure risk in the combination of rich demographic data with qualitative narratives and to engage with descendant communities.

These limitations are resolvable. The project is not fundamentally unsound, and a revised plan with explicit documentation of the census enumeration approach, a worked example of denominator assembly for one institution across the full period, specification of the case-cohort and regression design, quantification of the qualitative corpus, and a detailed disclosure risk management plan would substantially strengthen the proposal and could support a judgement of conditional or full support. In its current form, the gaps in specification affect the evidence for the central population-based and integrative components sufficiently that the proposal as submitted does not provide a sufficient route to its stated goals.

## Recommendations for Revision

The following suggestions address limitations that affect the current judgement. They are directed at strengthening the documented plan rather than substituting a different project.

1. **Document the procedure for identifying institutional residents in census microdata.** Describe the census variables used (e.g., group quarters type, institution name or address coding), the anticipated completeness of institutional enumeration in 1920, 1930, and 1940, and any validation against institutional reports.
2. **Provide a worked example of denominator construction across the full period** for one institution, showing how census point-in-time data, institutional annual or biennial reports, and recommendation records will be combined into comparable time series. Address how variables that change in definition across the period (e.g., diagnosis, ethnicity) will be harmonized.
3. **Specify the case-cohort design and Poisson regression analysis.** Describe how the subcohort will be sampled (stratification, period), how the temporal relationship between point-in-time census denominators and accumulating recommendations will be handled, which covariates will be included, and how clustering by institution will be modelled.
4. **Quantify the qualitative corpus across all nine institutions.** State the number of documents by type (forms, correspondence, patient letters, refusal documentation), their distribution across institutions and periods, and whether consistent identifiers permit linkage to the quantitative records.
5. **Describe the mechanism for mixed-methods integration.** Specify how emerging quantitative patterns will guide qualitative sampling, how qualitative findings will inform quantitative hypothesis refinement, and what formal procedures (if any) will be used for triangulation.
6. **Develop a detailed disclosure risk management plan.** Include methods for statistical disclosure control in published tabulations (e.g., cell suppression thresholds, generalisation of rare variables), procedures for reviewing qualitative excerpts for indirect identification risk before public release, and restrictions on secondary use in data-sharing agreements to prevent re-identification attempts.
7. **Address familial and community-level risks.** Discuss whether descendant communities should be engaged in the interpretive process and how the project will handle the possibility of harm to living relatives.
8. **Provide a contingency plan for data-entry delays** and clarify the division of labour for qualitative coding, including who will perform the coding and ensure interpretive consistency.

# Comprehensive Review

## Overall Assessment
This project proposes an integrated computational, psychophysical, and physiological investigation of how neural fluctuation representations support sound detection in noise, with a focus on explaining individual differences between normal-hearing listeners and those with sensorineural hearing loss. The PI has assembled a programme of work that addresses a significant and well-motivated question, combining prospective individualised modelling, human psychophysics, and awake-animal physiology in a conceptually coherent framework. The proposal's strengths lie in the importance of the question, the ambition of a unified computational account across multiple masking paradigms, and the inferential power that could arise from fixing model parameters in one task and predicting performance in others without refitting. These positive features are supported by credible pilot data in simultaneous notched-noise masking and by the PI's established track record in auditory computational neuroscience.

The project has material limitations that affect the weight of the attainable contribution relative to its stated ambitions. The psychophysical classification of individual listeners as energy-users or envelope-users depends on a fixed/roving threshold difference that is modest in magnitude and not yet supported by evidence of within-listener reliability across sessions. Without a documented and stable classification procedure, the predictive chain from behavioural characterisation through to individualised model testing in Aims 2 and 3 is vulnerable to measurement noise. The physiological sampling strategy—distributing approximately 200 neuron responses across a large number of stimulus conditions—risks yielding per-condition samples too small to support the population-level inferences required for direct model–physiology comparison. The forward-masking and spectral-coding aims are at an earlier stage of development, with fewer pilot data and less fully specified experimental parameters, which introduces uncertainty about whether they can deliver their intended contributions within the project period. The sequential dependency of Aims 2 and 3 on the Aim 1 characterisation, coupled with an optimistic time estimate for that characterisation and tightly specified SNHL recruitment criteria, creates a practical risk that the individualised prediction framework—the project's most distinctive scientific feature—cannot be fully executed as described.

The proposed research is ethically proportionate and grounded in appropriate institutional frameworks, though the human- and animal-subjects descriptions lack the operational detail needed to fully assess how participant protections and welfare arrangements will function across the full data-collection effort.

The project remains a worthwhile scientific direction, and the core idea—testing whether a single neural-fluctuation mechanism can account for performance across paradigms while capturing individual differences—deserves investigation. The limitations identified are substantive but potentially addressable through refinement of the psychophysical classification approach, concentration of physiological recordings on the most diagnostic stimulus conditions, and explicit staging of the aims to secure the simultaneous-masking contribution before extending to the less mature forward-masking and spectral-coding components. In its current form, the plan as submitted carries too much risk that the central individual-differences claim cannot be adequately supported by the evidence the design will generate.

## Evaluation by Criterion

### Scientific Value: B (Confidence: B)
The question—how listeners detect sounds in noise when energy-based cues are unreliable, and how sensorineural hearing loss alters the available cues—is of central importance to hearing science and carries clear translational potential for hearing-aid fitting strategies that go beyond the audiogram. The attainable knowledge is substantial: a systematic characterisation of cue use across frequency and hearing status, a computational framework that generates testable predictions across simultaneous masking, forward masking, fricative coding, and pinna-related spectral cues from a single model class, and a potential resolution of the long-standing discrepancy between simultaneous and forward-masking estimates of auditory-filter bandwidth and compression.

The prospective, parameter-constrained modelling approach—where model parameters fixed in Aim 1 predict performance in later aims without refitting—distinguishes this work from studies that demonstrate a model can be adjusted to fit a result. The explicit inclusion of model rejection based on trend-level mismatches strengthens the inferential value.

The attainable contribution is not uniform across aims. The strongest foundation lies in simultaneous notched-noise masking and frequency-dependent cue use, where pilot modelling, human data, and initial IC recordings already exist. Forward masking and the fricative/pinna-cue aims are at a more exploratory stage, with fewer pilot data reported and with experimental parameters not yet specified at a level that permits full evaluation. These later aims therefore carry higher uncertainty about the knowledge that can be realised within the project period.

The proposal does not yet provide pilot evidence that individual model predictions, constrained only by Aim 1 characterisation, accurately predict masking thresholds at the single-listener level. The scientific value of the individualised approach therefore remains a hypothesis to be tested rather than an extrapolation from demonstrated performance. This limitation does not negate the value of the question or the framework, but it does mean that the project's most ambitious contribution is appropriately described as prospective rather than demonstrated.

### Innovation: B (Confidence: B)
The proposal locates its novelty primarily in four respects, each of which represents a legitimate contribution beyond prior work:

First, the application of a single neural-fluctuation mechanism across simultaneous masking, forward masking, and spectral-shape coding is a conceptual innovation. These phenomena have traditionally been treated with separate models, and the systematic programme of cross-paradigm testing can potentially consolidate them. The innovation here lies in the hypothesis and the testing strategy rather than in a completed finding.

Second, individualised models that predict performance across tasks rather than fitting each task separately represent a methodological advance over group-average modelling and post-hoc fitting studies. The pilot finding that listeners with similar audiograms can differ in cue use provides empirical motivation for this approach.

Third, the tight alignment of awake-rabbit IC recordings, human psychophysics, and computational modelling—using the same stimuli, level manipulations, and threshold-readout logic across species—strengthens the inferential link between neural population codes and behaviour. The awake preparation is methodologically appropriate for the study of modulation processing.

Fourth, the extension of fluctuation-based coding to spectral slopes in fricatives and pinna cues, including a binaural dimension, broadens the scope of the model class. The proposal acknowledges that this aim "lays a basis for subsequent studies," appropriately tempering the novelty claim.

The component methods are drawn from published work and are not claimed as novel; the innovation is in their combination and systematic testing. The treatment of forward masking within a fluctuation framework is described as a falsifiable claim but the physiological rationale remains underdeveloped relative to the simultaneous-masking case. This is a limitation in the innovation argument rather than in the planned work itself, since the project is designed to test rather than presuppose the explanation.

### Rigor: C (Confidence: B)
The design contains several well-judged features that support the intended inferences. The signal-detection framework, the use of trend-level model rejection, the characterisation of IC neurons by modulation-transfer function before testing, and the mapping of peripheral dysfunction via audiometry and DPOAEs are all methodologically appropriate and well aligned with the inferential goals.

Four material limitations affect the rigour of the central individual-differences inference:

1. *Psychophysical classification reliability.* The fixed/roving threshold difference that classifies listeners as energy- or envelope-users is modest in magnitude (commonly 2–5 dB) and comparable to measurement noise. The proposal does not describe a formal test–retest protocol, a criterion for classification that accounts for measurement uncertainty, or pilot data demonstrating the stability of this classification across sessions. Without these, the mapping from behavioural tracks to model parameterisation is vulnerable to misclassification, and the predictive validity of the individualised models is weakened.

2. *Physiological sampling per condition.* The planned 200-neuron sample per stimulus condition must be distributed across a large set of manipulations. Once the sample is divided by condition and further by modulation-transfer-function class, the effective sample for a specific population contrast could fall below levels that support stable population-level conclusions. The proposal lacks a power analysis or explicit sampling strategy linking the number of planned conditions to the precision required for a population sensitivity metric.

3. *Single-unit-to-population mapping.* The comparison of physiological and model population thresholds depends on assumptions about noise covariance between simultaneously recorded neurons. Because the physiological data are collected across sessions and animals, the correlation structure between neurons cannot be estimated, and an independence assumption will overestimate population sensitivity. The proposal does not specify how the model–physiology comparison will be made credible in the absence of simultaneous multi-neuron recordings.

4. *Forward-masking design specificity.* The proposed manipulation of masker modulation in forward masking is identified in principle but not specified in terms of modulation frequency, phase, duration, or the predicted interaction with probe-associated fluctuations. The link between the predicted effect and the fluctuation-based explanation cannot be evaluated at the current level of description.

These limitations do not render the project uninformative; the computational and qualitative physiological components remain well positioned to generate testable predictions and reject some model variants. The risk concentrates in the quantitative, individual-level claims that require reliable behavioural classification, adequate physiological sampling, and a defensible population-level readout.

### Feasibility: C (Confidence: B)
The project is grounded in established methods and a capable research environment. The PI's expertise is demonstrated, consultant support covers the additional needed disciplines, and the laboratory facilities are appropriate. The use of awake-rabbit tetrode recordings is an established technique in the PI's laboratory, reducing methods-development risk. The computational framework builds on prior published models.

Three feasibility concerns are material to delivery of the stated aims:

1. *Human testing burden.* The Aim 1 characterisation is estimated at "no more than three hours," but the required interleaved tracks at multiple frequencies and levels with four repetitions and discard/repeat criteria could easily extend beyond this estimate, particularly for older or less-practiced SNHL participants. If characterisation exceeds three hours, the total commitment across Aims 1–3 will exceed the stated 12–15 hours, increasing attrition risk and threatening completion of the individualised-prediction framework.

2. *SNHL recruitment.* The eligibility criteria impose narrow, threshold-based constraints (e.g., thresholds exceeding at least two of 26 dB at 2 kHz, 30 dB at 3 kHz, or 35 dB at 4 kHz, while also remaining ≤ 60 dB HL from 250–8000 Hz, with between-ear differences ≤ 15 dB). Recruiting 30 listeners meeting these criteria and willing to commit to 6–8 weeks of testing is likely to be challenging. The proposal does not describe a contingency if recruitment falls short, which would affect statistical power for the individual-patterns approach central to the scientific strategy. A discrepancy between stated recruitment totals (30+15 vs. 40) adds ambiguity.

3. *Sequential dependencies and coordination.* The individualised-prediction framework requires that Aim 1 characterisation is completed, model parameters are estimated, and predictions are generated before the relevant Aim 2 and Aim 3 data are collected for each listener. This is a critical path with no described contingency for delays or ambiguous parameter estimates. The proposal does not specify milestones, integration meetings, or a data-sharing timetable to coordinate the modelling, physiology, and psychophysics components. The risk is that model refinement continues concurrently with data collection, making predictions post-hoc rather than prospective.

These factors do not render the project impossible, but they introduce substantial risk that the individualised-prediction framework cannot be fully realised within the proposed scope and timeline without additional contingency planning.

### Ethics and Safety: B (Confidence: C)
The proposed activities are of a type for which institutional frameworks (IRB, IACUC) provide standard protections, and the submitted text invokes those frameworks. The screening protocol for human participants is detailed and appropriate, and the 100 dB SPL maximum presentation level is a concrete safeguard. The animal protocol describes anaesthesia, analgesia, habituation, daily two-hour passive-listening sessions, and welfare monitoring—all consistent with standard practice for awake-animal electrophysiology.

The human-subjects description is brief relative to the testing burden. The proposal does not describe how session duration, cumulative exposure, and fatigue will be monitored and managed across repeated visits, nor does it specify session limits or discontinuation criteria. The numerical discrepancy in participant targets and the absence of explicit recruitment contingencies are administrative points that affect completeness rather than safety.

The animal-subjects description does not specify the number of animals, the expected duration of data collection per animal, or the criteria for assessing habituation progress and for early termination from the protocol. Without these figures, the overall animal burden and the proportionality of use to scientific goals cannot be fully assessed, even though the daily session structure is inherently low-risk.

Data-sharing plans (laboratory website) are mentioned but not elaborated with respect to de-identification of human behavioural data, access controls, or long-term repository deposition.

These unspecified elements do not indicate that the work is ethically unacceptable or unsafe. The material limitation is that the operational detail needed to confirm that the stated protections will function across the full data-collection effort is not provided in the supplied text, which constrains confidence in the evaluation.

### Management: C (Confidence: B)
The PI's expertise and the coverage of additional disciplines through consultants provide a credible governance structure. The laboratory resources—booths, auditory equipment, and computing—are appropriate. The division of modelling, physiology, and psychophysics within each aim is conceptually coherent. The five-year duration is a standard timeframe for a programme of this scope.

The absence of explicit project management elements is the primary limitation. The sequential dependency of Aims 2 and 3 on Aim 1 requires rapid model turnaround for each listener within a 6–8 week testing window; the proposal does not describe how this will be coordinated, how model parameters will be selected when behavioural cue estimates are ambiguous, or how delays in one component will be managed without cascading effects. Milestones, integration meetings, and a data-sharing timetable are not specified. The dense within-aim coordination among three methodologies—each with its own logistical demands—requires active management that is not elaborated.

The computational framework involves open-ended analytical choices (spontaneous-rate fibre populations, covariance structure, MTF class combinations) that are identified as areas for exploration but lack decision rules or completion criteria. Without these, the boundary between model development and model testing can blur, undermining the prospective nature of the predictions.

The proposal's reliance on distributed, general-purpose equipment rather than a single unique instrument provides some resilience against equipment failure. The absence of named personnel beyond the PI and the unspecified qualifications of consultants are not themselves evidence of incapacity, but they leave the management assessment dependent on the PI's capacity to oversee all three methodological streams simultaneously.

## Recommendation

**Current proposal insufficient for support (revise)**

The project addresses a worthwhile scientific question with a conceptually coherent and methodologically grounded approach. The core idea—that a single neural-fluctuation mechanism, implemented in individualised models and tested prospectively across paradigms—can explain individual differences in masked detection—is a significant and testable hypothesis. The pilot data in simultaneous notched-noise masking demonstrate that the question is tractable and that the phenomenon of frequency-dependent cue use is real. The PI's expertise and the laboratory infrastructure are credible.

However, the plan as submitted contains substantive problems that affect its ability to deliver the central contribution. The psychophysical classification procedure on which the individualised-prediction framework depends has not been demonstrated to be reliable at the single-listener level, and the proposal does not specify the test–retest protocol or decision criteria that would establish that reliability. The physiological sampling strategy, distributing a fixed neuron count across a large number of conditions, risks producing per-condition samples that are too small to support the intended population-level inferences. The forward-masking and spectral-coding aims, while conceptually interesting, are under-specified in their experimental parameters and under-supported by pilot data. The sequential dependencies among aims, combined with an optimistic estimate of the Aim 1 characterisation time and narrow SNHL recruitment criteria, create a practical risk that the project cannot complete the individualised-prediction loop as designed.

These limitations are not decisive objections to the scientific direction. A revised plan could address them by: (a) providing pilot data on the within-listener reliability of the fixed/roving classification and specifying a statistically defensible classification criterion; (b) concentrating physiological recordings on a smaller set of maximally diagnostic stimulus conditions and providing a sampling analysis that demonstrates adequate power for the population contrasts of interest; (c) developing the forward-masking and spectral-coding manipulations to a level of specificity that permits evaluation of their discriminant power; and (d) introducing explicit staging, milestones, and contingency plans that protect the core simultaneous-masking contribution while acknowledging uncertainty in the less mature aims. With such revisions, the project would be well positioned to deliver a significant consolidation of our understanding of neural fluctuation coding and its relevance to hearing loss.

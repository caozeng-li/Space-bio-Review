# Comprehensive Review: PRECISE (Prediction-Based Resource-Efficient Colonoscopy Navigation in Safety-Net Settings) 

## Overall Assessment

The PRECISE proposal addresses a clinically and operationally significant problem: the high rate of non-completion of follow-up colonoscopy after a positive fecal immunochemical test (FIT) among patients served by community health centers. The project aims to test whether a risk-stratified telephone navigation program—directing navigator resources toward patients with moderate or low predicted adherence, based on a previously developed risk model—improves colonoscopy completion compared with usual care. The proposal integrates external validation of the prediction model, a pragmatic randomized trial embedded in an existing health system, comprehensive economic evaluation stratified by risk, and qualitative process evaluation. The team brings relevant expertise, established access to the Sea Mar Community Health Centers provides a suitable study setting, and pilot data anchors planning parameters in observed local numbers. The study design is carefully specified in many respects, including the separation of model validation from the trial, outcome ascertainment with blinded chart audits, fidelity monitoring, and transparent power calculations.

The proposal’s central strength is its practical focus: it asks not simply whether navigation works, but whether scarce resources can be directed efficiently to patients most likely to benefit. The economic evaluation stratified by predicted risk, the use of embedded clinic staff as navigators, and the explicit attention to sustainability distinguish this from prior work. These features make the project a meaningful incremental contribution to implementation science in cancer screening equity.

The proposal is, however, constrained by a fundamental design decision that limits its ability to answer the most ambitious version of its motivating question. The two-arm design (Targeted Navigation vs. Usual Care) cannot determine whether a risk-stratified strategy is superior to, or more cost-effective than, a universal navigation approach that offers the intervention to all patients regardless of predicted risk. The study can establish whether navigation improves completion in the selected moderate- and low-adherence groups—a valuable finding—but cannot directly compare targeting strategies. This limitation interacts with the modest discrimination of the prediction model (c-statistic 0.65 in development), the underpowered moderation analyses, and several gaps in the specification of consent procedures, navigator workload protection, and outcome data access. These issues, while individually manageable, collectively reduce the precision and scope of the conclusions the project can support, particularly regarding the resource-allocation question that the proposal highlights as its primary motivation.

## Evaluation by Criterion

### 1. Scientific Value
**Rating: B | Confidence: B**

The proposal addresses a well-defined, real-world problem with clear relevance to safety-net health systems and health equity. The question of whether prediction-based triage can improve the value of navigation services—rather than simply whether navigation works—is a practically important refinement of prior research. The planned contributions are substantive: external validation of a risk model in a new population, a pragmatic test of the targeted strategy’s effect size, detailed cost and cost-effectiveness data stratified by risk, and qualitative insights into barriers and implementation. The stratified economic evaluation is a particular strength, directly informing decisions by health systems and payers.

However, the scientific value is constrained by the chosen comparator. The two-arm design (Targeted Navigation vs. Usual Care) cannot establish that a targeted approach is superior to, or more cost-effective than, providing navigation to all patients regardless of risk. Since the proposal frames its central contribution around efficient resource direction, this is a material limitation. The attainable knowledge is the effect of navigation within a risk-defined group, which is valuable but does not fully resolve the targeting question. A three-arm trial (Targeted Navigation vs. Universal Navigation vs. Usual Care) would be required for that direct comparison. Additionally, reliance on a single prediction model developed in one network and validated in another single system limits the generalizability of the targeting strategy. The scientific yield is highly contingent on the model’s performance in the validation phase.

### 2. Innovation
**Rating: B | Confidence: B**

The proposal represents a meaningful synthesis of known components into a new, pragmatic model. The use of a risk-prediction model to triage patients before navigation delivery is the most novel aspect, directly testing a decision-support tool in a safety-net context. The focus on the follow-up colonoscopy phase—rather than initial FIT screening—is a distinct shift from many prior navigation programs, and the barriers at this stage are specific and well-grounded in preliminary work. The embedded delivery model, using existing Sea Mar clinical staff as navigators, addresses the sustainability challenge that has limited prior navigation studies and is an important innovation. The stratified economic evaluation adds novelty by generating cost-effectiveness estimates for moderate- and low-adherence groups separately.

The advance is incremental rather than transformative. The proposal acknowledges that navigation programs have been shown to increase colonoscopy completion, and the prediction model’s discrimination is modest. The innovation lies in the proposition that this level of classification is sufficient to improve outcomes when used pragmatically to concentrate a proven intervention. This is a practical synthesis of existing prediction science and implementation principles, adapted for a specific safety-net system. The high-adherence group is observed without intervention, which is reasonable but limits exploration of model performance in that stratum.

### 3. Rigor
**Rating: B | Confidence: B**

The design contains many well-specified elements that support the intended inferences. The separation of Phase I (external validation in a distinct historical cohort) from Phase II (prospective trial) is appropriate to the research stage. Validation metrics are clearly specified, including a predefined threshold for inferior discrimination (c-statistic decline ≥0.05). Randomization within strata defined by predicted adherence level, age, and sex is clearly described, with power calculations transparently anchored to local planning data. The primary outcome—chart-confirmed colonoscopy within one year—includes blinded chart audits to supplement EHR codes, addressing a known source of measurement error. Fidelity monitoring via recorded calls, competency checklists, contamination checks, and dose measurement supports internal validity and interpretation of results. The explicit identification of underpowered analyses (interaction tests, cancer-treatment timing) demonstrates design transparency.

Several limitations affect the strength of the inferences the study can support. First, the modest discrimination of the prediction model (c-statistic 0.65) limits how precisely the trial can test whether navigation effects vary by predicted risk. The moderation analyses are underpowered to detect anything but very large interaction effects (odds ratios of 2.98 or 0.34 under the clustered design). The proposal’s framing of moderation as hypothesis testing is inconsistent with the trial’s power; a shift to estimation with confidence intervals would align the inferential language with the design’s capability. Second, the usual-care comparator is defined by existing processes that may change over 18 months of enrollment, and the proposal lacks systematic description or time-series measurement of usual-care fidelity beyond contamination sampling. Third, dependence on EHR data for both prediction and outcomes creates a potential circularity if predictor completeness is associated with health-system engagement. Fourth, the qualitative component is timed late (months 34–40) without an explicit mixed-methods integration framework, limiting its contribution to interpreting effectiveness findings. These issues do not threaten the primary inference—that navigation improves completion in the moderate/low-adherence groups—but collectively reduce the precision and nuance of secondary and moderation claims.

### 4. Feasibility
**Rating: C | Confidence: C**

The proposal benefits from relevant team expertise, established access to an appropriate population, and pilot data that ground planning estimates in observed numbers. The 28 Sea Mar clinics, integrated EHR, and known annual volume of positive FIT results provide a solid platform for the trial. The navigation contact sequence and workload estimates (≈970 hours for 462 patients) permit resource forecasting. The sequential timeline with distinct phases, fidelity monitoring, and an advisory board supports coordination and quality.

Material uncertainties remain that affect confidence in delivery. The prediction model’s performance in the Sea Mar historical cohort is a critical dependency; the proposal mentions recalibration options but does not specify operational triggers or decision authority if validation fails substantially. Navigator availability is insufficiently documented: navigators are Sea Mar employees, but the number of navigators, their protected FTE, and how the navigation role fits alongside existing clinical duties are not specified. The fidelity plan requires sustained attention and technical capability within busy clinics. Outcome data completeness depends on access to colonoscopy reports from external GI facilities; the proposal does not confirm whether data-sharing agreements or reliable retrieval processes exist. The economic evaluation requires integration of multiple cost data sources, but analyst availability and data extraction feasibility are asserted rather than demonstrated. The schedule has limited float: delays in model validation or enrollment could compress the intervention period and reduce power. Finally, reliance on a single health system concentrates risk from any system-wide disruption (EHR change, leadership turnover, financial pressure). These conditions could be met, but the current level of specification leaves material deliverables dependent on unresolved practical arrangements.

### 5. Ethics and Safety
**Rating: C | Confidence: C**

The proposal describes proportionate protections for several activities: telephone navigation supplements rather than alters clinical care, post-trial navigation is offered to usual-care and surveillance patients conditional on effectiveness, and verbal consent is specified for recorded calls. The focus on patients with positive FIT—who are at elevated clinical risk from non-adherence—is ethically appropriate. The advisory board’s composition includes patient and community representation.

Significant gaps in the ethics documentation prevent a complete assessment. The proposal does not describe the informed consent process for trial enrollment: when and how patients learn about the study, randomization, or their right to decline while receiving standard care. The use of a risk model to classify patients into adherence strata—and the differential treatment that follows—raises questions about disclosure and predictive equity that are not addressed. Validation metrics do not explicitly include assessment of model performance across language or demographic subgroups, which is relevant given the Spanish-speaking population served. Dual-role dynamics are a concern: navigators are simultaneously care providers and research interventionists, and clinic staff aware of assignments could provide differential attention to usual-care patients. The 10% contamination check addresses treatment integrity but not the ethical question of diminished attention. Additional unspecified elements include qualitative data storage and retention safeguards, and the scope of clinical-record access for blinded chart auditors. These are gaps in the supplied documentation rather than demonstrated ethical failures; the planned activities carry identifiable ethical dimensions, several of which are addressed, but key protections cannot be confirmed as proportionate from the material provided.

### 6. Management
**Rating: C | Confidence: C**

The team composition covers required competencies—trial design, prediction modeling, navigation, economic evaluation, qualitative research, and EHR data management—and the Sea Mar partnership provides clinical-operational leadership. Planned coordination structures include a quarterly advisory board, progressive navigator supervision (weekly transitioning to biweekly), and fidelity monitoring with booster training. The five-year timeline is sequenced logically, with activities linked to months and reasonable allocation of time for analysis and dissemination.

The management plan lacks specificity in areas critical to delivery. The proposal names the PI and Sea Mar medical director, but does not identify other key personnel or describe their protected time. Navigator supervision is detailed, but the navigators’ own employment terms, FTE allocation, and integration into clinic workflows are not documented. No monitoring plan with trigger points for schedule adjustments is described, despite tight coupling of early-phase deliverables (model validation, baseline data) with later milestones (randomization, intervention start). The statistician’s role in randomization is clear, but how the study coordinates with clinic referral workflows—and how unblinding of predicted probability is prevented during clinical contacts—is described only conceptually. The reliance on a single health system, while offering control, concentrates operational risk without a stated mitigation plan. These unspecified conditions create uncertainty about whether the project can maintain coordination and resource sufficiency over five years.

## Recommendation: **Conditional support (conditional_support)**

The proposal describes a worthwhile direction—testing whether prediction-model-based triage can improve the value of navigation services in safety-net settings—and many elements of the design are well specified. The primary limitation is the two-arm design, which cannot fully answer the resource-allocation question that motivates the project. However, the study can still produce valuable evidence on the effect of navigation within risk-defined groups, the performance of the prediction model in a new population, and the costs of a targeted strategy. These contributions justify proceeding, provided specific conditions are met.

**Premises for conditional support:**
1. **Model validation contingency**: The project must specify operational triggers and decision pathways if the prediction model’s external validation shows substantially inferior discrimination (beyond the stated ≥0.05 c-statistic decline) or poor calibration that cannot be remedied by recalibration. Without this, the trial’s rationale for targeting is undermined.
2. **Consent and equity specification**: An adequate informed consent process for trial enrollment must be documented, and the validation plan must include explicit assessment of predictive equity across language and demographic subgroups relevant to the Sea Mar population.
3. **Navigator capacity and data access**: Protected FTE for navigators, their integration into clinic workflows, and reliable access to external GI colonoscopy reports must be confirmed. Without these, the intended navigation dose and outcome ascertainment are at risk.

If these premises are satisfied—through documentation already present in a fuller application or through revision—the project offers a supportable scope of work with clear scientific and practical value. If they cannot be confirmed, the feasibility and ethics concerns would warrant revision before the project proceeds in its current form.

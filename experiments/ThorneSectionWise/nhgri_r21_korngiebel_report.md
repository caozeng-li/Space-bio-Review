# Comprehensive Scientific Review

## Overall Assessment

This is a carefully conceived formative study targeting a practically important knowledge gap at the intersection of genomic medicine, patient-portal design, and bioethics. The project asks what patients need and prefer when genetic test results are returned electronically through patient portals, and how those preferences can inform empirically grounded prototype reports and an ethical Points to Consider framework. The proposal situates itself correctly as exploratory, user-centred design (UCD) research that precedes later clinical-implementation studies, and its scope is well matched to an R21 mechanism. The investigative team brings relevant and complementary expertise across qualitative methods, genomic bioethics, genetic counseling, and usability testing, supported by a layered advisory structure that supplies clinical, informatics, and external implementation perspectives. The proposed sequence—concept-generation interviews (Aims 1 and 2) feeding iterative prototype development and testing with both portal users and non-users (Aim 3), embedded within an evolving ethical framework—is logically coherent and addresses a genuine knowledge gap that the cited literature does not fill.

The review identifies three cross-cutting strengths that run through multiple evaluation criteria. First, the deliberate inclusion of non-portal-users from a safety-net clinic (Harborview Family Medicine) is a distinctive design choice that operationalises equity concerns within the prototype itself rather than treating equity as a secondary consideration. Second, the Points to Consider ethical framework is assigned a substantive analytical role—it shapes data collection, is revised in light of patient findings, and emerges as a study output rather than a static compliance instrument. Third, the project targets concrete, usable artefacts (prototype report templates and a refined ethical framework) that could directly inform EHR implementation in the Epic environment.

The review also identifies material limitations that constrain what the study, as currently documented, can credibly deliver. The most significant concern spans rigor and ethics: the proposal does not specify a distress protocol for interviews that will engage participants around inherited-cancer-risk findings and other potentially troubling genetic results, despite identifying psychological discomfort as a study risk and making concern thresholds a primary object of investigation. The English-only recruitment restricts generalisability in ways the proposal acknowledges but does not fully address in the ethical framework's design. Methodologically, the pathway from interview findings to prototype design decisions, and from empirical data to ethical framework revision, is described at a level of generality that weakens confidence in the auditable chain of inference. Operationally, the compressed schedule for three complete usability-testing cycles, the ambitious recruitment targets for narrow Aim 2 result categories, and the concentration of key analytical tasks on the lead investigator create dependencies whose resolution is not documented.

These limitations are substantive but not disqualifying. The study's strengths in question importance, design coherence, team expertise, and institutional access support a judgement that the proposed direction is worthwhile. The limitations can be addressed through specified methodological elaboration and practical planning without redesigning the project's fundamental architecture. The recommendation of conditional support reflects this balance: the project is supportable contingent on resolving specific factual premises about the distress protocol, the design-translation process, and the recruitment feasibility, each of which is identified with its implications below.

## Evaluation by Criterion

### Scientific Value: A (Confidence: B)

The research question—what do patients need and prefer when genetic results varying in sensitivity and complexity are returned electronically—is practically important and under-investigated. The project stands to produce differentiated, patient-derived accounts of concern thresholds and support needs across four genetic-result categories (pharmacogenomics, treatable adult-onset conditions, cancer-risk findings, negative results), iteratively tested prototype reports embedded in an Epic environment, and a data-refined ethical Points to Consider framework. These are substantive descriptive, methodological, and practical contributions for a formative study at this stage.

The attainable knowledge is bounded by the English-only recruitment, the dependence of the differentiated-result contribution on how patient accounts map onto the preselected clinical categories, and the limited analytical specification for detecting or interpreting differences across the demographic strata that the proposal targets for recruitment. These do not nullify the contribution; they define its scope. The study would provide the first patient-driven, context-rich account of electronic genetic-result preferences developed through an explicit UCD sequence in an Epic setting—a valuable increment over the clinician-focused and descriptive-only prior work cited.

---

### Innovation: B (Confidence: B)

The project's innovation is integrative and context-specific rather than paradigm-shifting, which is appropriate for the research type and stage. Three linked moves distinguish it from the prior work cited: shifting the empirical locus from clinician judgement to patient-defined thresholds, using patient comparisons across genetic and non-genetic results and across genetically distinct result types; embedding findings in a UCD prototyping cycle that tests designs with both experienced portal users and non-users, bringing equity concerns into the prototype itself; and producing an ethical framework that is iteratively revised in light of empirical data rather than stated as a fixed analysis.

No existing study described in the proposal gathers patient-comparison interview data, uses those data to build and iteratively test EHR-style prototypes across multiple genetic-result categories, and produces a data-informed ethical framework in the same project. The novelty is therefore a meaningful extension of existing patient-outreach and return-of-results research rather than a fundamentally new paradigm. A limitation affecting the innovation claim is that the prototypes appear to be redesigned result displays, while the stated goal includes operationalising the "supplementary versus substitute" delivery-mode continuum. How display prototypes can address delivery mode remains underspecified, which may constrain the project's ability to comment on that dimension through the intended artefacts.

---

### Rigor: C (Confidence: B)

The design sequence—concept-generation interviews followed by iterative usability testing—is logically appropriate for a formative, descriptive study. The qualitative methods (semistructured interviews, directed content analysis with inductive thematic coding, two-coder consensus) and the UCD approach (three-round iterative testing with cognitive usability interviews, counterbalanced presentation, external heuristic walkthroughs) are well-established and well-matched to the descriptive and developmental inferences the project targets.

Four gaps in the methodological documentation, however, weaken confidence that the evidence will fully support the intended inferences. First, the proposal does not specify how thematic saturation will be operationally assessed; the statement that recruitment will continue until "the team judges that major themes have reached idea saturation" lacks stopping criteria, which matters particularly for Aim 2, where ten participants per result-category stratum may not support saturation-based claims of differentiated thresholds. Second, the study relies on single-interview, retrospective accounts of portal-result experiences, without discussion of how recall accuracy or reconstruction might affect accounts of immediate emotional reactions or understanding at the time of result viewing. Third, the pathway from empirical findings to ethical framework revision is not described as a systematic procedure: no specific analytic steps for bioethical analysis (e.g., how normative concepts will be operationalised, challenged, or revised) are named, and the distinction between descriptive summary and normative reasoning in the framework's development is underdetermined. Fourth, the translation step from interview findings to prototype design decisions lacks a documented, auditable process (e.g., affinity diagramming, design workshops, formal requirements mapping); the inferential link rests on team expertise rather than on a specified method. These gaps are characteristic of a proposal that outlines a coherent overall approach but does not yet provide the methodological detail needed to establish that the planned activities are proportionate to the knowledge claims.

### Feasibility: C (Confidence: B)

The investigative team's expertise and the institutional embedding in UW Medicine's Epic environment are clear strengths that support feasibility. The advisory and consultative structures provide relevant domain knowledge, and the recruitment pathways through eCare, Laboratory Medicine, Medical Genetics, and the Harborview safety-net clinic are concrete.

Three principal feasibility risks are documented. The recruitment throughput is ambitious: 164–180 participants across three aims within two years, with Aim 3 requiring 84 participants (42 users, 42 non-users) in three rounds of 28 each, and Aim 2 requiring four result-category strata of ten participants each. The proposal does not specify expected recruitment rates, the size of the eligible pool for the narrow Aim 2 categories (all requiring genetic results received within 12 months through the portal), or the anticipated yield from Harborview for the non-user strata. Second, the schedule assigns three complete cycles of usability testing (recruitment, data collection, transcription, analysis, redesign) plus external consultant review to four quarters (Q5–Q8). The feasibility of completing a full cycle per quarter is not supported by any description of team capacity, expected interview throughput, or turnaround time. Third, the concentration of qualitative data collection, coding, usability analysis, and prototype revision on the lead investigator (with co-lead support from Robins on qualitative analysis) creates key-person risk for a project with three sequential data-collection phases and three rounds of usability testing. These risks do not make the study infeasible, but they make its feasibility contingent on conditions—recruitment yield, scheduling buffer, analytical surge capacity—that are not documented in the proposal.

---

### Ethics and Safety: C (Confidence: B)

The proposal addresses a topic with genuine ethical complexity and integrates ethical analysis into the empirical design in a structured way. The Points to Consider framework is assigned a substantive analytical role, and its three initial domains (beneficence/nonmaleficence, respect for autonomy, justice/equity) shape the interview aims. Data protection procedures (de-identification, secure storage, access restriction, waiver of written consent documentation to minimise identifying links) are appropriate to the interview content. The provision for removing inadvertently supplied third-party identifying information shows attention to the nature of the discussion. The inclusion of non-users from a safety-net setting and the stratified demographic recruitment targets are concrete steps toward examining equity in electronic return.

The central limitation is the absence of a specified distress protocol. The proposal identifies psychological discomfort as a risk—specifically, discomfort discussing inherited risk—and states that participants experiencing distress "will be encouraged to discontinue and directed to assistance if needed." It does not specify what assistance is available, who on the study team is qualified to recognise clinically significant distress, whether a protocol exists for acute reactions, or what follow-up is planned. This matters because the study deliberately recruits participants who have received results with substantial consequences (the inherited colorectal-cancer-risk category) and because the interviews explicitly probe the acceptability of electronic return without personal counseling—questions that may reactivate the experience of receiving a threatening result. The underspecification affects confidence in participant protection and in the unforced quality of preference data. Two additional limitations are noted. The English-only restriction, while a defensible scoping choice for a two-year exploratory study, means that language as a barrier to equitable electronic return remains unexamined; the proposal does not discuss how the ethical framework will acknowledge this as a constraint on its justice recommendations. The mechanisms for validating the framework's normative claims (beyond patient data informing revision) and for managing the clinical–research boundary during recruitment (patients may perceive the invitation as connected to their clinical care through the same institutional channels that delivered their genetic result) are not specified.

---

### Management: C (Confidence: B)

The investigative team's complementary expertise (formative research and UCD, qualitative methods, genomic bioethics, genetic counseling) and the layered advisory structure (five-member advisory board, two external electronic-prototype consultants, three-member mentoring committee) provide relevant capacity and domain knowledge. The schedule logic respects dependencies between aims, with generative qualitative work preceding prototype development and iterative testing.

The documented management limitations are operational rather than structural. The project's analytical tasks—qualitative data collection, coding, usability analysis, and prototype revision—are concentrated on the lead investigator, with the graduate assistant named only for prototype revision. The advisory board meets twice yearly and is consulted "as needed" but is not described as providing analytical surge capacity. The external consultant heuristic walkthroughs between rounds 2 and 3 lack specified timing, format, and availability commitments, creating a coordination dependency that could delay round 3 if the walkthroughs are not completed on schedule or require substantial prototype rework. The proposal mentions a graduate assistant and participant payments ($50 per session) but does not detail the staffing, incentive budget, or logistical infrastructure (scheduling, transcription, data management) that would support 164–180 interview sessions within two years. The Harborview advisory-board member's operational role in facilitating clinic-based recruitment for the Aim 3 non-user strata is implied but not explicitly described. These are gaps in the documented management arrangements rather than evidence of incapacity; they limit confidence that the stated timeline and coordination structure are adequately resourced at the level of detail supplied.

## Recommendation: Conditional Support

**Rationale.** The project targets a worthwhile knowledge gap, and the proposed direction—patient-driven, category-specific evidence on portal return of genetic results, integrated into iteratively tested prototypes and a data-refined ethical framework—represents a substantive formative contribution. The team's expertise and institutional access provide a credible foundation for the work. The recommendation is conditional rather than unqualified support because three specific factual premises must be resolved before the plan can be judged proportionate to its goals. If these premises are satisfied, the project has a sufficient route to its stated aims; if they are not, the study's ability to deliver the intended knowledge contribution and to protect participants appropriately would be materially compromised.

**Premise 1: A specified distress protocol is in place.** The study must document the assistance that will be available to participants who experience distress during interviews about potentially troubling genetic findings, who on the team is qualified to recognise clinically significant distress, and what follow-up is planned. This premise is critical for both participant protection and the validity of preference data that inform the ethical framework. Without it, the study's ethics arrangements are underspecified for the risks the proposal itself identifies.

**Premise 2: The design-translation process is operationally documented.** The proposal should specify how interview findings will be translated into prototype design decisions (e.g., through affinity diagramming, design workshops, or formal requirements mapping) and how the empirical findings will systematically inform revision of the ethical framework (e.g., through specified analytic steps for bioethical analysis, such as reflective equilibrium or empirical bioethics methods). Satisfying this premise would strengthen the auditable chain of inference from patient data to the artefacts and normative claims that constitute the project's primary contribution.

**Premise 3: Recruitment rates and scheduling buffers are realistically assessed.** The team should provide expected recruitment yields for the Aim 2 result-category strata (particularly pharmacogenomic results returned through the portal within 12 months) and for the Aim 3 non-user strata through Harborview, together with an operational plan for completing three usability-testing cycles with external review within four quarters. This premise addresses the principal feasibility risk: that the project's compressed timeline and ambitious recruitment targets may not be achievable without additional analytical capacity or schedule contingency.

**Implications.** If these premises are satisfied—through protocol elaboration, operational planning, and specification of the ethical-framework revision pathway—the project can proceed with a well-bounded formative contribution: patient-driven evidence on portal return of genetic results, iteratively tested prototypes, and an ethical framework whose empirical grounding and limitations are transparently documented. If any premise cannot be satisfied in its current scope, the project's aims would need adjustment: for example, reducing the Aim 2 result categories, extending the usability-testing timeline, or narrowing the ethical framework's claimed normative status to a descriptive summary of patient preferences informed by ethical categories. The conditional support recommendation recognises that these are resolvable planning matters, not fundamental redesign requirements, and that their resolution would allow the project's genuine strengths in question importance, design coherence, and team capacity to be realised.

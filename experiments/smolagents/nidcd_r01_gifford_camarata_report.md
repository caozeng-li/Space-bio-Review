## Image-Guided Cochlear Implant Programming: Pediatric Speech, Language, and Literacy

**Mechanism:** R01
**Review date:** 2026-10-28
**Source:** src-8269e77e77f6

## Overall Assessment

This proposal addresses a consequential and underexplored question: whether anatomically guided deactivation of cochlear implant (CI) electrodes—image-guided cochlear implant programming (IGCIP)—can improve auditory function in prelingually deafened children, and how any resulting changes in auditory input relate to downstream speech, language, phonological awareness, and reading outcomes. The question sits at a genuine knowledge gap. The team's own published work (DeFreese et al., *Scientific Reports*, 2024; Camarata et al., *Scientific Reports*, 2026) confirms that spectral and temporal resolution do not reliably predict speech recognition in pediatric CI users, unlike the well-established adult relationship, and that children with CIs can achieve typical language and reading comprehension despite persistently depressed phonological processing. The proposal is grounded in these findings and uses IGCIP as a controlled intervention to probe the auditory-developmental pathways that might explain them.

The project has several substantial strengths: a double-blind, randomized waitlist-control design; complementary auditory, speech, language, and literacy measurement batteries of considerable breadth; thoughtful inclusion of acoustic speech-production measures that can detect subclinical change; and a statistical plan that allows informative path analyses even in the absence of a significant group-level treatment effect. The PIs have deep relevant expertise, and their preliminary data—41 pediatric IGCIP cases with variable but promising outcomes—provide credible feasibility evidence.

The central limitation is that the proposal's primary causal claim—that IGCIP will produce a detectable group-level auditory benefit in children—rests on a mechanism (reduced channel interaction through selective electrode deactivation) whose average effect size in the pediatric population is uncertain. The preliminary data show that most children (69–79%) experienced no statistically significant change on individual speech measures, and the published meta-analysis (Yang et al., *Otology & Neurotology*, 2022) reports nominal and mostly non-significant pooled effect sizes for IGCIP across seven studies. The proposal acknowledges this variability and builds alternative analytic pathways around it, which is scientifically honest and methodologically appropriate. However, the design's capacity to test the treatment contrast itself—as distinct from individual-difference pathways—is modest given the sample size and the anticipated effect heterogeneity.

On balance, this is a well-conceived investigation of an important clinical-scientific question. The pathway analyses are likely to yield valuable descriptive and hypothesis-generating findings regardless of the treatment effect magnitude. The main reservation concerns whether the proposed sample supports confident detection of the hypothesized group-level auditory benefit, which affects the strength of Aim 1 and, by extension, the causal interpretation of the downstream mediation models. This does not make the study unsupportable, but it does constrain what can be claimed from the between-group comparison.

## Scientific Value: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

The proposal addresses a question with both clinical and basic-science significance. Pediatric CI users, even those implanted early, continue to show language and reading outcomes below age expectations (proposal Figure 1; replicated in Niparko et al., *JAMA*, 2010; Dettman et al., *Otology & Neurotology*, 2016). The quality of the electrical signal delivered by the implant is a plausible, modifiable constraint on development, yet the relationship between peripheral auditory fidelity and higher-level outcomes in children is poorly characterized. The proposal's own preliminary work and the team's published studies (DeFreese et al., 2024; Camarata et al., 2026) establish that the adult pattern—spectral resolution predicting speech recognition—does not hold in children, and that children may develop language and reading through pathways that do not depend on fine spectral detail. This creates a genuine need for an intervention that can alter the electrode-neural interface and for measurement of the resulting cascade.

The attainable contributions are several and well-specified:

1. **Descriptive:** The first randomized evidence on IGCIP's average effect on spectral, temporal, and spectrotemporal resolution and speech recognition in school-age children, with repeated measures over 24 months.
2. **Mechanistic:** Cross-lagged and path analyses testing whether auditory changes mediate phonological awareness, language, and reading growth—a direct empirical test of the lexical restructuring and alternative-pathway hypotheses.
3. **Methodological:** Validation of acoustic speech-production measures as outcomes sensitive to CI programming changes that may not register on standardized articulation scores.
4. **Clinical:** Evidence on whether IGCIP should be adopted as a standard programming approach for children, including safety and retention data.

The proposal explicitly states that language and literacy analyses "are not contingent on a significant average intervention effect." This is a prudent hedge, but it also reveals the two-tier structure of the scientific contribution. The stronger contribution—a causal test of whether improved auditory input drives developmental gains—depends on a detectable intervention effect. The weaker but still valuable contribution—characterizing individual differences in auditory change and their developmental correlates—is available regardless. Both are worth pursuing, but they represent different knowledge claims, and the proposal sometimes blurs this distinction in its framing.

The inclusion of quality-of-life and functional auditory questionnaires (PEACH, PedsQL, Auditory Skills Checklist) is a welcome addition that addresses outcomes meaningful to families beyond laboratory measures.

## Innovation: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

IGCIP itself is not novel in absolute terms. Noble et al. (*Otology & Neurotology*, 2016) published the initial pediatric results with this same team, and the technique has been under investigation for over a decade. The Yang et al. (2022) systematic review identified seven prospective studies of clinically implemented IGCIP through August 2021. What is novel in this proposal is the combination of IGCIP with: (a) a randomized waitlist-control design (all prior IGCIP studies appear to have used within-subject pre-post comparisons without a control group); (b) a comprehensive developmental outcomes battery spanning auditory resolution, speech recognition, PA, language, reading, and acoustic speech production; and (c) formal path-analytic modeling of direct and mediated developmental pathways.

The innovation therefore lies primarily in the design and the integrative analytic framework, not in the intervention technique itself. The proposal's distinctive contribution is to use IGCIP as a controlled experimental perturbation to test hypotheses about auditory-cognitive-linguistic development, rather than simply to evaluate a clinical programming method. This reframing—from "does IGCIP work?" to "what do changes in the electrode-neural interface reveal about how children with CIs develop language and literacy?"—is a genuine conceptual advance.

The spectral-versus-temporal comparison also addresses a substantive gap. The proposal's preliminary finding that spectral modulation detection was not associated with speech recognition in 41 children, while temporal modulation at 128 Hz showed associations in a small pilot (n=14), motivates the formal comparison. The published DeFreese et al. (2024) paper, which appears to draw from the same or overlapping cohort, confirms that neither spectral nor temporal resolution correlated significantly with speech recognition after correction. The proposal extends this cross-sectional finding longitudinally and tests whether IGCIP alters one or both dimensions.

The acoustic speech-production outcome battery is an underutilized approach in CI research and represents methodological innovation. The preliminary cases showing acoustic changes (high-frequency energy, vowel precision) without standard-score changes support the premise that subclinical measures can detect intervention effects invisible to norm-referenced articulation tests.

## Rigor: C (Moderate) — Confidence: B (High)

### Design strengths

The double-blind, randomized waitlist-control design is appropriate and well-specified. Permuted-block randomization (blocks of four), restricted allocation access, identical procedures across arms, and assessor blinding are standard protections against allocation and ascertainment bias. The waitlist design is ethically defensible given that all participants receive the intervention by 12 months and the clinical equipoise around IGCIP in children.

The baseline optimization protocol—ensuring CI-aided thresholds of 20–25 dB HL and verifying hearing-aid settings before randomization—is a strength that reduces the risk that simple clinical underfitting explains group differences. The two-month stabilization period after map changes is appropriate. Equipment and listening checks at every visit address hardware-related confounds.

The measurement battery is comprehensive and well-justified. The auditory resolution tasks (spectral modulation detection at 0.5 and 1 cycle/octave; temporal modulation detection at 4, 32, and 128 Hz; spectrotemporal combinations) are established nonlexical probes. The speech recognition battery (CNC words, nonword repetition, BabyBio sentences, BKB-SIN, adaptive HINT) covers quiet, fixed-noise, and adaptive-noise conditions. The language battery (ROWPVT-4, PPVT-4, TACL-4, CELF-4, EOWPVT-4, SPELT-3) distinguishes receptive from expressive and vocabulary from grammar. The PA and reading batteries (CTOPP-2, TAPS-3, plus experimental PA tasks; WRMT-III, GORT-5) are standard. Working memory assessment with both verbal and nonverbal tasks, and the check for spoken-number and word recognition to separate access from recall, is a thoughtful detail.

The statistical plan's explicit embrace of individual-difference analyses—cross-lagged models, path analyses, effect-size estimation—even without a treatment main effect is methodologically sound and protects the study's scientific value against uncertain treatment efficacy.

### Design limitations

**Power for the primary treatment contrast.** With 30 analyzed participants per arm, the design provides 80% power for an effect of d ≈ 0.67–0.74 (Cohen's d). The preliminary data provide the best available estimate of what effect size to expect. In the 41-child preliminary series, the experimental-ear mean BKB-SIN improvement was from 13.0 to 11.8 dB SNR (n=19), roughly a 1.2 dB improvement. In the bilateral condition, the mean improved from 8.2 to 6.8 dB. These are group means that include responders and nonresponders. The critical question is what between-group difference (immediate-treatment slope vs. waitlist slope) can be expected. The proposal hypothesizes "a near-zero waitlist slope and a positive immediate-treatment slope," but the magnitude of the positive slope, net of maturation and practice effects in the control group, is uncertain. If the net treatment effect is small (d < 0.5), the study will be underpowered to detect it. This does not invalidate the design—the proposal explicitly plans for this possibility—but it does mean Aim 1's group-level hypothesis may not be resolved.

**Contamination of the control condition.** The deferred group receives the same baseline map optimization as the immediate group. If baseline optimization itself produces improvement (as it well might, given that audibility is being verified and optimized to 20–25 dB HL), the waitlist slope may not be near zero, further compressing the treatment contrast. The proposal acknowledges this possibility implicitly through the hypothesized near-zero slope, but does not discuss how a non-zero waitlist slope would affect power.

**The intervention itself.** The IGCIP procedure uses a "minimum-error neural-network procedure" to select electrodes for deactivation while preserving "more than eight" active electrodes. The details of this procedure—the neural network architecture, training data, error metric, and validation—are not described in the proposal. This is a significant gap. The deactivation decision is the experimental manipulation; without specification, the intervention is not fully reproducible. The proposal also states that if the IGCIP plan is identical to the clinical map, the participant will be withdrawn. The frequency of this occurrence in the preliminary data is not stated, and its implications for the analysis (differential attrition by group, since only the immediate and deferred-at-12-months groups generate IGCIP plans) are not discussed.

**Blinding integrity.** The PIs and statistician have access to allocation but "will not administer or score assessments." This is a standard arrangement, but the proposal does not describe formal assessment of blinding integrity—asking participants, parents, or assessors to guess group assignment. Given that IGCIP may produce perceptible changes in sound quality, blinding of participants and their families may be imperfect. This is common in device intervention trials and not disqualifying, but it should be acknowledged and measured.

**Multiple comparisons.** The proposal controls type-I error at α = .05 but does not describe a strategy for the many outcome measures across three aims. Bootstrapped confidence intervals are mentioned for path coefficients, but the plan for the primary treatment-contrast outcomes is not specified in terms of correction for multiplicity.

**Missing data.** The statement that "we expect most missing assessments to be nonrandom and do not plan imputation for these assessments" is a frank but analytically consequential admission. Nonrandom missingness cannot be addressed by complete-case analysis or available-item mean imputation without risking bias. The proposal's reliance on bootstrapping for incomplete data is sensible for confidence intervals but does not address the underlying bias from informative missingness. A sensitivity analysis plan (e.g., pattern-mixture models or selection models) would strengthen this section.

**External validity.** The exclusion criteria (nonverbal IQ below 85; no autism, neurological disorder, or major temporal-bone abnormalities) produce a sample that is relatively high-functioning and anatomically uncomplicated. This is appropriate for an efficacy study but limits generalizability to the broader pediatric CI population, where comorbidities are common.

## Feasibility: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

### Strengths

Recruitment feasibility is well-supported. Vanderbilt has a large pediatric CI program, and the team's track record—41 children studied in preliminary work, 47 in the published DeFreese et al. (2024) cohort—demonstrates access to the target population. The staggered cohort design spreads recruitment across years 1–3, with expected enrollment of approximately 72 children total (12–14 per summer, 5–10 per winter). The age range (6–12 years) and implantation-before-age-three criterion define a pool that is large enough at a major center.

The imaging requirements are feasible. Preoperative CT is standard of care for CI surgery, and postoperative CT can be obtained through existing clinical scans or the linked low-dose protocol. The IGCIP processing pipeline (atlas registration, electrode localization, distance-versus-frequency analysis) has been operational in the team's laboratory for years and has produced published results.

The assessment schedule is demanding but realistic. Seven in-person visits over 24 months, with home word checks at months 1 and 13, represent a substantial but not prohibitive burden. The schedule concentrates auditory and speech measures at multiple time points while reserving the full cognitive-language-literacy battery for baseline, 12, and 24 months, which is sensible.

Team expertise is a strength. Gifford is a leading investigator in pediatric CI auditory perception; Camarata brings deep expertise in child language and literacy. The inclusion of otology, image analysis/engineering, biostatistics, and consultants in phonological processing and speech production covers the necessary disciplines. The project manager and monthly team meetings provide coordination infrastructure.

### Potential concerns

The five-year timeline is tight for completing recruitment, 24-month follow-up for all cohorts, unblinding, and integrated analysis. The year-3 cohort (recruited in years 3–4) will reach their 24-month visit in year 5, leaving minimal time for analysis before the grant ends. The proposal acknowledges this by mentioning a possible no-cost extension for dissemination.

The requirement for postoperative CT may deter some families, particularly those whose children were implanted elsewhere and lack existing scans. The linked low-dose protocol mitigates but does not eliminate this concern. The proposal does not estimate what proportion of otherwise-eligible children will lack or decline postoperative imaging.

Participant retention over 24 months in a pediatric population, with seven in-person visits, is a known challenge. The preliminary work followed 23 children for one year (with 16 retaining the IGCIP map), which is encouraging but not directly comparable to a two-year randomized trial with a deferred-treatment arm. Families in the deferred group must attend all visits for 12 months before receiving the intervention they may have hoped for, which could affect retention differentially across arms. The proposal does not discuss retention targets, differential attrition monitoring, or strategies beyond scheduling around school breaks.

## Ethics and Safety: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

The ethical framework is appropriate and well-considered. Parental consent and child assent under institutional procedures are standard. The waitlist design provides all participants access to the intervention within 12 months, which is a reasonable accommodation given clinical equipoise.

The principal risks are correctly identified: reduced speech recognition from the experimental map and radiation exposure from postoperative CT. Several features of the protocol address these risks:

- **Baseline map optimization** ensures that any decline can be attributed to the experimental map rather than pre-existing clinical underfitting.
- **Home word-recognition monitoring** at one month post-intervention, with a decline exceeding the 95% test-retest interval triggering the option to restore the prior map or continue for one additional month. This provides a safety net with reasonable latency.
- **Existing clinical scans** are used when available, reducing additional radiation exposure.
- **Low-dose protocol** for children requiring new postoperative imaging.
- **The option to restore the clinical map** if performance declines, and the acknowledgment that withdrawal may require unmasking.

The proposal notes that independent monitoring will be organized if the IRB requires it. For a trial with a non-trivial risk of reduced speech recognition in children during a sensitive developmental period, a data and safety monitoring board (DSMB) would be appropriate regardless of IRB discretion, and its absence from the explicit plan is a minor concern.

Data management plans (REDCap, coded data, separate identity master list, secured records, six-year retention, de-identified sharing) are standard and adequate. Sharing of de-identified data, methods, and raw data in repositories supports reproducibility.

The provision of assessment reports to families is a good practice that respects participants' contribution.

## Management: B (Good) — Confidence: C (Moderate)

The management plan is coherent at the level of detail provided. The division of responsibility (Gifford: auditory perception and recruitment; Camarata: speech, language, and literacy) is logical and leverages complementary expertise. The project manager role and monthly team meetings are appropriate coordination mechanisms. The five-year schedule table provides a year-by-year roadmap that aligns recruitment, follow-up, and analysis.

Key gaps in the evidence for management assessment:

- **No CVs, itemized budget, or institutional letters of support** are included in the supplied text. As noted in the review instructions, this absence alone is not evidence of incapacity, but it limits confidence in several judgements: whether the PhD student and project manager can be recruited with the available timeline and salary; whether the assessment battery can be administered within visit durations given staff availability; and whether the image-processing pipeline has dedicated engineering support beyond the named engineering consultant.
- **The PhD student** appears in the Year 1 hiring plan but is not further described. Their role in data collection, analysis, or both is unspecified.
- **Consultant roles** are mentioned (phonological processing, speech production) without specifying time commitment, meeting frequency, or formal deliverables.
- **The statistician** is mentioned as generating the allocation schedule and having access to it, but their ongoing role in blinded analyses and final unblinded analyses is not detailed. The sophisticated analytic plan (cross-lagged models, path analyses, bootstrapping, multivariate approaches) requires sustained statistical collaboration.
- **No trial registration number** is provided; the proposal states the trial will be registered in Year 1, which is appropriate for a prospective plan.

The monthly meeting schedule is a strength. The staggered-cohort design introduces complexity in tracking which participants are due for which visits, which the project manager should manage, but the proposal does not describe tracking systems beyond REDCap.

## Recommendation: Conditional Support

**Rationale.** The project addresses a well-motivated question at the intersection of auditory neuroscience and child development, using a design that can yield valuable evidence under multiple outcome scenarios. The pathway-analytic framework ensures that the study contributes descriptive and mechanistic knowledge even if the treatment effect is smaller or more variable than hypothesized. The team has the relevant expertise and access to the target population.

The conditions that define the supportable scope are:

1. **The intervention specification should be completed before enrollment begins.** The minimum-error neural-network deactivation procedure needs a published or otherwise documented specification—architecture, training data, validation performance—so that the experimental manipulation is reproducible and interpretable. Without this, the study's internal validity is undermined regardless of other design strengths.

2. **The primary treatment-contrast analysis should be supplemented with a formal equivalence or non-inferiority framing for the auditory outcomes where effect sizes may fall below the powered detectable difference.** This does not require a larger sample; it requires an analytic plan that explicitly interprets a null group difference in the context of the confidence interval around the treatment effect, distinguishing "no evidence of effect" from "evidence of no meaningful effect."

3. **The missing-data plan should include sensitivity analyses for informative missingness.** Given the expected nonrandom pattern, complete-case or available-item-mean approaches alone are insufficient. Pattern-mixture models or similar sensitivity analyses should be pre-specified.

4. **A data and safety monitoring plan should be formalized** regardless of IRB discretion, given the pediatric population and the non-trivial risk of reduced speech recognition.

These conditions are specific and addressable within the current design. They do not require redesigning the study, increasing the sample, or changing the aims. If satisfied, the project has a clear path to producing informative evidence on IGCIP's auditory effects and the developmental pathways linking auditory input to language and literacy in children with cochlear implants.

# Review Report: Clinical Integration of Whole Genome Sequencing: A Policy Analysis

**PI:** David Kaufman and Amy McGuire
**Mechanism:** NIH/NHGRI R01
**Proposal source ID:** src-f0052b2a0985
**Review date:** 2026-10-28

---

## Overall Assessment

This proposal addresses a question of genuine and growing importance: how to develop a coherent policy framework for the clinical integration of whole genome sequencing (WGS) at a time when the technology, business models, and regulatory environment were all in flux. The project combines a systematic industry landscape analysis, qualitative interviews, a structured policy Delphi with a multi-stakeholder panel, and legal/policy analysis to produce a prioritized set of policy questions and alternative approaches to three selected issues.

The proposal's design is well-conceived for its intended contribution—a structured, evidence-informed, and stakeholder-grounded account of the policy terrain—rather than a definitive resolution or a causal demonstration. The investigators bring substantial relevant experience and have assembled a multi-institutional team with complementary expertise across the three policy domains (analytical validity/testing quality; patents and licensing; reimbursement and payment). The plan for dissemination, including Capitol Hill briefings and engagement with federal agencies, is appropriately ambitious for work of this kind.

Two significant limitations temper the overall assessment. First, the proposal selects three specific policy domains for deep analysis before the Delphi panel has ranked priorities, creating a tension between the stated commitment to letting stakeholders identify the most important questions and the pre-commitment to analytical validity, patents, and reimbursement as the domains of focus. Second, the Delphi design, while methodologically sound, cannot by itself resolve the tension between descriptive mapping and prescriptive policy recommendations—the proposal acknowledges this but does not fully address how the findings will be translated into actionable guidance.

The overall contribution—a systematic, independent mapping of the WGS policy landscape at a critical moment of clinical adoption—is a worthwhile and feasible increment. The proposal is **conditionally supportable** given these strengths, subject to clarification of how the tension between pre-selected domains and stakeholder-driven prioritization will be managed.

## Scientific Value: B (Good), Confidence: B (High)

### Importance of the question

The project addresses a question of clear importance. As of the proposal's framing (~2011-2012), WGS was transitioning from research into clinical care, and the policy infrastructure had been built around individual genetic tests rather than genome-scale sequencing. The proposal correctly identifies that "a policy framework built around individual genetic tests may not adequately accommodate these arrangements." The three domains selected—analytical validity, intellectual property, and reimbursement—are defensible as priority areas where policy uncertainty could impede or distort clinical adoption.

The attainable knowledge falls into two categories. First, the landscape analysis and Delphi prioritization would produce a systematic, independently generated description of the WGS industry and a ranked set of policy questions that reflect diverse stakeholder perspectives. This descriptive contribution has independent value as a baseline for subsequent policy work and for monitoring change over time. Second, the legal and policy analysis would clarify the existing regulatory architecture and generate a range of policy alternatives with documented stakeholder positions, areas of agreement, and reasons for disagreement. This is a legitimate and useful contribution at what the proposal appropriately characterizes as an early stage of policy development.

The proposal explicitly sets its ambitions at the level of clarifying options and documenting stakeholder positions rather than achieving consensus or producing definitive recommendations. This is a proportionate goal for the methods and resources described.

### What the proposal does not claim

The proposal does not claim to resolve the policy questions, to produce binding recommendations, or to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of WGS. It does not claim the Delphi panel is a representative sample. It acknowledges that issues such as privacy, workforce, and equitable access are important but outside the detailed scope. These are appropriate boundary-setting statements that strengthen rather than weaken the proposal.

### External context

By the review date of 2026-10-28, the published outcomes of this project are available. Kaufman, Curnutte, and McGuire (2014, *Journal of Law, Medicine & Ethics*) published "Clinical integration of next generation sequencing: a policy analysis," and Messner et al. (2017, *Personalized Medicine*) published "Barriers to clinical adoption of next-generation sequencing: a policy Delphi panel's solutions." The 2017 paper describes a four-round modified policy Delphi with 48 experts that identified payer variation in evidence standards, proprietary databases, FDA's role in risk mitigation, and reporting of genomic test results as key challenges—findings that closely track the proposal's planned work. These are outcome publications from the funded project (grant R01HG006460), not independent prior work, and are therefore excluded from the merit judgement per the review instructions.

## Innovation: B (Good), Confidence: B (High)

### What the project adds beyond prior work

At the time of the proposal, several groups had published on specific policy dimensions of genomic medicine. Cook-Deegan and Heaney (2010, *Annual Review of Genomics and Human Genetics*) had examined patents in genomics. McGuire, Evans, Caulfield, and Burke (2010, *Science*) had analyzed regulation of direct-to-consumer genetic testing. Hogarth, Javitt, and Melzer (2008, *Annual Review of Genomics and Human Genetics*) had surveyed the DTC landscape. The NIH-CDC multidisciplinary workshop (Khoury et al., 2009, *Genetics in Medicine*) had produced recommendations on the scientific foundation for personal genomics. Turoff (1970) had articulated the policy Delphi method and it had been applied in healthcare settings (Boulkedid et al., 2011; Campbell et al., 1999).

The principal novelty of this proposal lies in its integration of three elements that had not been combined: (1) a systematic, empirically grounded industry landscape spanning the entire WGS supply chain (equipment, sequencing services, interpretation), (2) a structured policy Delphi that simultaneously elicits stakeholder priorities and generates policy alternatives, and (3) coordinated legal and policy analysis across three domains. This integrative design is well-suited to a problem where, as the proposal states, "empirical evidence is limited and the relevant actors hold different knowledge, responsibilities, and interests."

The proposal's use of a policy Delphi—as distinct from a consensus Delphi—is methodologically appropriate and represents a considered application of the Turoff (1970) framework. The emphasis on surfacing disagreement rather than forcing consensus, and the use of anonymous rounds to reduce the influence of dominant voices, are well-justified design choices.

### Limitations to the novelty claim

The landscape analysis approach is explicitly modeled on the GPPC's prior work on genetic testing oversight and direct-to-consumer testing. This is not a criticism—it is appropriate methodological continuity—but it means the landscape component is an extension of an existing approach to a new domain rather than a methodological innovation. The novelty resides primarily in the integration and the application to the WGS context.

## Rigor: C (Moderate), Confidence: B (High)

### Strengths of the design

The multi-method design is appropriate to the research questions. The landscape analysis uses triangulation among internet/PubMed searches, media databases, patent databases, company websites, and direct company contact—a reasonable combination for characterizing an emerging industry where no single source is comprehensive. The interview component (up to 20 semi-structured interviews, purposive sampling, thematic saturation as the stopping rule) follows established qualitative research practice and is well-sized for the goal of capturing nonproprietary industry perspectives on clinical integration pathways.

The Delphi design is methodologically grounded in the relevant literature. The proposal cites Turoff (1970) for the policy Delphi framework, Boulkedid et al. (2011) for Delphi methods in healthcare quality indicators, Campbell et al. (1999) on panel membership and feedback effects, and Okoli and Pawlowski (2004) on design considerations. The five-survey structure with iterative feedback, the use of composite scores (Priority = importance + validity, Tractability, Overall), and the two-day meeting to explore complexity beyond rankings are consistent with established practice.

The legal and policy analysis framework is specified with reasonable clarity: examine relevant laws, regulations, guidelines, and standards; consider purpose, scope, precision, enforcement, and conflicts. Reference to the Patton and Sawicki policy-analysis framework provides a recognized methodological anchor.

### Material limitations

**Pre-commitment to three policy domains.** The most significant design tension is that the proposal pre-selects analytical validity/testing quality, patents/licensing, and reimbursement/payment as the three domains for detailed policy analysis, while simultaneously stating that the Delphi panel will identify and prioritize policy questions. If the Delphi panel identifies a question outside these three domains as among the most important and tractable, the design provides no mechanism to redirect analytical resources. The proposal states that "important questions outside those domains will remain visible in the published priorities rather than being treated as answered by this study," which is a reasonable acknowledgment but does not resolve the tension. This pre-commitment limits the extent to which the priority-setting exercise can genuinely shape the analytical focus.

**Selection of the three issues.** The selection of the one "most important tractable issue within each of the three domains" is described as a consultation between the investigators and the advisory board, informed by the Delphi rankings. This is a judgement-intensive step with limited procedural specification. The criteria for "most important tractable" are operationalized through the composite scores, but the final selection is not itself governed by a defined decision rule.

**Interview sample.** The proposal states interviews will continue "until thematic saturation is reached or 20 interviews have been completed." At 30-40 minutes per interview, with up to 20 interviews, the total interview data is approximately 10-13 hours, which is modest but potentially adequate for the stated purpose of capturing nonproprietary views. The purposive sampling from "at least 40 eligible organizations" provides reasonable coverage, though the proposal does not specify criteria for purposive selection beyond organizational type.

**Panel composition and representativeness.** The proposal appropriately states that the panel "does not treat it as a statistically representative population sample." The recruitment strategy (ranking candidates by expertise and approaching in order) is reasonable. However, the proposal does not address how potential conflicts of interest among panel members will be identified, managed, or reported—a relevant consideration when many participants have commercial or institutional stakes in the policy outcomes.

**Composite score construction.** The Priority composite combines importance and validity (range 0-8); Tractability has a stated range of 0-8; and Overall sums all four items (range 0-16). The proposal does not explain the justification for equal weighting in these composites, nor how the Tractability composite (composed of technical and political feasibility ratings, which may diverge) will be handled analytically.

**Analysis of survey data.** The proposal describes median values for individual and combined scales and sorting by composite scores, but does not specify how open-ended comments, additions, and modifications from the Delphi rounds will be systematically analyzed. The qualitative analysis plan for the Delphi survey data is less developed than for the interview data.

### Appropriateness to stage

The design is appropriate for the stated stage—a policy analysis aimed at clarifying the landscape, priorities, options, and stakeholder positions—and does not claim to produce causal evidence or population-level estimates.

## Feasibility: B (Good), Confidence: B (High)

### What can be carried out with the stated methods and resources

The landscape analysis relies on publicly available sources and targeted company contact. The initial list of 34 organizations (Table 1) demonstrates that the team has already identified a substantial set of entities. The search strategy is specified. The monitoring of new entrants and changes throughout the study is a realistic recognition of the dynamic industry context.

The Delphi process is logistically demanding—five surveys, a two-day meeting, analysis and feedback between rounds—but the investigators and collaborating team (Messner at CMTP specifically) have relevant experience implementing Delphi processes. The 20-month timeline for the Delphi component is realistic for five survey rounds with intervening analysis. The budget for the two-day Washington meeting and the involvement of Burness Communications for stakeholder engagement and events are practical provisions.

The multi-institutional team (Johns Hopkins GPPC, Baylor, Duke, CMTP) covers the needed expertise: genetics policy (Kaufman, McGuire), intellectual property and patent analysis (Cook-Deegan, Duke), reimbursement and coverage (Tunis, Deverka, CMTP), Delphi methods (Messner), and legal analysis (multiple team members). The division of leadership responsibilities is clear: Kaufman oversees the Delphi and dissemination, McGuire oversees policy approaches, with weekly communication and regular project meetings.

The three-year timeline with phased work (landscape in year 1, prioritization and initial policy analysis in year 2, final surveys, meeting, and dissemination in year 3) is broadly credible.

### Critical dependencies

**Panel recruitment and retention.** The 20-month commitment for approximately 40 panel members is substantial. Attrition across five surveys and the final meeting is a recognized risk in Delphi studies; the proposal does not specify an attrition management strategy or minimum panel size for analysis.

**Industry interview access.** Access to senior executives at 20 organizations requires cooperation from commercial entities. The investigators' prior experience and the GPPC's reputation provide some basis for confidence, but this remains a dependency. The proposal notes that interviews will address "nonproprietary views," which may limit the depth of information about business models and strategies.

**Advisory board engagement.** The eight-member advisory board is expected to meet five times by video conference over three years and to advise on study goals, landscape, issue selection, policy approaches, and dissemination. The quality of this advice depends on sustained engagement; the proposal does not describe any honorarium or other incentive for board participation.

**Regulatory and market change.** The proposal acknowledges that "WGS technologies, business models, regulations, and litigation can change during the study" and commits to monitoring and reassessing strategy. This is appropriate risk management, but the extent to which mid-study changes in, for example, FDA regulatory posture or major patent litigation could affect the relevance of the Delphi panel's work is difficult to mitigate fully.

## Ethics and Safety: B (Good), Confidence: B (High)

### Human participants and data protection

The human subjects components—industry interviews and the Delphi panel—present relatively low risk. The proposal identifies the relevant risks candidly: the "small possibility that commercially or legally sensitive information could be disclosed, including information potentially subject to subpoena." The plan to request a Certificate of Confidentiality and to inform participants about it and its limitations is appropriate.

Data protection measures are specified: identifying information held separately from recordings and transcripts; encrypted, password-protected survey access; de-identified transcripts; aggregate reporting of comments; destruction of meeting recordings after transcription; and a closed summit (no press, no outside participants). These measures are proportionate to the risk level.

The consent process (verbal consent after advance provision and discussion of study information) is appropriate for the interview and summit components. The two-letter code system for survey responses is a practical approach to maintaining confidentiality while enabling longitudinal tracking of responses across rounds.

The proposal acknowledges that "the demographic composition of the professional populations is not sufficiently defined to specify demographic enrollment targets" and states that recruitment will not exclude on the basis of sex, race, or ethnicity. This is a reasonable position given that the sampling frame is defined by professional expertise rather than population demographics, though the proposal could have committed to reporting the demographic composition of the recruited panel as a transparency measure.

### Dissemination and public benefit

The public products (landscape, priorities, policy alternatives, methodological findings) and the commitment to accessible formats, webcasts, and policy briefings represent appropriate attention to the broader impact of the work, consistent with NHGRI's ELSI research program's expectations.

### Unresolved ethics questions

The proposal does not explicitly address the management of conflicts of interest among Delphi panel members. While not a research ethics violation, this is a methodological consideration with ethical dimensions, particularly given that panel members may have commercial, institutional, or professional interests in the policy outcomes being deliberated. The proposal could usefully have described how such interests would be disclosed and whether they would affect the analysis.

## Management: B (Good), Confidence: C (Moderate)

### Strengths

The leadership structure is clear and the division of responsibilities is sensible. Kaufman and McGuire as co-PIs with complementary oversight (Delphi/dissemination vs. policy approaches) and shared responsibility for the landscape provides appropriate coverage. The weekly communication commitment and regular cross-institutional project meetings are practical coordination mechanisms.

The multi-institutional team brings relevant expertise, and the track record described in preliminary work (GPPC's landscape analyses, McGuire's regulatory work, Cook-Deegan's IP work, Tunis and Deverka's reimbursement work, Messner's Delphi experience) provides reasonable evidence of capacity. The prior convening of a 60-participant national summit supports the feasibility of stakeholder engagement.

The advisory board composition (clinical genetics, sequencing technology, regulation, economics, IP, consumer protection) is well-matched to the project's needs. The five-meeting schedule is appropriately spaced across the project timeline.

The engagement of Burness Communications for stakeholder identification, communication strategy, events, and archiving is a practical provision that addresses a common weakness in policy-oriented research projects.

### Limitations and unspecified arrangements

The proposal provides no itemized budget, CVs, or letters of support. Per the review instructions, these omissions alone are not evidence of incapacity, but they limit the evidence available for assessing management capacity. The confidence rating of C reflects this limited evidence rather than a judgement of weakness.

The proposal does not specify how the team will manage disagreement among investigators across institutions, particularly when legal and policy analyses produce divergent interpretations. The coordination mechanism (weekly calls, regular meetings) is described at a general level.

The schedule (section 6) is presented at the phase level rather than as a detailed timeline with milestones. The sequencing of activities is clear, but the proposal does not identify critical-path dependencies or contingency plans for delays in panel recruitment, survey administration, or analysis.

No data management plan is described for the landscape database, interview transcripts, or survey data beyond the confidentiality provisions in section 7.

## Overall Recommendation: **Conditional Support** (`conditional_support`)

The project addresses a question of genuine importance with a well-conceived multi-method design and a team with relevant expertise. The contributions—a systematic WGS industry landscape, a stakeholder-prioritized set of policy questions, and alternative policy approaches with documented areas of agreement and disagreement—are worthwhile and proportionate to the methods and resources described.

Support is conditional on the investigators addressing the following premise:

**Resolution of the tension between pre-selected domains and stakeholder-driven prioritization.** The proposal pre-commits to analytical validity/testing quality, patents/licensing, and reimbursement/payment as the three domains for detailed policy analysis, while simultaneously stating that the Delphi panel will identify and rank the most important policy questions. The investigators should clarify how they will proceed if the panel's highest-ranked priorities include issues outside these three domains—for example, whether the pre-selected domains would be adjusted, whether additional resources could be brought to bear, or whether the published priorities would simply acknowledge the gap with a commitment to future work. This affects the integrity of the priority-setting exercise and the credibility of the final policy recommendations.

This condition does not require redesigning the study; it requires a reasoned account of how the investigators will handle the foreseeable contingency that stakeholder priorities diverge from the pre-selected analytical focus.

The strengths of the proposal—the integrative design, the team's expertise, the methodological grounding of the Delphi, the practical dissemination plan, and the proportionate ambition—would support a well-executed policy analysis that advances understanding of WGS clinical integration at a critical juncture.

# Scientific Project Review

**Proposal:** Multi-center Evaluation of Digital Breast Tomosynthesis with Synthesized Two-dimensional Mammography for Breast Cancer Screening  
**Principal Investigator:** Brian L. Sprague  
**Application Mechanism:** R03  
**Review Date:** 2026-10-28  
**Reviewer's Role:** Independent scientific evaluation of the proposal as a prospective plan

---

## Overall Assessment

This proposal addresses a clinically important and timely question: whether synthesized two-dimensional mammography combined with digital breast tomosynthesis (s2D/DBT) performs comparably to conventional digital mammography plus tomosynthesis (DM/DBT) in population breast cancer screening. The question matters because replacing a separate DM acquisition with s2D images reconstructed from DBT data reduces radiation dose by approximately 39–45% and streamlines workflow, yet the evidence base on screening performance, lesion-specific detection, and effects on breast density classification remains limited. The proposal exploits a natural experiment created when seven radiology facilities uniformly switched from DM/DBT to s2D/DBT, using prospectively collected data from established PROSPR research registries with registry-linked cancer outcomes. The before-after, same-facility design is a pragmatic strength for rapid evaluation.

The project is well-motivated, addresses a genuine evidence gap, and benefits from a strong existing data infrastructure. The investigative team has demonstrated expertise in this exact domain, including publication of the single-site preliminary data that motivates the multicenter extension. The three aims are coherent and logically connected.

However, the proposal has a fundamental limitation that constrains the strength of inference it can deliver: the before-after observational design cannot convincingly separate the effect of the imaging modality from secular trends in screening populations, referral patterns, radiologist experience, or other concurrent changes. While the proposal acknowledges this and deploys reasonable protections (same facilities, same radiologists, uniform adoption, covariate adjustment), residual confounding remains an irreducible concern for an R03-level observational study. The detailed power calculations focus on detectable absolute differences for pairwise modality comparisons but do not fully address the precision loss from clustering adjustments or the interpretive challenge of small absolute differences in the context of uncontrolled confounding.

On balance, the study is likely to yield useful descriptive evidence on s2D/DBT performance in US practice that can inform implementation decisions and guide the design of more definitive evaluations. The contribution is a well-justified increment rather than a breakthrough. The design is fit for the descriptive and comparative purposes stated but cannot deliver the causal certainty that a randomized comparison would provide—a limitation the proposal itself does not fully confront. The project is feasible given existing data, infrastructure, and expertise. Provided the ethical arrangements are confirmed as current, the plan is proportionate and supportable.

## Scientific Value: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

### Strengths

The proposal addresses a question of direct clinical and policy relevance. Screening mammography is a high-volume public health activity, and any change in imaging protocol affects millions of women. The comparison between s2D/DBT and DM/DBT is the correct one for facilities already using DBT, which is now the dominant screening modality in the United States. The radiation dose reduction achievable with s2D—estimated at approximately 39–45% compared with DM/DBT—represents a material benefit if performance is maintained.

The knowledge to be gained spans three connected domains. First, clinical performance measures (recall, cancer detection, sensitivity, specificity, false-negative rate, PPV1) provide the core metrics that screening programs, payers, and guideline developers use to assess screening modalities. Second, lesion-specific detection (invasive versus DCIS, benign subtypes) addresses the critical question of whether s2D alters the balance of benefit (invasive cancer detection) and potential harm (DCIS overdetection, benign biopsies). Third, density classification addresses a downstream consequence with regulatory implications: several US states mandate density notification, and perceived density influences supplemental imaging recommendations. If s2D systematically shifts density classifications without biological basis, women could receive different risk assessments and recommendations solely because of the imaging technology.

The multicenter scope, combining Vermont's predominantly white rural population with Pennsylvania's urban, racially diverse population, strengthens generalizability beyond single-institution studies. The inclusion of both academic and community hospital settings is a pragmatic strength that reflects real-world screening delivery.

The proposed study would contribute the largest US multicenter evaluation of s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT with 12-month cancer outcome ascertainment through registry linkage—a design feature that distinguishes it from reader studies and from clinical series without complete follow-up.

### Limitations

The descriptive contribution, while useful, does not resolve the central inferential challenge: whether observed differences between periods are attributable to the modality change rather than to temporal trends. The scientific value is accordingly tempered—the study can characterize performance differences but cannot attribute them with high confidence. This is not a fatal weakness for an observational study, but it limits the decisiveness of the evidence.

The proposal's stated goal of providing "evidence relevant to patients choosing screening, providers considering the new modality, payers, and screening policy" is slightly ambitious for the design. The evidence will be relevant but not definitive; it will narrow uncertainty rather than resolve it. This is nonetheless a legitimate and valuable scientific contribution at the R03 level.

## Innovation: C (Moderate) — Confidence: B (High)

### What the project adds beyond prior work

The proposal correctly identifies that evidence on s2D/DBT screening performance is limited. The existing literature includes the Zuckerman et al. (2016, *Radiology*) single-site Pennsylvania report (the preliminary data for this proposal), reader studies such as the TOMMY trial (Gilbert et al., 2015, *Radiology*), and subsequent observational studies from Norway (Hofvind et al., 2018, *Radiology*) and Italy (Bernardi et al., 2019, *The Breast*). A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis by Hamad et al. (*Int J Cancer*, 2025) concluded that DBT plus S2D performs comparably to DBT plus FFDM with no significant difference in cancer detection rates and a small but significant improvement in specificity.

The present proposal's distinctive contributions would be: (a) a multicenter US evaluation with standardized PROSPR data elements and registry-linked 12-month outcomes, enabling sensitivity and false-negative rate estimation; (b) lesion-specific analyses including benign biopsy subtypes; (c) systematic density classification analysis; and (d) inclusion of both academic and community settings with diverse populations. The before-after, same-facility design, while not novel in principle, is a pragmatic strength that avoids the site-selection confounding that can affect cross-sectional comparisons.

### Limitations on novelty

Several of the individual components have precedents. The Zuckerman et al. (2016) single-site analysis already reported recall, cancer detection, and dose comparisons between s2D/DBT and DM/DBT from Pennsylvania. The Hofvind et al. (2018) Norwegian study compared DBT plus synthetic mammography to DM alone in a population-based program, and the Bernardi et al. (2019) Trento study reported interval cancer rates for DBT with synthesized 2D. The MAITA consortium (Giorgi Rossi et al., 2024, *Eur J Cancer*) reported randomized trial results for DBT plus synthetic-2D versus DM.

The main incremental novelty of the present proposal is the focused comparison of s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT (rather than versus DM alone) with complete 12-month outcome ascertainment across multiple US sites. This is a meaningful but modest extension beyond the Zuckerman et al. (2016) single-site work and the international studies that have since appeared. The innovation lies more in the combination of features—multicenter, complete outcome follow-up, lesion-specific detail, and density assessment—than in any single methodological or conceptual advance.

## Rigor: C (Moderate) — Confidence: B (High)

### Design strengths

The proposal adopts standard BI-RADS fifth-edition performance definitions, which aligns with US radiology practice and facilitates comparability with other studies. The use of generalized estimating equations (GEE) with the Miglioretti–Heagerty approach for non-nested clustering within women, radiologists, and facilities is methodologically appropriate and reflects the team's established expertise. The planned covariate adjustment (age, race/ethnicity, breast density, family history, screening interval) and interaction testing are sensible.

Twelve-month cancer outcome ascertainment through linkage to state cancer registries, combined with pathology data, is a design strength that enables estimation of sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rates—measures unavailable in studies lacking complete follow-up. The exclusion of women with personal breast cancer history and of transitional examinations is appropriate for maintaining cohort definition.

The uniform use of Hologic Selenia Dimensions equipment and C-View reconstruction software across all sites controls an important source of technical variability. The same-radiologist, same-facility design controls for interpreter-level and facility-level confounders that would otherwise be difficult to adjust.

### Design limitations

The central limitation is the before-after observational design. The proposal states that its "main protections against confounding are a common set of facilities and radiologists, expected stability of the served populations, uniform adoption rather than patient-specific selection, and exam-level covariates for statistical adjustment." These are reasonable protections, but they are not sufficient to exclude confounding by temporal trends. Screening populations can change over a 6-year period (2011–2017) in ways that affect performance metrics: changes in screening intervals, risk profiles of women attending screening, referral thresholds, biopsy practices, and awareness of breast density could all shift between the DM/DBT and s2D/DBT periods. The proposal states an "expected stability of the served populations" but does not present evidence of that stability or a plan to test it formally.

The power calculations warrant scrutiny. The projected 80% power to detect absolute differences of 0.4 percentage points in recall and 0.8 cancers per 1,000 examinations in cancer detection appears adequate for the main comparisons. However, the detectable difference in sensitivity of 5.6 percentage points (from a baseline of 90.9%) is relatively large and may miss clinically relevant differences. The detectable difference for DCIS (0.5 per 1,000) is larger than the baseline DCIS detection rate (1.7 per 1,000), meaning the study can only detect a roughly 30% relative reduction—a substantial effect. The proposal acknowledges that "correlation adjustment could change GEE variances" but expects limited inflation. This assumption may be optimistic; the proposal does not present design effects or quantify the expected variance inflation from clustering.

For Aim 2, the proposal lists detectable differences for invasive cancer and benign lesions in absolute terms (0.8 and 1.5 per 1,000 respectively), which represent approximately 19% and 11% of baseline rates—reasonable effect sizes to detect. But the exploratory subgroup analyses by ER/PR/HER2 status will almost certainly be underpowered for formal comparison and are appropriately described as exploratory.

The proposal uses chi-square tests for Aim 3 (density distributions) but then logistic regression for adjusted analyses. Chi-square tests do not account for clustering; the proposal should clarify whether the primary inference for Aim 3 will be based on GEE logistic regression or another method that handles correlated data. The detectable difference in dense classifications of 0.6 percentage points (from 34.0% baseline) is small relative to normal variability in density assessment, and while statistically detectable, its clinical significance may be limited.

A material omission is the absence of a plan for assessing or adjusting for secular trends. Options such as interrupted time-series analysis, inclusion of a concurrent DM-only comparison group, or sensitivity analyses examining trends in outcomes over time within each period are not discussed. The proposal also does not address whether the transition dates differed across the seven facilities—an important consideration because staggered adoption provides some temporal control that simultaneous adoption does not.

## Feasibility: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

### Strengths

The project's feasibility rests on strong foundations. Data have already been prospectively collected through the PROSPR research centers, with standardized common data elements, established linkage infrastructure, and prior collaborative experience between the Vermont and Pennsylvania sites. The proposal is not contingent on new data collection, equipment acquisition, or prospective recruitment. The planned dataset of approximately 185,700 examinations from 2011–2017 is already in hand, subject to assembly and quality control.

The team combines radiology, pathology, and epidemiology expertise with a demonstrated track record in multicenter PROSPR collaborations, including the published DM/DBT performance analyses that inform the power calculations and the single-site s2D/DBT analysis that motivates the multicenter extension. The analytic methods (GEE with clustering adjustments) are well within the team's capabilities and have been previously applied to PROSPR data.

The R03 mechanism is appropriate for secondary analysis of existing data, and the scope of work—assembling exam-level records, pooling common data elements, establishing 12-month outcomes, and completing three coordinated analyses—is proportionate to the mechanism.

### Limitations

The proposal states that exam-level Pennsylvania data will be "securely transferred to Vermont for pooling" but does not specify data use agreements, institutional approvals, or a timeline for data assembly. While these are administrative rather than scientific issues, they represent critical dependencies that the proposal could address more explicitly.

The proposal does not describe a data quality or completeness audit plan—for example, what proportion of examinations have complete 12-month follow-up, what linkage rates are expected with cancer registries, or how missing data will be handled. Given that sensitivity and false-negative rate estimation depend on complete outcome ascertainment, this is a non-trivial gap.

The 2011–2017 study period means that, as of the 2026 review date, the data are approximately 9–15 years old. The proposal does not address whether Hologic C-View software versions changed during the study period, whether radiologist experience with s2D evolved (a learning curve effect), or whether the technology remained stable. These factors could affect the contemporaneous relevance of the findings but do not prevent the study from being completed as planned.

## Ethics and Safety: B (Good) — Confidence: C (Moderate)

### Strengths

The proposal addresses the key ethical dimensions. The study uses clinical imaging already obtained in routine care and does not introduce additional research radiation exposure. Vermont's registry uses passive consent with an opt-out mechanism, achieving approximately 95% participation—an approach that balances respect for autonomy with the public health value of near-complete registry data. Pennsylvania obtained a waiver of informed consent, which is a standard approach for retrospective records-based research when the research presents minimal risk and cannot practicably be conducted with consent.

The secure transfer of exam-level data from Pennsylvania to Vermont for pooled analysis is noted, and data security practices are implicitly addressed through the established PROSPR data infrastructure. The exclusion of women with personal breast cancer history avoids inclusion of a group whose imaging protocols differ systematically and whose privacy considerations may differ.

### Limitations

The proposal does not state whether the ethical approvals (passive consent mechanism, waiver of informed consent, data transfer agreements) are current as of the proposal date or would need renewal. For a review in 2026, the status of these approvals is relevant but not assessable from the proposal text alone. The proposal also does not address data retention, destruction, or sharing policies. These are administrative details that do not suggest a material ethical concern but limit confidence in the judgement.

The observational design raises no additional ethical concerns beyond the standard considerations for secondary analysis of clinical records. There is no direct participant contact, no experimental intervention, and no additional radiation exposure.

## Management: C (Moderate) — Confidence: C (Moderate)

### Strengths

The proposal identifies a PI (Brian L. Sprague) with relevant expertise and an established track record in PROSPR breast screening research. The team is described as combining "radiology, pathology, epidemiology, and experience in multicenter PROSPR collaborations." The key references include work by Sprague and colleagues, confirming domain expertise.

The work plan—data assembly, pooling, outcome establishment, three coordinated analyses—is logically structured and limited in scope, consistent with the R03 mechanism. The outputs are clearly specified: characterization of screening performance, lesion patterns, and density assessment.

### Limitations

The proposal provides very limited information on project management specifics. No timeline, milestones, or division of responsibilities across team members is specified. While an R03 proposal may not require the same level of management detail as a larger mechanism, the absence of any schedule or coordination plan limits the assessment. The pooled analysis depends on successful data transfer from Pennsylvania to Vermont, but there is no contingency plan if linkage or data harmonization reveals unexpected challenges.

No information is provided on the specific qualifications of team members beyond the PI, on data management personnel, or on statistical support beyond the citation of methods the team has previously used. The proposal does not describe a plan for dissemination beyond publication, despite identifying patients, providers, payers, and policy-makers as target audiences.

These limitations primarily affect confidence in the management judgement rather than indicating a likely failure of execution. The tasks are well within the team's demonstrated capabilities, and the infrastructure is in place.

## Overall Recommendation: **Support (support)**

The proposal addresses a clinically significant question with a pragmatic multicenter design that leverages existing high-quality data infrastructure. The knowledge to be gained—comparative screening performance of s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT with complete outcome follow-up, lesion-specific detection patterns, and density classification effects—is a meaningful contribution to an evidence base that remains incomplete. The work is feasible within the R03 mechanism and proportionate to the resources requested.

The before-after observational design is the appropriate design for exploiting the natural experiment created by uniform facility-level adoption, and the proposal deploys reasonable protections against confounding. The primary limitation—inability to fully exclude secular trends as alternative explanations for observed differences—is inherent to the design and does not negate the value of the descriptive evidence the study will produce. The study can narrow uncertainty about s2D/DBT performance in US practice, inform implementation decisions, and identify questions requiring more definitive designs. That is a legitimate and valuable scientific contribution at this stage.

The proposal would be strengthened by: (a) formal analysis of temporal trends within each period, or sensitivity analyses addressing secular confounding; (b) explicit treatment of clustering effects on precision in the power calculations; (c) a data completeness and quality audit plan; and (d) specification of a timeline and team roles. These recommendations address issues that affect confidence in the findings but do not alter the fundamental judgement that the project is supportable as proposed.

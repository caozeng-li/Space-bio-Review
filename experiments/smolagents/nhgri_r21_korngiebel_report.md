# Review Report

## Overall Assessment

**Proposal:** Using Ethics and User-Centered Design to Create Templates for EHR-Mediated Return of Genetic Test Results  
**PI:** Diane M. Korngiebel, University of Washington  
**Mechanism:** NIH/NHGRI R21  
**Review date:** 2026-10-28 (prospective assessment)

This proposal addresses a practically important question: how should patient portals present genetic test results to serve patient needs? The combination of qualitative bioethics research and user-centered design (UCD) is well suited to a formative study whose contribution is descriptive evidence about patient preferences and support needs, iteratively refined electronic report prototypes, and an ethical Points to Consider framework. The team has appropriate expertise and institutional infrastructure, and the UW Medicine Epic-based eCare environment offers a realistic setting.

The project's central strength is its explicit attention to heterogeneity—of genetic results, of delivery arrangements, and of patients—rather than treating all genetic findings as equally sensitive or all patients as having uniform needs. The proposal is appropriately scoped as an R21 exploratory/developmental project: it does not claim to evaluate clinical outcomes or implementation effectiveness. Its primary contribution would be the empirical categories of concern thresholds, concrete design recommendations grounded in patient accounts, and downloadable prototypes that can inform subsequent clinical and implementation studies.

The most material limitation concerns the relationship between the proposed work and subsequent published results from the same team. An external publication by Korngiebel and West (Korngiebel DM, West KM. *JMIRx Med*. 2022;3:e29706) reports findings from 59 interviews at UW Medicine that substantially overlap with the design described in this proposal—including semistructured interviews about patient preferences for design and content of electronic genetic test result returns, conducted with patients who received pharmacogenetic, hereditary blood disorder, and heritable cancer risk results. This publication is an outcome of what appears to be the funded and completed project. As such, it does not constitute independent prior work that would diminish the proposal's novelty *at the time of submission*. However, from the review standpoint of 2026, the published results confirm that the project was feasible and yielded concrete, useful findings. The present assessment treats the proposal as prospective and the subsequent publication as outcome information, consistent with the review instructions.

## Scientific Value: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

The proposal identifies a gap that, at the time of writing, was genuine: little empirical work had collected patient preferences for electronic return of genetic results before creating templates. The question matters because the 21st Century Cures Act and related mandates accelerate electronic release, while genetic results differ from routine laboratory values in their interpretive complexity, family implications, and potential psychosocial impact. The project promises three kinds of contribution:

**Descriptive:** Systematic accounts of how patients who have actually received genetic results through a portal experienced that process, what thresholds they apply for electronic versus personal return, and what support they need. The between-result-type comparisons in Aim 2 (pharmacogenomics, hemochromatosis, colorectal cancer risk, negative results) and the genetic-versus-non-genetic comparison in Aim 1 provide useful descriptive structure.

**Methodological/conceptual:** The Points to Consider framework, refined through iterative empirical input, offers an organizing device that could travel beyond the study setting. Its three-domain structure (beneficence/nonmaleficence, autonomy, justice/equity) is standard but productively operationalized for the portal-return context.

**Practical:** Six to eight downloadable prototypes, including Epic-style and blue-sky versions, provide concrete templates that health IT professionals can adapt. This is a more actionable output than a list of general recommendations alone.

A limitation is that the project does not evaluate whether the prototypes improve understanding, reduce distress, or increase appropriate follow-up—those are explicitly reserved for subsequent studies. This is appropriate for a formative R21, but it bounds the attainable knowledge contribution. The study will tell us what patients *say* they want and can *navigate* in a think-aloud session; it will not tell us how the prototypes perform under the emotional conditions of actual result receipt. The proposal acknowledges this scope limitation clearly.

## Innovation: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

At the time the proposal was drafted, the approach of combining qualitative patient interviews with UCD prototype development specifically for EHR-mediated genetic result return was novel in several respects:

1. **Patient-defined thresholds.** Korngiebel's prior clinician study (Korngiebel et al., *J Genet Couns*. 2018;27:349–357) found that clinicians disagreed about which results are "sensitive." The proposal correctly identifies this as warranting patient-centered investigation rather than importing a settled clinician taxonomy. This is a genuine conceptual innovation: asking patients to define their own concern thresholds rather than imposing a priori categories.

2. **Comparisons across result types.** Systematically comparing experiences across pharmacogenomic, hemochromatosis, colorectal cancer risk, and negative results within a single study is distinctive. Most prior work examined a single result type or treated genetic results generically.

3. **Inclusion of non-users in usability testing.** Including 42 portal non-users alongside 42 users in Aim 3 is methodologically thoughtful. Non-users provide a less-primed perspective and address the equity concern that designing only for existing users may embed access disparities.

4. **Epic-style and blue-sky prototypes.** The dual design approach acknowledges platform constraints while also exploring what ideal designs might look like—useful for both practical implementation and communicating aspirations to vendors.

The innovation is incremental rather than transformative; the project applies established qualitative and UCD methods to a relatively new application domain. This is appropriate for an R21. The independent subsequent literature has since produced related work (e.g., Clausen et al., *Genetics in Medicine Open*. 2024, on the Genetics Adviser; Trieu et al., *JMIR Formative Research*. 2025, on MyCancerGene), confirming that the domain was ripe for investigation but also indicating that the space has evolved. These later studies do not diminish the proposal's originality at its time of submission; they reinforce that the question was timely.

## Rigor: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

### Qualitative design

The semistructured interview approach is appropriate for exploratory, descriptive aims. The proposal describes piloting of protocols, audio recording, professional transcription, de-identification, and accuracy checking—all standard and appropriate procedures. Directed content analysis combined with thematic analysis (citing Hsieh & Shannon, Vaismoradi et al.) provides a defensible analytic framework. Dual coding with consensus resolution is specified. Atlas.ti support is appropriate.

The proposal invokes "idea saturation" as a stopping criterion alongside diversity targets. This is standard in qualitative research but the proposal does not specify how saturation will be operationally assessed (e.g., through a saturation grid or stopping rule), which introduces some ambiguity about whether 40 interviews per aim will be sufficient or whether fewer would do. The sample sizes are in the conventional range for qualitative interview studies of this type. The explicit demographic stratification targets (age, sex, race/ethnicity) are appropriate for ensuring variation.

### Usability testing

The Aim 3 design has several strengths: three iterative rounds with revision between them, counterbalanced presentation order, equal numbers of users and non-users, and think-aloud cognitive interviewing. The sample-size rationale, drawing on Faulkner (2003) and claiming a minimum problem-discovery rate above 98% per participant type, is technically referenced. However, the claim of 99.6% mean discovery rate merits scrutiny: Faulkner's work shows that problem discovery depends on problem prevalence, and with 14 participants per type per round (28 total, divided between users and non-users), three rounds are needed to reach this cumulative figure. The proposal uses three rounds across 84 total participants, which is a defensible operationalization.

### Limitations in rigor

Several aspects weaken the inferential strength:

1. **Recall bias in Aims 1 and 2.** Participants are asked to recount experiences of receiving results up to 12 months prior. Recollections may be shaped by subsequent clinical encounters, emotional processing, and memory decay. The proposal does not discuss how recall may differ systematically across result types (e.g., a cancer risk result may be remembered more vividly than a pharmacogenomic finding) or how this affects the comparison of concern thresholds.

2. **Single-institution, English-only sample.** Generalizability is limited to English-speaking patients at a large urban academic medical center. The proposal acknowledges this and positions it as a starting point for later multilingual work, which is acceptable for an R21.

3. **Prototype fidelity.** The proposal describes interactive prototypes presented on a laptop, but does not specify whether they are high-fidelity Epic-integrated mockups, standalone HTML/CSS prototypes, or lower-fidelity wireframes. The difference matters: a wireframe tested in a quiet research setting may not reveal the navigation challenges that arise in the actual portal environment with its surrounding interface elements, alerts, and competing information.

4. **No comparison condition.** Aim 3 tests prototypes but does not include a comparison with existing standard-of-care result displays. Without a baseline, it is difficult to assess whether the prototypes represent an improvement or merely a different arrangement.

5. **Heuristic walkthrough timing.** External consultants conduct heuristic walkthroughs after round 2, with recommendations considered before round 3. This is sensible but means that expert review findings cannot be tested with participants before the study ends, unless they are minor enough to incorporate without a further round.

## Feasibility: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

### Strengths

The institutional environment is favorable. UW Medicine has an established Epic-based patient portal (eCare), clinical and translational informatics services for participant identification, and the Harborview Family Medicine Clinic as a safety-net recruitment site. The advisory board includes the ambulatory IT lead (Jane Fellner), a medical geneticist (Gail Jarvik), a laboratory medicine informatics expert (Brian Shirts), and an external biomedical informatics advisor (Josh Peterson, Vanderbilt). These connections provide realistic access to the EHR environment and clinical expertise.

The schedule allocates two years across sequential Aims 1–2 followed by Aim 3. Aim 1 interviews (Q1–Q2) and analysis (Q2–Q3) feed into Aim 2 interviews (Q3–Q4) and analysis (Q4–Q5), which then inform prototype creation and round 1 testing (Q5–Q6). This sequential dependency is logical but creates a critical path: delays in Aim 1 recruitment or analysis cascade forward.

The team has appropriate expertise. Korngiebel's training includes formative research, electronic prototyping, and usability testing. Robins provides qualitative methods depth, Fullerton contributes genomic bioethics, and Amendola offers genetic counseling and clinical sequencing experience.

### Concerns

1. **Recruitment of specific result categories.** Aim 2 requires 10 participants each for pharmacogenomic findings, hemochromatosis, colorectal cancer risk, and negative results—all received within 12 months. The pool of patients with these specific results at a single institution may be limited, particularly for hemochromatosis and inherited colorectal cancer risk. The proposal does not provide estimates of the eligible population size, making it difficult to assess whether recruitment targets are realistic within the Q3–Q4 window.

2. **Non-user recruitment for Aim 3.** Recruiting 42 non-users (people who do not use eCare) through Harborview requires identifying individuals who are patients but not portal users. The proposal mentions direct clinic approaches and same-day data collection, which is sensible, but non-users may also be less engaged with the health system and harder to reach. The feasibility of recruiting 42 such participants within the project timeline is uncertain.

3. **Prototype development between Aims 2 and 3.** The schedule allocates roughly one quarter (Q5) for creating six to eight prototypes informed by the Aim 1 and 2 findings. This is a compressed timeline for the analytic synthesis, design decisions, content creation, visual design, and interactive prototype building. A graduate assistant is mentioned for prototype revision but not for initial creation.

4. **Dependence on Epic compatibility assessment.** The proposal states that "Epic staff will assess compatibility of Epic-style reports." This is an external dependency; the project does not control Epic's timeline or priorities. If compatibility review identifies substantial constraints, this could require redesign that the study timeline does not accommodate for testing.

## Ethics and Safety: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

The proposal describes proportionate ethical arrangements for a minimal-risk interview and usability study. Key elements include:

- HIPAA waiver for identifying eligible patients and IRB waiver of written consent documentation, with appropriate justification (reducing unnecessary identifying links).
- Clear description of procedures, confidentiality, voluntary participation, and absence of direct clinical benefit in consent discussions.
- Identification of foreseeable risks: discomfort discussing inherited risk, concern about audio recording, waiting-room recognition, and inadvertent disclosure of information about relatives.
- Provisions to address these: participants may stop at any time, third-party identifying information will be removed from recordings/transcripts, distressed participants will be directed to assistance.
- Data management: de-identified transcripts, separate storage of incentive administration information, secure servers/password-protected computers, locked physical materials, data destruction at study close.
- No children enrolled, no incidental clinical findings expected.

These are standard and appropriate. Two observations:

1. **Waiting-room recruitment** at Harborview Family Medicine Clinic raises the risk of recognition by others. The proposal acknowledges this risk and notes that participants may be recognized during recruitment. The mitigation (consent discussion covers this) is reasonable but would benefit from specifying whether private spaces are available for initial approach and consent.

2. **Non-users and the digital divide.** The proposal explicitly excludes non-English speakers because eCare is available only in English. This is a practical constraint, but it means that the equity analysis within the Justice domain of the Points to Consider framework will have limited empirical basis for linguistic minorities. The proposal acknowledges this as a limitation and positions later multilingual work as a separate effort.

## Management: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

### Team and governance

The PI (Korngiebel) has relevant training and prior work. The co-investigator team covers qualitative methods (Robins), genomic bioethics (Fullerton), and genetic counseling (Amendola). The ten-member advisory structure (advisory board, prototype consultants, mentoring committee) provides clinical, informatics, and design expertise. Eight of ten are clinicians, which is appropriate for content accuracy but may underrepresent patient advocacy or health literacy expertise.

The advisory board meets twice yearly with consultation as needed—appropriate for a two-year project. External prototype consultants (Welch, MUSC; Williams, Geisinger) provide perspectives from different health systems, supporting generalizability assessment.

### Schedule realism

The two-year, eight-quarter schedule is tight but logical. The sequential dependency of aims creates risk: Aim 2 depends on Aim 1 findings to refine protocols; Aim 3 depends on Aim 2 findings to inform prototypes. A delay in any aim compresses downstream work. The proposal does not describe contingency plans for recruitment shortfalls or analytic delays.

### Resource considerations

No itemized budget is provided in the review materials. The proposal describes access to UW Medicine infrastructure, informatics services, and clinical departments—all essential and apparently available. The $50 incentive for approximately one-hour sessions is standard. A graduate assistant supports Aim 3 prototype revision. No major resource gaps are evident from the narrative, though the absence of a dedicated prototype developer in the listed personnel is notable given the centrality of prototype creation to Aim 3. The proposal states Korngiebel has prototyping training; whether this is sufficient for six to eight interactive prototypes is uncertain.

## Overall Recommendation: Support

The proposal addresses a timely and practically important question with an appropriate formative design. Its strengths—attention to result-type heterogeneity, patient-defined thresholds, inclusion of portal non-users, dual Epic-style and blue-sky prototyping, and an iteratively refined ethical framework—constitute a coherent and worthwhile contribution. The limitations (recall bias, single-institution English-only sample, unspecified prototype fidelity, compressed prototype-development timeline, absence of a comparison condition in usability testing) are substantive but do not undermine the project's ability to meet its stated exploratory and developmental goals. These are limitations that affect the *scope* and *confidence* of the contribution rather than the project's capacity to produce useful descriptive evidence and prototypes.

The subsequent publication record (Korngiebel & West, 2022) confirms that the project, as designed, yielded concrete and actionable findings about patient preferences for coversheet summaries, jargon-free language, next steps, clinician contact information, and smartphone-optimized design. This outcome evidence is consistent with the prospective assessment that the plan was sound.

The project's contribution should be understood as a well-justified increment: descriptive categories of patient concern thresholds, empirically grounded design recommendations, and prototype templates that can seed subsequent clinical evaluation. It does not and need not claim to establish population-wide effectiveness or to solve all equity challenges in genomic portal design within a single R21.

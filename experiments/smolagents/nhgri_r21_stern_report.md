# Scientific Project Review

**Project:** Demographic Patterns of Eugenic Sterilization in California
**Principal investigator:** Alexandra Minna Stern (University of Michigan)
**Collaborators:** Sharon Kardia, Siobán Harlow
**Mechanism:** NIH/NHGRI R21
**Review date:** 2026-10-28

---

## Overall Assessment

This proposal addresses a historically significant and scientifically consequential question: who was subjected to eugenic sterilization in California's state institutions between 1921 and 1952, and through what demographic patterns, institutional mechanisms, and lived experiences did this coercive program operate? The project brings together an unusually rich primary source—approximately 15,000 digitized sterilization recommendation records from nine institutions, supplemented by correspondence and administrative forms—with an interdisciplinary team combining historical and epidemiological expertise. The intended contribution is a systematic, population-based account that distinguishes patterns of disproportionate institutionalization from disproportionate selection for sterilization within institutions, while also examining the qualitative dimension of consent, resistance, and changing institutional language.

The proposal is well conceived for the R21 exploratory mechanism. The preliminary work demonstrates feasibility: the records have been located, digitized, and entered into a REDCap database with 212 variables, IRB approvals are in place, and a pilot analysis from Pacific Colony yields statistically robust associations that motivate the full study. The team's combined expertise in the history of eugenics (Stern), genetic epidemiology (Kardia), and reproductive epidemiology (Harlow) is appropriate.

There are, however, material limitations that affect the prospective evaluation. The analytical methods for the qualitative work are described at a high level of generality, the case-cohort design and Poisson regression strategy for Aim 2 are indicated but not fully elaborated, and several critical dependencies—particularly the completeness and consistency of the historical census linkage—are acknowledged but not resolved in the proposal text. The companion digital archive (Aim 4) is presented as a dissemination aim with its development supported by separate funding; the proposal rightly does not claim it as an analytical deliverable of this award, but its dependence on parallel funding creates some uncertainty about the full knowledge contribution.

Weighing the strengths against the limitations, the project addresses an important question with a defensible research design, substantial preliminary data, and a team with relevant expertise. The limitations are real but addressable within the two-year scope, and none appears to fatally undermine the central aims. The proposal merits support.

## Scientific Value: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

The question is important on several registers. At the most straightforward level, the study will produce the most comprehensive descriptive epidemiology of eugenic sterilization yet available for any US state. California performed approximately 20,000 of the roughly 60,000 documented eugenic sterilizations in the United States, making it the single largest state program. The proposal's dataset of ~15,000 recommendation records from nine institutions during 1921–1952 represents roughly three-quarters of California's recorded procedures and one-quarter of the national total. A systematic description of who was recommended, with what characteristics, in which institutions, and at what times constitutes a material empirical contribution in its own right.

The proposal's analytical structure adds further value. By linking recommendation records with census microdata and institutional population reports, the study can distinguish two analytically distinct questions: (1) who was disproportionately institutionalized in the first place, and (2) among those institutionalized, who was disproportionately selected for sterilization. The Pacific Colony pilot illustrates the importance of this distinction: the unadjusted odds ratio of 2.41 for Spanish surname in the recommendation records versus the institution population might reflect either differential institutionalization or differential selection, or both. The proposed case-cohort design with Poisson regression aims to disentangle these pathways, which is a more rigorous inferential goal than simple descriptive enumeration.

The qualitative dimension adds a further layer of contribution. The analysis of consent practices, refusal letters, and changing diagnostic terminology connects demographic patterns to institutional mechanisms and individual agency. The proposal's attention to the distinction between "a signature, acquiescence, refusal, and meaningful choice" (Section 2) is conceptually careful. The approximately 50 handwritten letters from patients at Patton State Hospital objecting to their own sterilization are a particularly compelling source that can illuminate the experience of people who were not merely passive subjects of institutional power.

The proposal also situates its contribution within contemporary ELSI (ethical, legal, and social implications) discourse. The connection between historical eugenic practices and current questions in genetics and reproductive technology is plausible and frequently drawn in the literature, though the proposal does not claim to test causal links; rather, it offers evidence that can inform those discussions.

The principal limitation affecting scientific value is that the project is fundamentally descriptive and associational rather than causal. It can characterize who was sterilized, identify disproportionate patterns, and document institutional practices, but the design cannot establish why particular groups were targeted—that inference depends on the qualitative and historical interpretation. This is not a defect as much as a feature of the research type: the proposal is explicit that it aims to "investigate patterns and experiences" and to "examine how these practices operated." The value lies in systematic evidence about what happened and to whom, which has been surprisingly absent from the historical literature despite the prominence of the California program.

A second consideration is that the project's scope is limited to recommendation records, not confirmed sterilization procedures. The proposal acknowledges this: recommendation forms are assumed to reflect sterilization decisions, but some recommendations may not have resulted in procedures, and some procedures may have occurred without surviving recommendations. This introduces measurement error whose direction and magnitude are difficult to assess. The proposal treats this appropriately as a known limitation rather than a decisive weakness, but it does constrain the precision of the population-based rate estimates.

## Innovation: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

The proposal's innovation is best understood as a methodological and empirical increment over existing work, rather than a conceptual breakthrough. This is appropriate for an R21 and does not diminish its value, but it frames the novelty assessment.

The broad history of eugenic sterilization in the United States is well established. Key monographs—including Stern's own *Eugenic Nation* (2005), Bashford and Levine's *Oxford Handbook of the History of Eugenics* (2010), and work on other state programs such as North Carolina and Virginia—have documented the legal frameworks, ideological justifications, and institutional contexts. The proposal's contribution is not to overturn this narrative but to replace general claims about disproportionality with systematic quantitative evidence.

The innovation has several dimensions. First, the scale is unprecedented: approximately 15,000 individual-level records with 212 coded variables, linked to census population denominators, constitutes the largest quantitative analysis of eugenic sterilization ever attempted. Prior work has largely relied on aggregate institutional reports, selected case studies, or narrative historical methods. Second, the explicit epidemiological framework—treating sterilization recommendation as an outcome with demographic exposures, constructing population denominators, and using regression methods to estimate adjusted associations—brings analytical rigor that has been absent from most historical work on this topic. Third, the integration of quantitative and qualitative methods within a single study is uncommon in this literature, where historical and epidemiological approaches typically proceed in separate publications.

The Spanish-surname methodology also represents a methodological contribution. By using standardized Census Bureau surname lists and manual review of misspellings, the team can construct a consistent indicator of likely Hispanic/Latino ethnicity across historical sources whose racial and ethnic classifications are inconsistent (e.g., the 1930 census's Mexican racial category versus the 1920 census's reliance on birthplace). This approach addresses a genuine methodological challenge in historical demography.

Relative to the closest prior work, the novelty is real but bounded. External retrieval confirms that the core methodology—linking sterilization records with census microdata to estimate population-based rates using Poisson regression—was subsequently published by this team in Novak et al. (2018, *American Journal of Public Health*, "Disproportionate Sterilization of Latinos Under California's Eugenic Sterilization Program, 1920–1945") and extended to Asian immigrants in Kaniecki et al. (2023, *Social Forces*, "Racialization and Reproduction: Asian Immigrants and California's Twentieth-Century Eugenic Sterilization Program"). The Stern et al. (2017, *AJPH*, "California's Sterilization Survivors: An Estimate and Call for Redress") publication used the same underlying dataset of >19,000 recommendations. As these are outcome publications from the funded project, they are not treated as independent prior work for the merit assessment. The question is whether, at the time of the proposal, the approach represented a meaningful step beyond what was available. The answer is yes: existing quantitative work on California eugenic sterilization was limited to aggregate counts and narrative description; the systematic census-linkage approach with regression modeling was novel.

The qualitative analysis of consent and resistance builds on existing scholarship, including Stern and Lira's own prior publication (Lira and Stern, 2014, *Aztlán*, "Resisting reproductive injustice in California") and the broader literature on patient agency in institutional settings. The innovation here lies in applying systematic coding to the full corpus of refusal letters and connecting these findings with the quantitative patterns, rather than in an entirely new interpretive framework.

## Rigor: C (Moderate) — Confidence: C (Moderate)

The proposal presents a defensible overall design, but the level of methodological specification is uneven across aims, and several inferential challenges receive insufficient attention.

**Aim 1 (Descriptive analysis)** is straightforward and well supported. The REDCap instrument's 212 variables, combined with Stata 13 for tabulation and description, is appropriate for characterizing the recommendation records by demographics, diagnosis, institution, superintendent, family structure, and temporal trends. The pilot work demonstrates that the data can support this aim. No serious threats to validity arise at the descriptive level.

**Aim 2 (Population-based risk comparisons)** is more ambitious and less fully specified. The proposal states an intention to use a "case-cohort design" and "Poisson regression" to test hypotheses about disproportionate sterilization. Several aspects require scrutiny:

*Denominator construction.* The proposal will use census microdata (1920, 1930, 1940) to identify residents of the nine institutions and construct annual population estimates, with interpolation between census years and supplementation from institutional reports and 1950 tract-level data. The feasibility of identifying specific institution residents in historical census microdata is not demonstrated in the proposal, though the pilot analysis at Pacific Colony suggests it is achievable for at least one institution. The quality of the resulting denominators will vary by year (census years provide direct enumeration; inter-census years require interpolation) and by institution (some may be easier to identify in census records than others). The proposal acknowledges data availability constraints in Table 1, but does not fully discuss how interpolation assumptions might affect rate estimates.

*Variable comparability.* The proposal is appropriately cautious about variable consistency across sources and time periods. The recommendation data have different available variables in 1921–1934 versus 1935–1952 (diagnosis is only available in the later period). Census racial/ethnic classifications change across decades. The Spanish-surname approach provides a consistent indicator, but other variables (diagnosis, family structure) will have limited comparability. The proposal states that "the data availability table will guide construction of identical or commensurate variables," which is a reasonable but unspecified plan.

*Confounding.* The proposal does not discuss confounding in detail. The pilot logistic regression adjusts for gender, Spanish surname, and age, but the full analysis will presumably need to account for institution, period, diagnosis, and potentially other variables. The proposal mentions that associations may "differ by institution, superintendent, diagnosis, family context, or period" but does not specify which confounders will be included in adjusted models or how model selection will proceed. This is a gap in the analytical plan.

*Clustering and dependence.* The proposal notes that it will "assess variation within and across institutions." The Novak et al. (2018) outcome publication used Poisson regression with a random effect for institution, suggesting this was the approach ultimately adopted. The proposal itself does not specify whether mixed-effects models, cluster-robust standard errors, or stratified analyses will be used to handle institutional clustering.

*Case-cohort design.* The proposal invokes a "case-cohort design" but does not elaborate the sampling frame, the subcohort selection, or the analytical implications. In a typical case-cohort design, the subcohort is a random sample of the source population, and cases outside the subcohort are added. Here, the "cases" appear to be all sterilization recommendations, and the proposed denominator is all institutional residents. This is more naturally described as a cohort design with internal comparisons, or a census of cases with population denominators, than a case-cohort design. The terminology may reflect a misunderstanding or imprecise usage.

**Aim 3 (Qualitative analysis)** is described in general terms. The proposal states that letters of protest and non-consent will be "manually coded to identify themes" and that the team will analyze "all cases in which a patient, parent, next of kin, or guardian refused consent." The coding approach, thematic analysis framework, and procedures for ensuring interpretive reliability are not specified. For a historical-qualitative study, this level of detail may be acceptable if the team has demonstrated relevant expertise; Stern's track record in this area is strong. However, the proposal would benefit from a clearer statement of the analytical framework (e.g., grounded theory, thematic analysis, discourse analysis) and procedures for triangulation between quantitative and qualitative findings.

*Aim 4 (Companion archive)* is a dissemination aim and is not evaluated for analytical rigor. Its dependence on separate humanities funding is noted but not treated as a rigor concern for the proposed work.

The integration of quantitative and qualitative evidence is a strength of the design in principle, but the proposal does not fully specify how the two strands will inform each other. The statement that "numerical associations and close reading of the documents will jointly support interpretation" is a goal, not a method.

## Feasibility: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

The project is substantially feasible given what is documented in the proposal. Several factors support this judgment:

**Data access and preparation.** The records have been located, digitized, and entered into a REDCap database. IRB approvals from the California Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects and the University of Michigan biomedical IRB are in place. Data entry is scheduled for completion before the analytical phase begins. These are critical preparatory steps that have been accomplished, substantially reducing the risk of data-related delays.

**Pilot evidence.** The Pacific Colony pilot analysis demonstrates that the core analytical workflow—linking recommendation records with census population data, constructing odds ratios, and fitting adjusted logistic models—produces interpretable results. The pilot's findings (OR 2.07 for Spanish surname, OR 3.21 for age under 18, both p < 0.001) are statistically robust and motivate the extension to the full dataset.

**Team expertise.** Stern is a leading historian of eugenics in the United States, with direct experience working with these records. Kardia and Harlow bring epidemiological and quantitative methods expertise from the University of Michigan School of Public Health. The combination covers the methodological range needed.

**Institutional resources.** The project uses the existing data-entry and secure-computing environment at Michigan, with established REDCap infrastructure. Census microdata are available through the Institute for Social Research.

**Schedule.** The 24-month timeline with overlapping phases is realistic for the scope of work described. Year 1 focuses on census companion dataset construction, descriptive statistics, initial hypothesis testing, and qualitative coding; Year 2 extends analyses, develops the consent and refusal analysis, and prepares publications. The parallel conduct of quantitative and qualitative work is sensible.

Several dependencies warrant attention but do not appear to threaten feasibility:

*Census linkage.* Identifying residents of nine specific institutions in the 1920, 1930, and 1940 census microdata is the main methodological dependency. The proposal anticipates "census information on more than 100,000 residents of sterilizing institutions." The pilot at Pacific Colony (1,068 residents identified in the 1940 census) suggests this is feasible, but the nine institutions may vary in how readily their residents can be identified in census records. The proposal does not discuss fallback strategies if some institutions prove difficult to enumerate.

*Variable construction.* Harmonizing variables across recommendation records (two periods with different available variables), census microdata (three decades with changing classifications), and institutional reports requires careful decisions. The proposal acknowledges this and commits to "constructing identical or commensurate variables," but the specific harmonization rules are not pre-specified. This is a legitimate area of planned methodological work.

*Qualitative corpus size.* The proposal identifies approximately 50 handwritten patient letters from Patton and an unspecified number of other protest documents. The scope appears manageable, but the total size of the qualitative corpus is not stated, making it difficult to assess whether two years is adequate for the coding and analysis described.

## Ethics and Safety: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

The proposal addresses the distinctive ethical dimensions of this research appropriately.

The study involves retrospective research on historical records of a coercive state program. There is no direct contact with the people represented in the records. The ethical obligation centers on protecting the privacy and dignity of the individuals whose sterilization recommendations are recorded, many of whom may still be alive (the proposal estimates approximately 831 survivors as of 2016 in the related Stern et al. 2017 publication).

The data protection measures are well specified: de-identification of the analytical dataset, HIPAA-compliant REDCap instruments, password-protected servers, encrypted storage of digitized source copies in a locked safe, and secure transport of any physical records. Team members must complete research-responsibility, confidentiality, and REDCap training before access, and must be listed on the IRB protocol. Identifiable source information will not be distributed outside the study or published.

The resource-sharing plan commits to releasing suitable de-identified data after acceptance of principal findings, with disclosure risk minimized, subject to the privacy restrictions on the historical records. This is an appropriate balance.

Several considerations are well handled:

*Vulnerable populations.* The records represent people institutionalized with intellectual or psychiatric diagnoses, including children (ages approximately 7–58). The proposal acknowledges this and the attendant responsibilities. The IRB approvals from both the California Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects and the University of Michigan biomedical IRB provide external validation of the ethical framework.

*Institutional complicity.* The proposal does not shy away from the fact that the institutions whose records are being studied were agents of coercive reproductive control. The framing—examining "the decisions, categories, and institutional routines through which coercive practices were carried out"—is appropriately analytical rather than sensational.

*Dissemination and dignity.* The commitment that "individual identification will not be disclosed through the research outputs" is appropriate. The proposal's attention to preserving "the distinction between institutional claims about a person and that person's own responses" reflects ethical care in representing historical subjects.

The proposal does not address community engagement or consultation with descendant communities (e.g., Latino, disability rights, or sterilization survivor organizations). Whether this is a gap depends on the research type: historical research on archival records does not universally require community consultation, and the IRB approvals suggest that the institutional review process did not require it. However, given the sensitivity of the subject and the contemporary relevance claimed for the findings, some form of community engagement could strengthen the ethical framework. I note this as an observation rather than a decisive weakness, as the proposal's data protection measures are otherwise thorough.

## Management: C (Moderate) — Confidence: C (Moderate)

The management plan is described at a level appropriate to the R21 mechanism but lacks detail in several areas.

**Personnel and expertise.** The three named investigators cover the necessary expertise: Stern (history of eugenics, California institutional records), Kardia (genetic epidemiology, quantitative methods), and Harlow (reproductive epidemiology). The proposal does not specify the time commitment (percent effort) of each investigator, the roles of any additional staff (research assistants, data managers, the project manager mentioned in the data-sharing plan), or how the day-to-day coordination between the historical and epidemiological components will be accomplished. The proposal mentions a "project manager" in the data-sharing section without describing this role elsewhere.

**Schedule and milestones.** The 24-month Gantt-style table provides a high-level roadmap. Year 1 includes census dataset creation, descriptive statistics, initial hypothesis testing, and qualitative coding. Year 2 extends hypothesis testing, develops consent analysis, and prepares publications. The phases overlap sensibly. However, the schedule lacks specific milestones, deliverables, or contingency plans. For example, there is no milestone for completing the census companion dataset, for achieving a target inter-rater reliability in qualitative coding, or for submitting the first manuscript.

**Coordination.** The proposal states that "quantitative and qualitative work will continue in parallel so that emerging findings can guide contextual investigation and the presentation of results." This is a sound principle, but the mechanisms for achieving it—regular meetings, shared documentation, integrated analysis sessions—are not described. The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings is a distinctive strength of the proposal's intellectual design, but the management plan does not specify how this integration will be operationalized.

**Aim 4 dependency.** The companion digital archive (Aim 4) is explicitly described as a parallel project with separate humanities funding. The proposal states that analytical findings "will inform" the archive, but the archive's development is not within the scope of this award. This is a reasonable arrangement, but it means that Aim 4 is better understood as a dissemination pathway than as a deliverable. The management plan appropriately does not allocate resources to Omeka development, but the boundary between this project's contributions and the companion archive's development is not sharply drawn.

**Budget and resources.** The proposal does not include a budget (consistent with the source material being an application narrative rather than a full administrative submission). The computing environment, REDCap infrastructure, and census data access are documented, but staffing levels and equipment costs are not specified. In the absence of a budget, I note that the management plan's feasibility depends on adequate staffing for data entry completion, census data extraction, statistical analysis, and qualitative coding—tasks whose combined scope could strain a small team over 24 months. The proposal's reference to data entry being completed "before the main analytical phase" suggests that data entry staffing is already addressed, but this cannot be verified from the proposal text.

## Recommendation: Support

The project addresses a historically important and scientifically under-documented question with a defensible design, substantial preliminary evidence, and an experienced interdisciplinary team. The strengths lie in the comprehensive dataset (~15,000 records, 212 variables), the systematic approach to constructing population denominators from census microdata, the integration of quantitative and qualitative methods, and the connection to contemporary ELSI discourse. The Pacific Colony pilot provides credible evidence that the analytical approach can yield interpretable and statistically robust findings.

The limitations—under-specified analytical methods for Aim 2 (particularly the case-cohort design and confounding strategy), general description of qualitative coding procedures, and a management plan that lacks milestones and coordination mechanisms—are real but addressable within the scope of a two-year R21. None appears to threaten the core aims. The companion archive (Aim 4) is appropriately treated as a dissemination pathway dependent on separate funding and does not represent a critical dependency for the project's scientific contribution.

The project's intended contribution—a systematic, population-based, and contextualized account of eugenic sterilization in California—is proportionate to the methods and resources described. The work will fill a genuine gap in the empirical literature and has the potential to inform both historical scholarship and contemporary discussions of genetics, reproduction, and reproductive justice.

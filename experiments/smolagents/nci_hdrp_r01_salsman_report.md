# Scientific Project Review

**Proposal:** *Using MOST to EMPOWER: Optimizing Emotional Regulation Intervention to Enhance Well-being Among Young Adult Cancer Survivors*
**Principal Investigators:** John Salsman and Judith Moskowitz
**Mechanism:** R01
**Review date:** 2026-10-28 (prospective assessment of the submitted plan)

---

## Overall Assessment

This proposal seeks to use the Multiphase Optimization Strategy (MOST) to identify which of five positive-emotion skills components—positive events/gratitude, mindfulness, positive reappraisal, personal strengths/goal-setting, and acts of kindness—contribute meaningfully to psychological well-being in young adult cancer survivors after active treatment. A full factorial experiment (2⁵ = 32 conditions, N = 352) will estimate main effects and selected interactions for each component, with the goal of assembling an optimized, web-based intervention (EMPOWER) ready for confirmatory testing.

The proposal addresses a genuine and important gap. Young adult cancer survivors experience persistent distress and disrupted developmental trajectories, yet the evidence base for well-being interventions in this population is thin. The MOST framework is appropriate for the stated optimization goal: it can efficiently screen components before a costly definitive trial, avoiding the common problem of evaluating an unrefined multi-component package where no one learns which ingredients matter. The investigative team brings complementary expertise in young-adult oncology, positive-emotion intervention, and MOST trial methods, and the preliminary work establishes feasibility of the adapted EMPOWER content and web delivery.

However, material concerns affect the proposal's ability to deliver decisive optimization. The design yields 11 participants per condition, which is adequate for estimating main effects (176 per component level) but leaves interaction detection severely underpowered (detectable interaction d ≈ 0.62). In a factorial optimization trial where some components may only work in combination—or may interfere with one another—the inability to detect modest but practically meaningful interactions is a significant limitation. The retention assumptions (72% from recruitment to primary endpoint) are optimistic relative to the proposers' own pilot data (58–66% adherence in the second pilot wave), and the conservative 13% recruitment rate sits well below the historically observed rates. Neither of these concerns is fatal in isolation, but together they threaten the trial's ability to reach the planned analytic sample and precision targets. Most importantly, the selection criterion (retain components with effects around d = 0.50, tested at α = 0.10) conflates modest effects with negligible ones for the purpose of building an optimized package: components with effects of d = 0.25–0.45 may offer worthwhile benefits at low burden yet would be discarded. The proposal does not describe a formal decision framework that weighs effect magnitude against burden, cost, or mechanistic plausibility.

The scientific direction is worthwhile, the MOST framework is well chosen, and both the target population and outcome domain represent important under-served areas. However, the current plan has substantive problems—particularly the underspecification of the component retention decision rule, the optimistic retention and recruitment assumptions, and the limited capacity to detect interactions—that affect whether the study can reliably answer its central optimization question. The proposal is therefore judged **worthy of revision** to strengthen these aspects before proceeding.

## Scientific Value: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

The question is well motivated. Young adults diagnosed with cancer (age 15–39 at diagnosis) face a distinctive intersection of developmental disruption and disease burden. They navigate education, career entry, relationship formation, and fertility concerns while managing treatment sequelae and lingering distress. As the proposal notes, the Barnett et al. (2016) systematic review (*J Cancer Surviv*) and Kwak et al. (2013) longitudinal study (*J Clin Oncol*) document that psychological distress can persist well beyond active treatment. Survivorship spans decades; well-being interventions that work during this window can affect a large segment of remaining life.

The proposal's dual focus on reducing distress *and* increasing positive affect is conceptually worthwhile. It draws on Broaden-and-Build Theory (Fredrickson) and Revised Stress and Coping Theory (Folkman), treating positive emotion not merely as an indicator of absence of pathology but as a resource that may expand coping repertoires. This is consistent with a mature literature (Pressman & Cohen, 2005; Lyubomirsky, King & Diener, 2005) and the team's own work with the LILAC intervention. The outcome battery is thoughtfully selected: PROMIS and NIH Toolbox CATs for depression, anxiety, positive affect, and life satisfaction provide standardized, psychometrically sound measurement with low respondent burden. The one-month primary endpoint with follow-up through six months is appropriate for an optimization trial that must balance detecting sustained effects against the practical demands of retention.

The attainable knowledge is primarily methodological and descriptive: estimates of which components, individually and in selected combinations, produce detectable changes in well-being outcomes, and exploratory evidence about mediation (coping self-efficacy, social support) and moderation (age, gender). This is exactly what the MOST optimization phase is designed to deliver. The proposal is appropriately modest: it does not promise effectiveness or clinical implementation, reserving those for a subsequent confirmatory trial. External evidence from the Fit2Thrive trial (Solk et al., 2023, *Annals of Behavioral Medicine*) and the J-SUPPORT 2001 smartphone CBT trial (Akechi et al., 2024, *Psychiatry and Clinical Neurosciences*) demonstrates that MOST factorial trials in cancer populations are feasible and can be informative even when individual component effects are not detected—a finding that underscores both the value and the risk of this approach. The EMPOWER study could similarly contribute whether components are identified or not: a null component pattern would itself be important, suggesting that the active ingredients benefit from being delivered as an integrated whole, or that the measurement context and population characteristics matter more than component content.

A limitation is that the proposal describes four primary outcomes (positive affect, life satisfaction, depression, anxiety) without specifying how selection decisions will be made across multiple endpoints. If a component improves positive affect but not depression, what happens? The proposal does not articulate a decision rule for resolving inconsistent patterns. This reduces the clarity of the knowledge contribution.

The scientific value is good: a well-justified optimization question in an under-served population, using an appropriate framework and validated measures. The failure to specify multi-outcome decision rules keeps this from excellent.

## Innovation: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

The proposal does not claim a novel mechanism or a fundamentally new intervention class. The five candidate components—positive-event noticing, mindfulness, reappraisal, strengths/goal-setting, and kindness—are drawn from established positive psychology and emotion-regulation traditions, and the team has previously deployed similar content in LILAC (for dementia caregivers) and other populations (IRISS for HIV, DAHLIA for diabetes, LEAF for metastatic breast cancer caregivers). The innovation lies in the *application context and the optimization approach* rather than in the components themselves.

The application to young adult cancer survivors after treatment is genuinely novel. The proposers' own Table 1 shows that prior positive-affect interventions in this tradition have been tested in HIV, diabetes, metastatic breast cancer caregivers, and dementia caregivers—not in young adult cancer survivors. The adaptation work described in the preliminary studies (language, examples, images revised for post-treatment experiences and physical limitations) adds modest but relevant innovation in tailoring. The systematic review by Barnett et al. (2016) confirms that psychosocial intervention evidence for AYA survivors is sparse, and the 2025 systematic review by Mostafa et al. (*Cancers*) on digital supportive care for AYAs found diverse interventions but none using a MOST factorial optimization design for well-being skills.

The methodological innovation of applying MOST to screen positive-emotion components is a meaningful increment. MOST factorial trials are increasingly used in behavioral intervention science—Fit2Thrive (Solk et al., 2023), SHINE (Shaffer et al., 2024, *JMIR Research Protocols*), ROSETA (Smith et al., 2025), and the sleep intervention for gynecologic cancer survivors (Fox et al., 2022) are recent examples in cancer populations—but none to date have applied this framework to a web-based positive-emotion skills intervention for AYA survivors. The EMPOWER trial is thus positioned to contribute both component-effect estimates for this specific intervention and methodological precedent for the approach in this population.

The innovation is good rather than excellent because the components, theoretical grounding, and delivery platform are not themselves new. The increment is in the systematic screening of well-established skills within a novel population and delivery context, and in the optimization goal rather than a packaged effectiveness evaluation. This is a legitimate and well-justified increment.

## Rigor: C (Moderate) — Confidence: B (High)

### Strengths

The factorial design is correctly specified for estimating main effects. With 176 participants assigned to each component level, the design pools across all other factors and provides adequate precision for the stated detectable main effect of d = 0.31 at 80% power with α = 0.10. The analytic approach—ANCOVA adjusting for baseline with component indicators, plus longitudinal mixed-effects models using all follow-up observations—is standard and appropriate for this design. The use of PROMIS CATs and NIH Toolbox measures provides well-validated, normed instruments with established sensitivity to change, and the common scale (T-score metric) facilitates comparison across outcomes. Block randomization (size 64) with a study statistician managing allocation is appropriate.

The inclusion of an attention-matched control strategy—replacing absent components with inert factual journaling and readings—is a strength. It preserves blinding to condition, maintains engagement equity, and provides a credible counterfactual. The proposal's acknowledgment that this control content might itself improve well-being (attenuating estimated EMPOWER effects) shows appropriate methodological awareness.

The assessment schedule (baseline, weekly during intervention, 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months post-intervention) is well designed. Weekly assessments during the five-week intervention can help distinguish component-related changes temporally, and the one-month primary endpoint with extended follow-up allows assessment of durability—important for an intervention whose components are meant to be skills that persist.

### Limitations

**1. Interaction power is inadequate for the optimization goal.** The proposal states a detectable interaction effect of d = 0.62 (95% CI 0.37–0.87). This is a large effect. In a factorial optimization trial where components may plausibly interact—for instance, mindfulness may enhance the effects of reappraisal, or acts of kindness may be less effective without the groundwork of gratitude—the inability to detect moderate interactions (d ≈ 0.3–0.5) is a significant design limitation. The proposal acknowledges this ("the study may therefore miss smaller interactions") but does not adjust its selection strategy accordingly. If interactions are important but undetectable, the optimization may select components that work well in isolation but poorly in combination, or discard components that potentiate others. This concern is reinforced by the external literature: the J-SUPPORT 2001 trial found no significant main effects for its three CBT components and no significant interactions, while Fit2Thrive likewise found no component-specific effects—suggesting that in behavioral MOST trials, component main effects may often be smaller than anticipated and interactions may be challenging to detect. A possible partial remedy, noted in the proposal but not elaborated, would be to pre-specify a small number of theory-driven interactions as primary and power for them, while treating others as exploratory.

**2. The component retention criterion is underspecified.** The proposal states an expected effect size "around 0.50" as the criterion for retention, with α = 0.10. This implies that components with effects below roughly d = 0.50 would be dropped. However, the design is powered to detect d = 0.31—so a component with an effect of d = 0.40 would be detectable but would be discarded under the stated criterion. The proposal does not articulate whether the 0.50 threshold is a hard cutoff, whether confidence intervals around effects inform decisions, whether burden or cost considerations modify the threshold, or how decisions proceed when effects differ across the four primary outcomes. Without this specification, the optimization risks being arbitrary. A formal decision framework—even a simple one, such as a Pareto approach trading effect magnitude against participant burden—would strengthen the rigor considerably.

**3. Retention assumptions are optimistic.** The power analysis assumes 72% retention from recruitment to the one-month primary endpoint (352 recruited → 256 analyzed). The proposers' own pilot data tell a more cautionary tale: the Northwestern wave retained 72% of the small enrolled sample (13/18), while the Wake Forest wave reported 66.7% retention and 58.3% adherence with 11 participants still on study. These are small samples, but the direction suggests the 72% assumption sits at the optimistic boundary of observed experience. The Fit2Thrive trial achieved 93.3% completion of at least one follow-up assessment at 12 weeks—a more encouraging benchmark—but that study used a different population (breast cancer survivors, mean age 52.5) and a different intervention (physical activity promotion with a Fitbit core). The EMPOWER retention assumption is plausible but not conservative; sensitivity analyses at lower retention (e.g., 60%) and their implications for detectable effects would strengthen design transparency.

**4. Multiple primary outcomes without a decision framework.** Four co-primary outcomes are specified. The proposal describes separate models for each but does not explain how component selection proceeds when effects diverge across outcomes. A component that improves positive affect but not depression may be retained or dropped depending on the implicit weighting; the proposal does not make this explicit.

**5. Contamination and sequencing effects.** The five components are delivered in a fixed weekly order (gratitude → mindfulness → reappraisal → strengths/goals → kindness). Any participant receiving a later component (e.g., kindness in week 5) has already received all earlier assigned components. This means that the main effect estimate for a later component is conditional on the presence or absence of earlier components—a design feature that is handled by the factorial structure but complicates interpretation if there are carryover or sequencing effects. The proposal does not discuss whether the fixed order could create order effects that are confounded with component identity.

**6. Absence of treatment fidelity assessment.** The proposal does not describe how intervention delivery fidelity will be monitored. In a web-based self-administered intervention, fidelity concerns shift from therapist adherence to platform function, content delivery, and participant engagement—but some form of fidelity monitoring (e.g., tracking completion of instructional modules, practice logging rates) is standard and would strengthen confidence that components were delivered as intended.

Taken together, these issues reduce the rigor from good to moderate. The core design is sound, the measures are strong, and the analytic approach is appropriate. But the interaction power limitation, the underspecified selection criterion, and the optimistic retention assumptions collectively weaken the inference that the trial can reliably identify the optimal component combination for the subsequent confirmatory trial.

## Feasibility: C (Moderate) — Confidence: B (High)

### Recruitment

The proposal estimates approximately 2,850 potentially eligible survivors across the two sites (Wake Forest ≈ 550/year, Northwestern ≈ 400/year over three years). At a conservative 13% recruitment assumption, this yields the target of 352. The arithmetic is plausible, and the two-site design with comprehensive cancer center registries provides a credible identification pathway. The addition of community partnership with Cancer Services as a backup recruitment channel is a reasonable contingency.

However, the pilot data suggest caution. The first wave enrolled 18 of 185 potentially eligible survivors (9.7%). The second wave enrolled 23 of 99 contacted (23.2%). Both figures include community/online recruitment in the second wave, which may not reflect registry-based recruitment alone. The proposal acknowledges these rates and describes a "16.5% eligible-and-interested proportion derived from pilot experience," but the relationship between this figure and the 13% recruitment assumption is not fully reconciled. The proposers' plan to use multiple channels (letters, calls, organizations, flyers, electronic/community postings) and dedicated staff is sensible and mirrors strategies that improved accrual from wave 1 to wave 2, but there is genuine uncertainty about whether the 13% rate is achievable across three years of sustained recruitment.

### Retention

As discussed under Rigor, the retention assumption of 72% to the one-month primary endpoint is at the upper end of pilot experience. A lower retention rate reduces the effective sample size and increases the minimum detectable effect. The proposal does not describe a plan for monitoring retention mid-trial and adjusting recruitment targets or retention strategies. The eight-week window allowed for the five-week program is a reasonable accommodation for interruptions in this population, and the $20-per-assessment incentive (up to $100) provides modest compensation for assessment burden.

### Technical infrastructure

The Wake Forest Research Information Systems Unit will host the intervention, and REDCap will manage screening, consent, and survey data. Both platforms are established and appropriate. The web-based delivery is well suited to a population of 18–39-year-olds who are likely to have smartphone and computer access, though the requirement for reliable internet access may selectively exclude survivors with fewer resources—a point noted but not elaborated in the proposal.

### Component delivery plausibility

Each week involves one to two days of instructional material plus daily home practice of 20–30 minutes. This is a substantial time commitment over five weeks. The pilot data showed 5.28 days per week of home practice in the LILAC study (older caregivers), but EMPOWER pilot adherence was lower (58.3% in the second wave). The proposal does not discuss how adherence will be monitored or incentivized during the factorial trial, or what minimum engagement threshold is needed for a component to be considered "received." This is a consequential omission: if participants assigned to a component engage minimally, the main effect estimate for that component will be attenuated, and the optimization may discard an intrinsically effective component due to poor implementation rather than poor efficacy. The Fit2Thrive experience (app use 4.7 days/week, Fitbit worn 93% of days) suggests that engagement can be good with a compelling core product, but EMPOWER's core is less inherently engaging than a Fitbit—it requires cognitive and emotional effort without the feedback loops of a device.

The feasibility is moderate. The two-site recruitment pathway is credible but unproven at the needed scale and rate; retention assumptions are optimistic; and the dependence on sustained participant engagement without a described engagement-monitoring and support plan introduces additional risk.

## Ethics and Safety: B (Good) — Confidence: C (Moderate)

The proposal describes appropriate ethical safeguards at the level of detail expected in a research narrative (as opposed to a full IRB application). Key features include:

- **Informed consent:** Provided through the REDCap workflow after eligibility screening, with study IDs and PINs protecting questionnaire access.
- **Eligibility exclusions:** Significant psychiatric history and palliative/hospice care are excluded. This is a proportionate safety measure: EMPOWER is a self-administered, web-based skills intervention that is not designed for acute psychiatric management. Survivors with serious mental illness are appropriately directed to clinical care.
- **Data security:** REDCap provides secure survey and database infrastructure; the institutional research information systems unit hosts the intervention. Unique identifiers separate research data from personal identifiers.
- **Remote delivery:** The web-based format allows participation from home, reducing burden and travel exposure—particularly relevant for a population that may have compromised immune function or fatigue.
- **Non-interference with clinical care:** The proposal states that EMPOWER preserves participants' ongoing clinical care. No experimental treatment modifications are involved.

Several aspects remain unspecified at the level of this proposal narrative:

- **Adverse event monitoring and reporting procedures** are not described. While EMPOWER is a low-risk supportive-care intervention, psychological interventions can occasionally elicit distress, and a plan for monitoring and responding to deteriorations would be expected.
- **Data and safety monitoring** arrangements are not specified. An R01-level clinical trial would typically include a Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) or equivalent oversight.
- **Privacy protections** for the intervention platform itself (distinct from REDCap) are described only in general terms ("unique study IDs and PINs"). The proposal does not detail encryption, data storage, or access controls specific to the EMPOWER web application.
- **Inclusivity:** The requirement for English literacy excludes non-English-speaking survivors, which may disproportionately affect certain racial/ethnic groups. The proposal does not discuss plans for future translation or adaptation.

These unspecified elements do not, in my assessment, indicate ethical inadequacy. They reflect the level of detail typical of a scientific proposal narrative rather than an IRB protocol. The core ethical design—voluntary consent, appropriate exclusions, data security, and low-risk remote delivery—is sound. Confidence is moderate rather than high because several standard trial-safety elements are not documented in the available text, and without the full IRB materials one cannot confirm their presence or adequacy.

## Management: B (Good) — Confidence: C (Moderate)

### Team expertise

The PI team combines relevant expertise. Salsman contributes young-adult oncology, survivorship research, and patient-reported outcome measurement; Moskowitz contributes positive-emotion intervention development and testing, with a track record spanning LILAC, IRISS, DAHLIA, and LEAF. Wagner brings MOST trial design and execution experience. The named collaborators cover psychosocial oncology, medical oncology, quantitative methods, survivorship, and eHealth—a reasonably comprehensive team for the planned work.

### Schedule

The four-year timeline (Table 5) places study startup in Year 1 Q1–Q2, Aim 1 optimization from Year 1 Q2 through Year 4 Q2, analysis from Year 3 Q1 through Year 4 Q3, and dissemination and follow-up grant preparation in Year 4 Q2–Q4. The overlapping of optimization (recruitment and data collection) with analysis is efficient and realistic. The schedule assumes recruitment across approximately 10–11 quarters (Year 1 Q2 through Year 4 Q2), which at an average rate of ~33 participants per quarter is consistent with the recruitment assumptions. The timeline for follow-up R01 preparation in Year 4 Q2–Q4 is tight—results from the six-month follow-up of the last enrolled participants would only become available late in Year 4, leaving limited time for analysis, manuscript preparation, and grant writing before the project end.

### Resource specification

The proposal does not include an itemized budget in the provided narrative, so resource adequacy cannot be assessed in detail. The availability of two NCI-designated comprehensive cancer centers with established registries, REDCap infrastructure, and the Wake Forest Research Information Systems Unit provides institutional resources consistent with the planned work. The proposal describes no unusual equipment, facility, or personnel needs that the described institutional setting could not meet. The absence of CVs, itemized budgets, or letters of support in the provided text limits confidence but does not, by itself, demonstrate inadequacy.

### Coordination

The two-site design requires coordination of recruitment, intervention delivery (hosted at Wake Forest), and data collection. The proposal does not describe a detailed coordination plan, data-sharing agreements, or site communication structures, though these would be expected in a full administrative application. The research narrative documents shared infrastructure (REDCap, single intervention host) that reduces coordination burden.

Management is rated good because the team expertise matches the project needs, the schedule is broadly plausible, and the institutional resources appear adequate. Confidence is moderate because the available text lacks administrative detail (budget, personnel allocation, coordination agreements) that would be needed for a complete assessment.

## Recommendation

**Current proposal insufficient for support (revise).**

The scientific direction is worthwhile: optimizing a web-based well-being intervention for young adult cancer survivors addresses an important gap, and the MOST framework is the right tool for the job. The team has relevant expertise, the measures are strong, and the preliminary work establishes that the adapted EMPOWER content is acceptable and deliverable.

However, the current plan has substantive problems that affect its central optimization goal:

1. **The component retention criterion is underspecified.** A single effect-size threshold (d ≈ 0.50) tested at α = 0.10 does not constitute a defensible decision rule, particularly when the design can detect effects as small as d = 0.31. Components with effects in the d = 0.25–0.45 range would be detectable but discarded—potentially eliminating worthwhile elements that, at low burden, could improve the assembled package.

2. **Interaction detection is underpowered.** The ability to detect only interactions of d ≥ 0.62 means that moderate but practically important synergies or antagonisms will be missed. If interactions matter—and theory and prior work suggest they might—the optimization could produce a suboptimal package.

3. **Retention assumptions are optimistic relative to the proposers' own data**, and sensitivity analyses at lower retention are not presented.

These are addressable concerns. A revised proposal could (a) articulate a formal decision framework that integrates effect magnitude, precision, burden, and mechanistic plausibility; (b) pre-specify a small number of theory-driven interactions as co-primary and adjust power accordingly, or acknowledge that interaction estimation is exploratory and adjust the optimization strategy to rely primarily on main effects with cautious assembly; and (c) model the consequences of lower retention on the selection decisions and describe a plan for mid-trial monitoring of retention with pre-specified contingencies.

The core concept and approach are sound. The weaknesses lie in the specificity of the decision framework and in assumptions whose failure would undermine the study's ability to answer its own optimization question. These are remediable through revision rather than redesign.

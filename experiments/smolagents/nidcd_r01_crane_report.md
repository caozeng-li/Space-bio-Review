# Scientific Project Review

**Proposal:** Multimodal Vestibular Perception
**Principal Investigator:** Benjamin T. Crane
**Mechanism:** R01
**Review date:** 2026-10-28
**Review type:** General scientific evaluation

---

## Overall Assessment

This proposal addresses a well-motivated question at the intersection of multisensory integration and clinical vestibular science: how humans combine visual and inertial information to perceive heading direction, when the two signals are judged to share a common cause, and whether persistent perceptual errors after unilateral vestibular loss can be reduced through adaptation. The work builds on a productive laboratory with established methods and published findings on visual-inertial heading perception. The central conceptual contribution—distinguishing explicit common-cause judgments from continuing cross-modal influence—is grounded in preliminary data and addresses a gap that existing designs have not systematically measured. The proposal spans basic psychophysics in healthy participants, parametric investigation of causal inference, perceptual adaptation protocols, and longitudinal and interventional studies in patients with unilateral vestibular loss. This breadth is both a strength and a challenge.

The strongest components are Aim I's causal-inference experiments (IA, IB) and Aim II's adaptation studies in healthy participants (IIA, IIB), which follow from demonstrated preliminary results and use well-characterized psychophysical methods. The patient-facing aims (IC, IIIA, IIIB) address important questions but encounter greater design complexity, recruitment uncertainty, and measurement challenges that weaken the inferential chain at several points. Overall, the project promises a meaningful descriptive and methodological contribution to understanding visual-inertial causal inference and perceptual adaptation, while the therapeutic implications for vestibular patients remain appropriately characterized as laying groundwork for future trials rather than establishing treatment efficacy.

## Scientific Value — B (Good)

**Confidence: B (High)**

The proposal addresses a gap with both basic and translational significance. Vestibular disorders are prevalent (Agrawal et al., 2009; Neuhauser et al., 2005) and perceptual symptoms are inadequately captured by reflex-based measures such as the VOR and VEMPs. The project's core question—whether explicit judgments of common causation between visual and inertial headings dissociate from continuing cross-modal influence—is well-formulated. The proposal states: "Preliminary observations suggest that visual motion can influence an inertial estimate even when participants explicitly report that the stimuli do not share a common cause." This dissociation, if systematically established, would constrain computational models of multisensory integration that treat causal inference and cue combination as tightly linked.

The three specific aims are appropriately structured:

- **Aim I** offers a parametric description of causal inference for visual-inertial headings across the full horizontal plane. The knowledge contribution includes establishing the discrepancy ranges for unity perception versus cross-modal attraction, the effects of temporal asynchrony, visual field size, and unilateral vestibular pathology. The proposal also promises a "unified model" integrating causal-inference effects by Year 5. This is a descriptive and conceptual contribution.

- **Aim II** tests whether heading perception adapts to sustained visual-inertial offsets or rotating virtual environments. The contribution is both mechanistic (does adaptation generalize beyond trained headings?) and methodological (establishing protocols for subsequent clinical application). The proposal explicitly positions this as groundwork: a subsequent "randomized clinical trial would be needed to test functional benefit."

- **Aim III** characterizes heading errors after unilateral vestibular loss longitudinally (IIIA) and tests whether perceptual adaptation produces durable correction in chronic patients (IIIB). The value lies in establishing the time course and possible origin of heading bias (otolith asymmetry versus rotational compensation) and providing first evidence on whether perceptual adaptation can be therapeutically directed.

A material strength is the independent measurement of visual and inertial heading estimates alongside unity judgments. Previous studies "have often examined small discrepancies near straight ahead, asked for only a single combined estimate, or measured one modality without independently measuring the other." The full 360-degree horizontal plane sampling in Aim IA extends beyond the limited ranges typical in prior work. The preliminary data showing that agreement judgments vary with absolute direction (higher at cardinal directions) adds a dimension not captured by models that treat all directions as equivalent.

The DHI monitoring in patient experiments appropriately acknowledges that the proposal's goal is mechanistic, not a claim of functional benefit. The mean DHI change of −0.8 in preliminary adaptation patients is negligible, and the proposal does not overinterpret this. The translational value is in methods and evidence for subsequent trials, not the trials themselves.

A limitation is that the unified model promised for Year 5 is underspecified. The proposal references "reliability-based integration models" in the background but does not state what form the model will take, which parameters it will estimate, or how it will be validated against the data. This reduces the attainable conceptual contribution of the integrative goal.

## Innovation — B (Good)

**Confidence: B (High)**

The proposal's most innovative feature is the simultaneous measurement of explicit common-cause judgments and separate heading estimates for each modality across the full horizontal plane. The Crane lab has already published work demonstrating that visual-inertial heading integration occurs even at large offsets (Rodriguez & Crane, 2019, *Experimental Brain Research*; Rodriguez & Crane, 2020, *Journal of Neurophysiology*). The 2020 paper (Rodriguez & Crane, *J Neurophysiol*, 123:1369–1379) reported that "inertial heading was biased toward the visual heading by 11–20° for separations of 30–120°" in eight healthy subjects and that "there was sensory integration even in conditions without reported common causation." The proposal's preliminary data in four participants substantially recapitulate this published finding.

The question, then, is what the proposed work adds beyond this already-published result. The proposal offers several extensions: (a) systematic manipulation of temporal asynchrony (50-ms steps through 200 ms), duration, and acceleration (Experiment IB); (b) manipulation of visual field size (98°, 45°, 20°, and potentially up to 200°); (c) application to patients with unilateral vestibular lesions (Experiment IC); (d) investigation of absolute heading direction effects with denser sampling if the 30° grid proves insufficient; and (e) integration of causal-inference findings with adaptation effects. These are genuine extensions rather than mere replication.

The adaptation component (Aim II) is distinct from the lab's prior aftereffect work (Crane, 2012, *Experimental Brain Research*, on limited interaction between translation and visual motion aftereffects). The proposal's adaptation protocols—sustained heading offsets and rotating virtual environments—represent a methodological contribution. The explicit test of whether adaptation generalizes beyond trained headings is well-conceived.

The longitudinal characterization of heading bias after acute unilateral loss (Aim IIIA) addresses an understudied question. The independent work by Liu et al. (2013, *PNAS*, 110:17999–18004) demonstrated perceptual heading deficits in macaques after unilateral labyrinthectomy, with elevated thresholds persisting at three months and biases toward the lesioned side. The proposal's human longitudinal design, with weekly-to-monthly assessments and complementary measures (calorics, VEMPs, vHIT, DHI), extends this animal work to the clinical population and adds the critical comparison of otolith-versus-rotation mechanisms.

The proposal's contribution is an incremental but well-justified advance rather than a paradigm shift. The central conceptual point—that cross-modal influence outlasts common-cause perception—has already been demonstrated by this group. The proposal's value is in mapping the parametric boundaries of this phenomenon, testing it in pathological cases, and connecting it to adaptation. A reviewer should not penalize the proposal for building on its own published work, but should recognize that the core finding is already in the literature.

## Rigor — C (Moderate)

**Confidence: B (High)**

### Strengths

The psychophysical methods are well-established in this laboratory. The forced-choice fitting with cumulative Gaussian functions, Monte Carlo maximum-likelihood procedures, and resampling-based confidence intervals are appropriate for estimating bias (PSE) and precision (threshold). The proposal specifies that p ≤ .01 serves as a conservative criterion. The use of interleaved staircases to reduce predictable sequences, standardized practice stimuli, and the absence of correctness feedback are methodologically sound.

The sample-size justifications, drawn from preliminary data, provide explicit power calculations (80% power, 5% Type I error). For IA: 18 participants needed, 20 planned. For IB: 8 needed, approximately 10 planned. For IIA: 13 needed under conservative assumptions, 15 planned. These are appropriately specified.

The decision to obtain visual and inertial estimates in separate blocks—because "reporting both in the same block proved confusing"—is a pragmatic choice supported by pilot experience. The use of three repetitions per block permits assessment of within-subject precision.

The proposal explicitly acknowledges potential confounds and proposes contingency plans. For example, "if 30-degree sampling misses narrow tuning or encourages expectations about agreement, visual headings close to each inertial heading will be sampled more densely while preserving full-circle coverage." For adaptation, "if necessary, the training range can be broadened rather than assuming global recalibration."

### Limitations

**1. The preliminary common-cause data overlap substantially with published work.** The proposal's preliminary data in four participants (Figure 2) closely mirror the published findings in eight participants from Rodriguez & Crane (2020, *J Neurophysiol*). The proposal would benefit from clarifying what the planned 20-participant experiment adds beyond the already-published 8-participant dataset. This is not a fatal concern—the proposal extends to parametric manipulations—but the incremental novelty within Aim IA specifically should be acknowledged.

**2. Confounded design in the adaptation experiments.** Experiment IIA uses a left/right judgment during adaptation exposures to maintain attention. However, the adaptation offset is itself a left/right discrepancy. This creates a potential confound between the attended feature (the left/right judgment) and the adapting discrepancy. Whether participants are making judgments about visual, inertial, or combined heading during adaptation is not clearly specified, and this could influence what adapts. The proposal could address this by specifying the attention task more precisely.

**3. The acute-lesion longitudinal design (IIIA) has critical uncontrolled variables.** Patients with vestibular neuritis, gentamicin treatment, and surgical lesions are pooled analytically (with separate etiological analysis promised) but differ substantially in lesion completeness, time course, and coexisting symptoms. Gentamicin and neuritis may produce incomplete lesions. The proposal acknowledges this but provides no clear criteria for lesion completeness or a minimum standard for inclusion. Without objective confirmation of lesion extent, the relationship between heading bias and residual vestibular function cannot be cleanly tested. The 50% anticipated attrition is realistic, but with an N of 16 required—implying ~32 must be recruited—the feasibility of completing the longitudinal analysis with adequate power at one year is uncertain.

**4. Weak inference for the otolith-versus-rotation mechanism.** The proposal states that "the longitudinal comparison will test whether later lesion-side heading errors accompany sway asymmetry, as expected for an otolith contribution, or follow rotational compensation with relatively little sway asymmetry." However, sway-translation bias and yaw-rotation bias are measured separately, and their covariance with heading bias is observational. A dissociation between sway asymmetry and rotational bias would be suggestive but does not establish causation. The proposal does not describe how alternative explanations (e.g., central compensation processes independent of either peripheral mechanism) would be excluded.

**5. The chronic adaptation experiment (IIIB) has limited internal validity for durability.** The design uses a fixed-order design for the two adaptation methods (early enrollees receive rotation first, later enrollees receive heading-offset first). This confounds method order with cohort, and with only ~20 participants, the design cannot efficiently separate method effects from order effects. A crossover or randomized order within-subject would strengthen inference. The "weekly sessions" tracking retention are appropriate, but the stopping rule ("adaptation stops when bias disappears or reverses") complicates the comparison of method effectiveness because the number of sessions becomes an outcome rather than a controlled variable.

**6. The DHI as a safety and outcome measure is limited.** The DHI has a floor effect in patients with mild-to-moderate dizziness (baseline mean 23 ± 14 in the preliminary sample). The proposal acknowledges that "a subsequent randomized clinical trial would be needed to test functional benefit," which is appropriate, but the DHI monitoring is then primarily a safety measure. Its sensitivity to detect worsening in patients with low baseline scores is limited.

**7. Multiple comparisons and analysis plans are underspecified.** The proposal mentions "ANOVA and t tests" for continuous estimates and p ≤ .01 as a conservative criterion, but does not detail correction for multiplicity across the numerous planned comparisons (multiple heading directions, offsets, time points, stimulus parameters, and participant groups). The risk of false positives across a five-year program with many experiments is material and should be addressed.

## Feasibility — B (Good)

**Confidence: B (High)**

The laboratory's track record, existing equipment, and institutional access support the core experimental program. The six-degree-of-freedom hexapod platform, 98° azimuth display, VR headsets, and custom multi-axis sled/rotator are already operational. The preliminary data from healthy participants and vestibular patients confirm that the key paradigms can be executed.

The recruitment plan draws on existing resources: a screened institutional database, recurring healthy participants, and patients from otolaryngology clinics. The total anticipated enrollment—approximately 120 normal controls and 30 participants with vestibulopathy over five years—appears realistic for a single-site study with these resources. Participants are reused across experiments where appropriate, which is efficient.

The schedule is logically organized, with causal-inference methods developed in Year 1 and progressively extended to parametric manipulations and patient groups. The parallel operation of mechanistic and lesion studies is feasible given the shared apparatus and the full-time technician.

Several feasibility concerns warrant attention:

- **Patient recruitment for IIIA (acute lesions).** The weekly-to-monthly follow-up schedule is demanding, especially for patients who travel long distances. The 50% anticipated attrition is acknowledged, but the absolute numbers are tight: with N=16 required and 50% attrition, approximately 32 acute patients must be recruited, of whom 16 must complete the protocol. The proposal does not estimate the catchment area's annual volume of eligible acute unilateral vestibular lesions, making it difficult to judge whether 32 recruits over the study period is realistic.

- **Patient recruitment for IIIB (chronic lesions with large bias).** The preference for participants with baseline heading deviations "above ten degrees" further narrows an already limited pool. The preliminary data show a mean deviation of 7.5 ± 3.4 degrees, suggesting that many chronic patients fall below this threshold.

- **Visual field manipulation.** The proposal plans visual fields of 98°, 45°, and 20°, with fields "up to 200 degrees" contingent on equipment availability. The wider fields are an explicit contingency, and their unavailability would not threaten the core aims, but their uncertain status should be noted.

- **The unified model (Year 5).** The proposal's ambition to "integrate causal-inference effects into a unified model" is mentioned without specification of the modeling approach, computational resources, or relevant expertise. The PI's published work is primarily experimental psychophysics; whether the team includes computational modeling capacity is not documented in the supplied text.

## Ethics and Safety — B (Good)

**Confidence: B (High)**

The proposal identifies the principal risks appropriately: motion sickness, worsening dizziness, and loss of privacy. The noninvasive nature of the testing is correctly noted.

The consent process includes standardized explanations, the right to discontinue at any time, and age-appropriate procedures for adolescents (parental consent plus assent). The DHI is administered during adaptation visits, providing a systematic screen for symptomatic worsening. Adverse events are to be documented with severity and relationship to participation and reviewed through institutional oversight.

The proposal describes several contingency responses to discomfort: "reducing exposure duration or rotational speed" and that "research participation will not prevent other clinical care." These are appropriate safeguards for a protocol that deliberately exposes participants to sensory conflict.

Data management separates identifying information from research measurements, with access limited to designated study personnel. Dissemination through scientific publications and presentations, with trial registration planned before data collection, follows standard practice.

Two points deserve comment:

- **Adolescent enrollment (ages 13–17).** The proposal includes ages 13–89 in the eligibility range but notes that recruitment "is expected to yield primarily adults aged 18–89." The adolescent component is a minor part of the plan, and the proposal provides parental consent and assent procedures. Whether the specific risks (motion sickness, worsening dizziness) are proportionately managed for adolescents is plausible but not elaborated.

- **Motion sickness in adaptation experiments.** The rotating virtual environment in Experiment IIB is acknowledged to produce motion sickness. The proposal states that exposure "at 40 degrees per second became subjectively unobtrusive after one or two minutes," but individual variation in susceptibility is substantial. The proposal's approach—monitoring, the right to discontinue, and parameter adjustment—is appropriate but the actual incidence and severity in a larger sample remain uncertain.

Overall, the ethics and safety provisions are proportionate to the risks described. No material deficiencies are evident.

## Management — C (Moderate)

**Confidence: C (Moderate)**

The proposal describes the PI overseeing all experiments with a full-time technician supporting recruitment and testing. The laboratory's existing equipment and clinical access are described. The five-year schedule allocates work across years in a logical progression.

The management description is thin in several respects that affect confidence in delivery:

- **Personnel.** A single PI and one full-time technician are described. The proposal does not specify whether graduate students, postdoctoral fellows, or clinical collaborators will contribute. The PI's other commitments are not documented. For a five-year program spanning basic psychophysics, patient recruitment, longitudinal follow-up, and computational modeling, the described personnel appears lean, particularly if the PI has clinical or administrative responsibilities beyond the project. This is not evidence of incapacity—no CV or effort documentation is provided—but it limits confidence in the management judgement.

- **Clinical coordination.** The patient-facing aims (IC, IIIA, IIIB) depend on timely identification and referral from otolaryngology clinics. The proposal mentions "patients with unilateral lesions from otolaryngology clinics" but does not describe the coordination mechanism, referral pathways, or whether clinical collaborators are formally part of the project. This is a critical dependency for Aims IC, IIIA, and IIIB that is acknowledged only in general terms.

- **Data management and analysis pipeline.** The proposal describes statistical methods but not the data management infrastructure. For a project generating large volumes of psychophysical data across multiple paradigms and participant groups, a systematic data pipeline, version control, and analysis plan are important for reproducibility. These are not described.

- **Contingency for recruitment shortfall.** The proposal acknowledges clinical variability and slower recruitment for patient experiments but does not describe specific contingency plans (e.g., extended recruitment window, multi-site collaboration, adjusted enrollment criteria) if recruitment falls below targets.

- **Integration across aims.** The proposal promises to integrate causal-inference findings into a unified model in Year 5. The mechanism for this integration—who will do it, with what tools, and how it will draw on data from all aims—is not specified. This weakens confidence that the integrative goal will be realized as described.

These management concerns are addressable and do not individually threaten feasibility, but collectively they reduce confidence that all three aims will be completed at the described scope within the five-year period.

## Recommendation: Conditional support (*conditional_support*)

The project addresses a worthwhile scientific question with established methods and promising preliminary data. The causal-inference and healthy-participant adaptation experiments (Aims I and II) are well-supported and likely to yield meaningful descriptive and methodological contributions. The patient-facing aims (IC, IIIA, IIIB) are scientifically important but the current design includes inferential weaknesses—particularly the confounded adaptation-method order in IIIB, the uncontrolled lesion-completeness variable in IIIA, and the observational nature of the otolith-versus-rotation comparison—that affect the strength of conclusions that can be drawn from these experiments.

The conditions for support are:

1. **For Aim IIIA:** Specify objective criteria for lesion completeness (e.g., canal paresis thresholds on caloric testing, vHIT gain criteria) and how incomplete lesions will be handled analytically. Provide a documented estimate of the annual volume of eligible acute unilateral vestibular patients in the catchment area to support the recruitment target.

2. **For Aim IIIB:** Adopt a design that does not confound adaptation method with enrollment cohort. A within-subject crossover with randomized order, or a parallel-group design with randomized assignment, would substantially strengthen inference about relative method effectiveness.

3. **For Aim I:** Clarify how the planned 20-participant Experiment IA extends beyond the already-published 8-participant dataset (Rodriguez & Crane, 2020). The parametric extensions (IB, IC) are clear; the incremental contribution of IA at larger N with similar methods should be explicit.

These conditions are specific and addressable. They do not require redesigning the project but rather tightening elements of the existing plan. If satisfied, the project would provide proportionate support for its stated goals and would merit support.

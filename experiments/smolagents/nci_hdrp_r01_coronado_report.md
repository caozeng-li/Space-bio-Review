**Mechanism:** R01  
**Review Date:** 28 October 2026  
**Source ID:** src-e6740c1ad73f

## Overall Assessment

The PRECISE proposal addresses a clinically and operationally important question: whether telephone-based patient navigation can improve follow-up colonoscopy completion after a positive fecal immunochemical test (FIT) in community health center populations, and whether a prediction model can efficiently target navigation to patients least likely to complete colonoscopy on their own. The question is well-motivated by the large gap between FIT completion and diagnostic follow-up in safety-net settings, where 40–50% of FIT-positive patients may fail to obtain timely colonoscopy.

The project is well-specified: a pragmatic, patient-randomized trial embedded in a real-world FQHC delivery system (Sea Mar Community Health Centers, Washington), complemented by external validation of a risk-prediction model and a formal economic evaluation. The PI and team bring deep, directly relevant experience from the STOP CRC trial and prior prediction-modeling work. The proposal's central logic—that navigation may be most cost-effective when targeted—is sound and clinically relevant.

However, the proposal as submitted contains a material tension. Its innovations include targeting navigation to patients with moderate or low predicted adherence and testing effects within these strata. These are worthwhile questions. Yet the scheme randomizes *only* the moderate- and low-adherence strata to navigation or usual care, while the high-adherence group is under surveillance only. This design can estimate the average effect of navigation in the randomized (lower-adherence) population, which is useful. But it cannot directly estimate the treatment effect in the high-adherence group, and the proposed interaction test comparing effects across strata is explicitly underpowered: the detectable interaction odds ratio at 80% power is 2.98 with the design effect and 2.17 without. This is large relative to plausible effect modification. The proposal therefore cannot confidently answer its own central question—whether navigation effects differ meaningfully by predicted adherence. This is a decisive limitation for the stated precision-health framing. The work remains valuable for estimating the overall navigation effect in a lower-adherence population and for producing cost and implementation evidence. But the stratification objective is more exploratory than confirmatory given the design.

The scientific value, feasibility, and ethical framework are strong; the innovation is moderate but real; rigor is good for the primary effectiveness question but limited for the stratification aim; and management is well-supported. The overall trade-off favors a judgement that a supportable scope exists—the effectiveness and economic evaluation in the moderate/low-adherence population—with the caveat that claims about differential targeting should be treated as hypothesis-generating.

## Scientific Value: B (Good) – Confidence: B (High)

The proposal addresses a persistent implementation gap in CRC screening: follow-through from a positive FIT to diagnostic colonoscopy. This is not a question about the efficacy of colonoscopy or FIT per se—both are established—but about the operational effectiveness of a delivery-chain intervention in a high-need population. That is a legitimate and important scientific question with direct implications for health-system resource allocation.

**What the proposal states:** The knowledge contribution is practical: (a) whether a centralized telephone navigation program increases 1-year colonoscopy completion in an FQHC, (b) at what cost and cost-effectiveness, (c) whether effects vary by predicted adherence, and (d) descriptive evidence on barriers, fidelity, and implementation from qualitative work. Each of these is a defensible contribution, though their weight differs.

**External evidence:** The subsequent published results (Coronado et al., *Annals of Internal Medicine*, 2025; NCT03925883) confirm that this study—when executed—found a 13.0 percentage-point improvement in 1-year colonoscopy completion (55.1% navigation vs. 42.1% usual care; adjusted OR 1.69; 95% CI 1.30–2.19). That is a clinically meaningful effect, and it is the largest randomized evaluation of navigation for FIT follow-up to date. The published results are outcome information and are set aside for the prospective merit judgement; they confirm the importance of the question and the feasibility of answering it, but do not independently establish the proposal's prospective quality.

**Prior work at the proposal stage:** The proposal cites Green et al. (2014), who found a non-significant 10.1 percentage-point improvement with nurse navigation in an integrated system (n=147, 91% vs. 81%, P=0.10), and Raich et al. (2012), who showed improved time to diagnostic resolution across cancer types. The proposal correctly notes that neither prior trial was large or specifically focused on FQHC FIT follow-up. The combined meta-analytic estimate from these two small trials was inconclusive (RR 1.21; 95% CI 0.92–1.60). The proposal thus targets a genuine evidence gap.

**Attainable contribution:** The primary effectiveness estimate (navigation vs. usual care in moderate/low-adherence patients) is attainable and policy-relevant. The economic evaluation from a health-plan perspective, with cost per additional colonoscopy and adenoma detected, would add useful implementation evidence. The qualitative work and fidelity assessment would provide process understanding. The stratification question—whether effects differ by predicted probability—is scientifically interesting but, as discussed under Rigor, the design provides limited inferential power for it.

**Distinction from routine optimization:** The proposal goes beyond routine quality improvement by embedding randomization, economic evaluation, and explicit risk-stratification methodology. The use of trained clinic staff rather than research-only navigators supports generalizability.

## Innovation: B (Good) – Confidence: B (High)

**What the proposal claims as innovation:** Targeting navigation to follow-up (rather than initial screening), testing effects within predicted-adherence strata, measuring cost-effectiveness across strata, and using clinic staff as navigators in a real-world delivery setting.

**Assessment:** Each element individually is an incremental rather than a transformative advance, but the combination is distinctive. At the time of the proposal (pre-2020, given the protocol publication in *Contemporary Clinical Trials*, 2019), the use of a validated prediction model to stratify patients for a navigation trial was novel. The published model-recalibration work (Petrik et al., *Journal of Medical Screening*, 2023) subsequently demonstrated that the original STOP CRC model did not transfer to Sea Mar (c-statistic dropped from 0.66 to 0.61), requiring redevelopment to a 12-variable model with bootstrap-corrected c-statistic of 0.61 in the redeveloped version and 0.58 in a further corrected estimate. This finding—that prediction models for colonoscopy adherence do not transport easily across FQHC settings—is itself a novel observation, even though it complicates the targeting premise.

**Comparison with closest prior work:** The Green et al. (2014) trial used nurse navigation in an integrated system with a high baseline completion rate (80.8% in usual care), limiting generalizability to FQHCs. The Raich et al. (2012) trial addressed diagnostic resolution broadly but not FIT follow-up specifically. Neither used risk stratification. The New Hampshire Colorectal Cancer Screening Program (Rice et al., 2017) showed large effects with navigation plus free colonoscopy, but used a non-randomized design. PRECISE advances beyond all three by combining randomization, an FQHC setting, economic evaluation, and stratification.

**Originality vs. scientific value:** The innovation lies primarily in the stratified design and the integration of prediction modeling with a pragmatic trial. Even if the stratification analysis proves underpowered (as it did in the eventual results: interaction P=0.16), the design itself represents a methodological contribution and the trial generates evidence on a question that had not been adequately tested.

## Rigor: C (Moderate) – Confidence: B (High)

The proposal's rigor is adequate for estimating the overall effectiveness of navigation in the randomized population, but has a structural limitation for the stratification aim that centrally motivates the design.

**Strengths:**

- **Randomization:** Individual-level randomization stratified by county, with allocation concealment via Sealed Envelope. This is appropriate and well-described.
- **Primary outcome:** Chart-confirmed colonoscopy within one year, with blinded chart abstraction and double adjudication. This is objective and minimizes ascertainment bias.
- **Power for main effect:** Assuming 44% completion under usual care in the moderate/low-adherence group, 924 patients, ICC 0.03, the detectable improvement is 12.9 percentage points at 80% power. This is a reasonable target given prior effect estimates.
- **Analysis plan:** Modified intention-to-treat, multilevel logistic regression with clinic as random effect, pre-specified subgroup analyses. The analytic approach is appropriate.
- **Outcome ascertainment:** EHR codes supplemented by chart abstraction for patients without coded completion, with requests to GI facilities for missing reports. The acknowledgement that EHR codes capture only about 80% of colonoscopy reports and the plan to supplement are appropriate.
- **Fidelity monitoring:** Recording of 25% of calls initially, with feedback and booster training; documentation review for timing and content. This is thorough.
- **Model validation approach:** External validation on a distinct historical Sea Mar cohort, with pre-specified criteria for acceptable discrimination (c-statistic decline <0.05), and plans for recalibration or redevelopment if needed. This is methodologically sound.

**Limitations:**

- **Underpowered interaction test:** The proposal explicitly states that the detectable interaction OR is 2.98 (or 0.34) with the design effect and 2.17 (or 0.46) without it. These are very large effects. The study is designed to detect a main effect of navigation, not to distinguish effects across strata with statistical confidence. The proposal acknowledges this but then treats the stratification question as a key deliverable (Aim 2: "Compare a centralized telephone navigation program with usual care for people at moderate or high risk of nonadherence"; Aim 3: "examine possible effect modification by predicted probability"). The tension between the epistemic goal and the design's inferential capacity is unresolved. The eventual published results confirm this: the test for treatment-effect heterogeneity was non-significant (P=0.16), with observed risk differences of 2.7, 15.5, and 14.8 percentage points across the three tertiles—an apparent pattern that the study cannot confirm. The proposal's own power calculation for the interaction should temper the stratification claims.

- **No randomization in the high-adherence group:** The highest-adherence stratum is under surveillance only. This precludes any comparison of navigation effects in that group, even descriptively with the randomized strata, because selection into strata is based on predicted probability, not randomization. The inference that high-adherence patients "do not need" navigation is an assumption the design cannot verify. The proposal's rationale—that it would be inefficient—is reasonable, but the consequence for the stated aims should be more explicitly acknowledged.

- **Prediction model performance:** The development model's bootstrap-corrected c-statistic was 0.65, which is modest. The proposal correctly plans external validation. However, a model with this discrimination has limited ability to separate patients at the boundaries between strata. The published recalibration work (Petrik et al., 2023) showed that the original model's c-statistic dropped to 0.61 (bootstrap-corrected 0.55) at Sea Mar, requiring full redevelopment. The redeveloped model achieved a bootstrap-corrected c-statistic of 0.58–0.61. This level of discrimination is adequate for population-level resource allocation (identifying broad groups) but limited for individual-level decision-making, which is consistent with the proposal's stated use. However, it also means that substantial misclassification across strata is inevitable, attenuating any true interaction effect and further reducing power to detect it.

- **Contamination risk:** Usual-care patients may receive some elements similar to navigation through existing referral coordination. The proposal plans a 10% audit of usual-care encounters, but the risk of attenuated effect estimates due to contamination is real and not fully addressed. The published trial's observed ICC of <0.0001 suggests minimal clinic-level clustering but does not speak to contamination.

- **Missing data handling:** The proposal mentions multiple imputation for missing predictors in the model but does not detail how missing primary outcome data (patients lost to follow-up) will be handled beyond the ITT framework. In the published results, 18 patients were excluded as randomized in error; this is reasonable, but the handling of patients who could not be contacted merits more explicit prospective specification.

- **Time to colonoscopy analysis:** The proportional hazards assumption is mentioned but the plan to use restricted mean survival time as the primary time-to-event metric is sensible and noted in the published analysis.

## Feasibility: B (Good) – Confidence: B (High)

The proposal is feasible in its core elements, with strong institutional partnerships and a realistic operational plan.

**What supports feasibility:**

- **Site commitment:** Sea Mar Community Health Centers is a co-investigator institution through medical director Ricardo Jimenez. The proposal documents Sea Mar's existing FIT outreach infrastructure, integrated EHR, and commitment of staff, training resources, and a clinical champion. The planning data (1,186 positive FITs annually, 9% positivity, 45% screening rate among 29,281 eligible patients) are based on actual Sea Mar performance data from July 2016–June 2017 and support the enrollment projections.
- **Team expertise:** Coronado led STOP CRC and has extensive experience with pragmatic trials in FQHC settings. The team includes statisticians (Leo), health economists, gastroenterologists (Mummadi), qualitative researchers, and implementation scientists. This covers the needed disciplines.
- **Navigation program design:** The adaptation of the New Hampshire program materials, the six-call contact sequence, the 126-minute-per-patient planning estimate (~970 hours for 462 patients), and the two-day didactic plus eight-week coaching training are all specified at a level that supports implementation planning. Bilingual navigators are planned, appropriate for the ~37% Latino population.
- **Enrollment timeline:** 18 months of enrollment with 924 randomized patients across 28 clinics (~33 patients per clinic, or ~1.8 per clinic per month) appears realistic given the annual FIT-positive volume.
- **Data infrastructure:** Secure quarterly EHR transfers, REDCap for navigation tracking, and chart abstraction procedures are described.

**Critical dependencies and risks:**

- **Prediction model transportability:** As discussed under Rigor, the model may not validate at Sea Mar. The proposal anticipates this and plans recalibration or redevelopment. The published experience (Petrik et al., 2023) confirms that redevelopment was needed. The proposal's contingency planning for this scenario is adequate.
- **Navigator turnover:** The proposal mentions navigators will be nurse practitioners or staff with a similar clinical role. The published trial experienced nine different navigators over the study period, and training was condensed during COVID-19. The proposal's plan for weekly-then-biweekly meetings and quarterly specialist consultation provides some resilience, but high turnover could affect fidelity and requires management attention.
- **COVID-19:** As a prospective review at October 2026, pandemic disruptions are historical context, not a prospective risk. The published results were affected by COVID-19-related care suspensions; future similar disruptions are not assumed.
- **Colonoscopy access:** The proposal relies on existing community gastroenterology practices (50+) rather than providing free colonoscopy. This enhances generalizability but means the navigation effect depends on community capacity. The proposal's adaptation phase (mapping local GI resources, insurance, low-cost options) addresses this.
- **Chart abstraction burden:** Supplementing EHR codes with chart audits and GI facility requests is labor-intensive but necessary for outcome completeness. The 5-year timeline allocates months 39–53 for chart abstraction, which is appropriate.

**The five-year schedule** (regulatory approval months 1–3, validation months 4–7, adaptation months 5–9, randomization months 12–14, intervention months 15–34, follow-up through month 46, analysis through month 60) is plausible, though the overlap of intervention delivery with follow-up data collection and qualitative work is ambitious and requires careful coordination.

## Ethics and Safety: A (Excellent) – Confidence: B (High)

**Ethical framework:** The proposal's ethical approach is proportionate and well-considered.

- **IRB approval:** Scheduled for months 1–3. The published trial received approval from the Kaiser Permanente Northwest IRB and the Sea Mar Research Committee, with a waiver of informed consent due to minimal risk. The proposal's description is consistent with standard practice for pragmatic trials of service-delivery interventions.
- **Risk profile:** Navigation is a low-risk support service that helps patients access clinically indicated care. It does not replace medical decision-making or alter clinical indications for colonoscopy. The risk of harm is minimal—primarily the inconvenience of telephone contacts or potential privacy concerns.
- **Verbal consent for recordings:** The proposal specifies verbal consent for recorded navigation calls (25% sampling initially, then 5%). This is appropriate for telephone-based research.
- **Data protection:** Study IDs, de-identified predictor data, secure quarterly transfers, and controlled clinical-record access are specified. The advisory board reviews de-identified interview data.
- **Vulnerable population considerations:** The population includes low-income, Latino, and Spanish-speaking patients—groups with documented disparities in CRC screening follow-up. The research addresses an equity-relevant question and includes bilingual navigators and Spanish-language interview capacity.
- **Post-trial access:** If navigation is effective, the proposal states it will be offered to usual-care and surveillance patients who have not completed colonoscopy. This is an appropriate ethical provision.
- **Community engagement:** A community advisory board including patients, clinic staff, providers, payers, and policymakers meets quarterly throughout the project. This supports accountability and relevance.

**Unresolved questions:** The proposal does not detail a data safety monitoring plan beyond mentioning that the trial "was overseen by a data and safety monitor" (from the published report). For a low-risk behavioral intervention trial, a formal DSMB may not be required, and the absence of detail is not a material concern. The waiver of informed consent is justified given the minimal-risk, pragmatic nature of the study; individual consent for a service-delivery intervention embedded in clinic workflows would bias the sample and reduce generalizability.

## Management: B (Good) – Confidence: C (Moderate)

**Strengths:**

- **Leadership structure:** Coronado as PI with Jimenez as Sea Mar medical director provides both research leadership and clinical-operational authority. The distributed expertise (navigation, gastroenterology, prediction modeling, health economics, qualitative methods, health IT) is appropriate.
- **Institutional partnership:** Sea Mar's commitment of staff, training support, tracking, and a clinical champion is documented. This is critical for a pragmatic trial embedded in clinic operations.
- **Advisory board:** Quarterly meetings with diverse stakeholders (clinic/patient representatives, GI providers, researchers, payers, policymakers) provide governance and facilitate translation.
- **Schedule with milestones:** The Gantt-style timeline identifies key activities and their temporal relationships. The five-year horizon accommodates the phased design (validation → adaptation → enrollment → follow-up → analysis).
- **Navigator supervision:** Weekly meetings for three months, then biweekly, with quarterly specialist consultation. Fidelity monitoring through call recording and EHR documentation review with feedback loops.

**Limitations and confidence issues:**

- **Staff CVs and budget detail:** The proposal narrative describes roles but does not include full CVs or an itemized budget. This is a limitation of the supplied text rather than a demonstrated deficiency. The absence of budget detail limits the ability to assess whether the economic evaluation resources (analyst time, data acquisition costs) are adequately resourced. However, the proposal narrative is sufficient to establish that the relevant expertise exists on the team.
- **Navigator staffing risk:** The proposal estimates 970 hours for 462 navigated patients (~2.1 hours per patient including outreach attempts). One centralized navigator at a time is planned, with the recognition that multiple individuals may serve in this role. Managing turnover and maintaining fidelity across navigator transitions is a practical challenge that the proposal addresses with training protocols and fidelity monitoring, but the management of this specific risk could be more explicit.
- **Coordination complexity:** The project spans model validation, materials adaptation, randomization, navigation delivery, outcome ascertainment, qualitative data collection, economic analysis, and dissemination. Managing these parallel workstreams across academic and clinical partner organizations requires strong project management, which is implied but not detailed.
- **Dissemination plan:** The proposal mentions manuscripts, a model specification guide, and decision-support tools for EHR embedding. These are appropriate outputs. A more explicit dissemination strategy (target journals, audiences, policy engagement) would strengthen the management plan but its absence does not threaten feasibility.

## Recommendation: **Conditional Support**

The proposal addresses a worthwhile question with a well-motivated design, strong institutional partnership, and an experienced team. The core contribution—estimating the effectiveness and cost of telephone navigation for colonoscopy follow-up in an FQHC population—is attainable and policy-relevant. The proposal's strengths in ethics, feasibility, and the quality of the investigative team are clear.

The recommendation is conditional rather than unqualified support because the proposal's most distinctive feature—targeting navigation based on predicted adherence risk and testing whether effects differ by stratum—is not adequately supported by the design. The interaction test is explicitly underpowered, and the exclusion of the high-adherence group from randomization precludes a full comparison. The risk-stratification framing should therefore be treated as exploratory, not confirmatory, in the study's primary deliverables.

**Conditions for a supportable scope:**

1. The primary declared contribution should be the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of navigation in the moderate/low-adherence population, with stratification analyses explicitly labeled as secondary and exploratory, consistent with their power.
2. The proposal should acknowledge that the high-adherence surveillance group provides descriptive benchmarks only, not a comparison that supports causal claims about differential targeting.
3. The prediction model's external validation and any redevelopment should be reported transparently, including calibration and discrimination metrics, with recognition that modest discrimination limits stratification precision.

With these adjustments to the framing of aims, the project offers a valuable, feasible, and ethically sound contribution to implementation science in cancer screening equity. The work would produce the largest randomized evidence base on navigation for FIT follow-up in FQHCs, with cost evidence directly relevant to resource-allocation decisions in safety-net systems.

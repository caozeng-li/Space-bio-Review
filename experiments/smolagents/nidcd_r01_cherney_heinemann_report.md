# Scientific Project Review

**Project:** Defining Trajectories of Linguistic, Cognitive-Communicative and Quality of Life Outcomes in Aphasia
**Principal Investigators:** Allen W. Heinemann and Leora R. Cherney
**Mechanism:** R01
**Review date:** 2026-10-28

---

## Overall Assessment

This is a well-conceived, large-scale prospective observational study that addresses a genuine and consequential gap in aphasia research: the absence of comprehensive, multi-domain recovery trajectories that simultaneously model linguistic, cognitive-communicative, participation, and quality-of-life outcomes alongside treatment exposure, genetic markers, and neuroimaging biomarkers. The core contribution would be an empirically grounded, integrated description of how people with post-stroke aphasia recover—or fail to recover—across impairment, activity, and participation domains over 18 months, together with internally validated models that distinguish individual variation from population tendencies. The project is ambitious in scope, methodologically sophisticated in its analytic approach (individual growth-curve modeling with bootstrap and cross-validation stability checks), and built on a team with relevant complementary expertise.

The proposal's greatest strengths are (a) the simultaneous measurement of three distinct but linked outcome domains using well-validated instruments, moving decisively beyond impairment-only designs; (b) the rigorous, prospective quantification of therapy exposure through therapist logs with reliability checks, which addresses a known limitation of billing-code-based studies; (c) the integration of candidate genetic polymorphisms, resting-state connectivity, and structural imaging within a single cohort; and (d) the explicit internal validation framework (bootstrap stability and Monte Carlo cross-validation against MCID thresholds). The weaknesses are concentrated in three areas: the limited evidence base linking the specific genetic variants to aphasia recovery (as distinct from motor recovery); the observational design's inability to support causal inferences about treatment effects despite the prominence of treatment-exposure hypotheses; and the absence of a formal sample-size justification for the explanatory models in Aim 2, particularly the gene-gene interaction component.

On balance, the proposed work is likely to produce a uniquely rich descriptive resource and exploratory predictor models that will advance the field meaningfully. The design is appropriate for its stated stage—an observational cohort study aimed at describing trajectories and identifying candidate associations—and the analytic strategy is well-matched to the longitudinal, multi-domain data structure. The proposal merits support, with the understanding that its principal contribution is descriptive and associational rather than causal or confirmatory.

**Overall recommendation:** Support

## Scientific Value: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

The project asks an important question: how do linguistic recovery, communicative participation, and quality of life co-evolve after stroke-related aphasia, and what patient, treatment, and biological characteristics are associated with different recovery patterns? This matters because aphasia rehabilitation currently operates with limited evidence about what to expect over time, for whom, and across which outcomes. The study's explicit hypothesis—that linguistic improvement plateaus earlier while participation and QoL continue to improve—is plausible, testable, and clinically consequential: if confirmed, it would argue for extending rehabilitation attention to participation-focused interventions beyond the period when impairment gains slow.

The knowledge contribution is multi-layered. Descriptively, the unconditional trajectory models (Aim 1) would provide the largest prospective characterization of aphasia recovery across three outcome domains yet published, using instruments (WAB-R, CPIB, SAQOL-39) that are well-established and recommended by the ROMA core outcome set. This alone would be a valuable resource for clinical expectation-setting, trial planning, and power calculations. Associatively, Aim 2 would generate hypotheses about which modifiable (treatment timing, intensity, duration, modality) and non-modifiable (lesion characteristics, genetic variants, connectivity patterns) factors differentiate recovery trajectories—hypotheses that could then be tested in future intervention studies or causal designs. Methodologically, the internal validation framework (Aim 3) provides a transparent assessment of model stability and predictive accuracy, distinguishing variables that appear consistently from those that capitalize on chance.

The practical contribution is the planned interactive trajectory tool, which would allow clinicians to enter patient characteristics and generate expected recovery curves. This is a sensible translational output, though its clinical utility depends on the prediction accuracy demonstrated in Aim 3. The proposal appropriately frames this as contingent on model performance rather than guaranteed.

The main limitation on scientific value is that the observational design cannot establish whether identified treatment-outcome associations reflect causal effects of therapy or confounding by indication (patients who receive more therapy may differ systematically from those who receive less). The proposal acknowledges this constraint explicitly—"treatment was not randomized" (p41)—and the knowledge contribution remains substantial even without causal claims. The descriptive resource, the identification of candidate predictors, and the trajectory characterization are independently valuable, and the study would appropriately serve as a foundation for subsequent causal or interventional work. A second consideration is that the genetic and imaging components, while innovative in combination, rest on a relatively thin evidence base for aphasia specifically (see Innovation and Rigor sections below); the incremental contribution of these biomarkers over clinical predictors alone is genuinely unknown, which is both a scientific reason to do the study and a source of uncertainty about the magnitude of the contribution.

## Innovation: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

The innovation of this project lies in its integration of elements that have typically been studied separately, rather than in any single unprecedented component. What is novel, taken together, is the simultaneous prospective measurement of (a) three outcome domains (impairment, participation, QoL) at five time points over 18 months, (b) detailed, modality-specific therapy exposure logs validated against video recordings, (c) a panel of ten candidate neuroplasticity-related SNPs analyzed for epistasis, and (d) resting-state functional and structural connectivity measures, all within a single cohort of 300–400 participants.

External evidence supports the claim that no existing study matches this scope. The published protocol (Cherney et al., 2024, *Archives of Rehabilitation Research and Clinical Translation*, identified as the protocol for this same project, R01 DC017174-01A1) states that "resulting models will provide an unparalleled representation of recovery from aphasia resulting from stroke." The systematic review by Kodani et al. (2025, *Neuropsychological Rehabilitation*) confirms that responsiveness and measurement error evidence remain scarce for aphasia outcome instruments, and that the WAB-R and SAQOL-39—both used here—are among the most-studied instruments but still lack comprehensive validation. The review by Panuccio et al. (2025, *Brain Sciences*) identifies the SAQOL-39 as one of the most-used aphasia tools, supporting its centrality but also implying that its properties in longitudinal trajectory contexts remain under-described.

The individual components each have precedents but not in combination. IGC models have been applied to spinal cord injury (Kozlowski & Heinemann, 2013; Tulsky et al., 2022) and traumatic brain injury (Hart et al., 2014) by members of this team, and the extension to aphasia represents a logical methodological transfer. The CPIB was validated for aphasia by Baylor et al. (2017, *Aphasiology*), who found minimal differential item functioning between persons with aphasia and the original calibration samples, providing support for its use here. The candidate-gene approach with BDNF, APOE, COMT, and neuroplasticity-related variants has been pursued in stroke motor recovery but is less established for aphasia; de Boer et al. (2017, *Neurorehabilitation and Neural Repair*) found no significant effect of BDNF Val66Met on aphasia recovery in 53 patients, and a recent systematic review (Moura Alves Seixas et al., 2026, *Biomedicines*) described language outcomes as "inconclusive" across 11 studies. The gene-gene interaction (epistasis) analysis using multifactor dimensionality reduction is a distinctive analytic approach that moves beyond single-SNP associations, though its application to aphasia recovery is exploratory.

The innovation is genuine but incremental rather than paradigm-shifting. The project does not introduce new measurement instruments, new imaging acquisition sequences, or new genetic targets; it combines existing methods in a more comprehensive design than has been attempted. This is a well-justified increment rather than a limitation. The distinction between originality and scientific value is clear: the value lies in the integrated description that the combination enables; the originality lies in the combination itself.

## Rigor: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

The design is generally well-suited to the study's descriptive and associational goals, with several notable strengths and a few areas where the inferential logic requires careful qualification.

**Strengths.** The multi-site design (three IRFs, expanded to four in the published protocol) enhances generalizability relative to single-center studies, though all sites are in the Midwestern US, which limits demographic and geographic representativeness. The assessment battery is comprehensive and uses instruments aligned with the ROMA core outcome set and NINDS Common Data Elements. The therapy-exposure measurement is a particular strength: therapist logs of start/stop time and target modality, validated against video recordings of 10% of sessions with a reliability criterion (90% agreement), provide substantially richer treatment data than billing codes alone. The pre-study survey of 56 SLPs at participating sites supports feasibility.

The analytic approach is methodologically sophisticated. The use of IGC models with random intercepts and slopes, model selection via AIC/BIC, and sensitivity analyses using categorical time are appropriate for the data structure. The combination of bootstrap stability assessment (500 resamples) and Monte Carlo cross-validation (500 splits) in Aim 3 provides a transparent, internal evaluation of model performance that goes well beyond reporting R² or p-values. The criterion that prediction error should not exceed the MCID for each outcome is clinically grounded, though its practical application depends on the availability of MCID estimates for the specific instruments at the relevant time points—a detail the proposal does not fully establish (see below).

The imaging analysis pipeline is well-specified, using established tools (FSL, SPM, FreeSurfer, Caret, HCP pipelines) with explicit attention to lesion-related registration challenges (lesion masks, virtual brain transplant). The use of multiple parcellation schemes (90–500 regions) and density thresholds (2–50%) for graph analysis addresses the known sensitivity of network metrics to processing choices.

**Limitations.** The most significant rigor concern is the absence of a formal sample-size or power analysis for Aim 2. The proposal acknowledges that "suitable long-term aphasia parameters are unavailable for reliable simulation-based sample-size estimation" and justifies N = 300 on feasibility and experience with mixed models. This is a reasonable approach for Aim 1 (describing unconditional trajectories), where 300 participants with five time points should support robust estimation of population-average curves and variance components. For Aim 2, however, the number of candidate predictors is large: demographics (age, sex, education, SES), clinical characteristics (lesion volume, location, comorbidities, NIHSS, initial severity), therapy variables (timing, intensity, duration, modality), ten genetic variants (plus interactions), and dozens of imaging measures (multiple graph metrics at multiple thresholds). The proposal's strategy of fitting separate conceptual-group models before combining them, and then applying backward selection, is sensible but does not eliminate the risk of overfitting when the candidate predictor space is large relative to N. The bootstrap stability assessment in Aim 3 provides some protection—variables that survive backward selection but are unstable across resamples will be identified—but does not fully compensate for the risk that the "parsimonious final model" may not replicate well.

A second concern relates to the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption for the longitudinal models. The proposal appropriately acknowledges that "dropout is especially important because it may challenge that assumption" and mentions that PROC TRAJ can model dropout. The published protocol adds that "we will create missing data patterns and include them as covariates in trajectory models." These are reasonable approaches, but the concern is that patients with more severe aphasia, greater disability, or worse outcomes may be disproportionately lost to follow-up, producing non-ignorable missingness. The proposal does not describe sensitivity analyses (e.g., pattern-mixture models or selection models) to assess the robustness of conclusions to departures from MAR.

Third, the MCID-based prediction-accuracy criterion in Aim 3 is conceptually appealing but operationally underspecified. The proposal states that "prediction errors should be no greater than the minimally clinically important difference for the relevant outcome and time point." Published MCID estimates for the WAB-R AQ, CPIB, and SAQOL-39 in longitudinal aphasia recovery contexts are limited. The scoping review by Kodani et al. (2025) specifically notes that responsiveness and measurement error evidence is rarely evaluated for these instruments. The proposal's plan to estimate sensitivity/specificity thresholds from the Global Rating of Change provides one route to establishing meaningful change, but this is a different construct from the MCID that would be used to evaluate prediction error. This does not invalidate the approach—the global rating data will itself produce useful thresholds—but it introduces uncertainty about whether the prediction-accuracy standard can be meaningfully applied at the analysis stage.

Fourth, the genetic analysis relies on a candidate-gene approach at a time when genome-wide approaches are standard for discovery. This is a pragmatic choice given sample size, and the proposal's framing of the genetic component as exploratory (integrated with other predictors rather than interpreted as independent causes) is appropriate. However, the evidence base linking these specific SNPs to aphasia recovery is thin: the de Boer et al. (2017) study found no BDNF effect on language recovery, and the Moura Alves Seixas et al. (2026) systematic review describes language outcomes as "inconclusive." The epistasis analysis with multifactor dimensionality reduction in a sample of 300 yields limited power to detect higher-order interactions, and the resulting classifications may be unstable. These are acknowledged risks of exploratory work rather than design flaws, but they temper expectations for the genetic component's contribution.

## Feasibility: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

The project appears feasible in its core operational elements, with some manageable risks.

**Strengths.** The three-site (expanded to four in the published protocol) recruitment plan is grounded in demonstrated IRF volumes. The published protocol reports that enrollment began in July 2019 and was scheduled through March 2022, with COVID-related extension through March 2024. This confirms that the consortium can identify and enroll participants at the planned rate. The team's prior work extracting billing records from 71 stroke admissions (median stay 12 days, SD 9.2) demonstrates familiarity with the clinical data environment. The 56-SLP survey establishes therapist willingness to participate in the logging protocol.

The assessment burden (3–4 hours per follow-up visit) is substantial but mitigated by the provision for split visits and home assessments. The published protocol adds a 3-month follow-up time point not mentioned in the current proposal, which would increase data density. The imaging protocol (30 minutes of scanning plus set-up) is within typical tolerability for stroke patients, and the exclusion of those with contraindications, claustrophobia, or inability to tolerate scanning is appropriate.

The five-year timeline is realistic: approximately 30 months of enrollment followed by staggered follow-up, with analysis concentrated in the final year. The schedule includes explicit contingency planning for slow recruitment (extension or additional site).

**Risks and mitigations.** Retention is the principal feasibility risk. The proposal anticipates 15% loss by 6 months and an additional 10% by 18 months (cumulative ~25%), which is optimistic for a stroke population with communication impairment. The published protocol notes that "patients with more severe linguistic and cognitive-communicative deficits may be less likely to enroll or more likely to drop out," which would produce informative dropout. The weekly phone contact during the first two months and monthly thereafter is a sensible retention strategy, but whether it achieves the projected retention rate remains to be demonstrated.

The therapy-logging protocol, while well-designed, depends on sustained clinician compliance across three sites over several years. The reliability training and 10% video verification provide quality assurance, but the day-to-day burden on SLPs to log start/stop times and modalities for every session should not be underestimated. The proposal's contingency—that sessions can be video-recorded and coded later by research assistants—is a practical fallback.

The genetic and imaging components require specialized equipment and expertise available at the lead site. The imaging subsample of 100 depends on AbilityLab enrollment alone, which should be achievable given the site's volume, but reduces the effective sample for imaging-outcome analyses. The proposal appropriately notes that participants unable to undergo MRI can still contribute core data; the imaging analyses will have lower precision than the behavioral analyses, which is an acceptable trade-off.

## Ethics and Safety: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

The ethics and safety provisions are appropriate for the study's activities and population.

The consent process using supported conversation techniques directly addresses the central ethical challenge of research with persons with aphasia: ensuring that communication impairment does not prevent understanding or voluntariness. The published protocol provides additional detail, noting that "research assistants describe the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, the types of activities, and risks. They use aphasia-friendly materials including illustrations, large print, and simplified language." This is consistent with best practice in aphasia research.

Risk identification is comprehensive: fatigue, frustration, MRI discomfort, and confidentiality. Mitigations are proportional: frequent breaks, split visits, home assessments, and MRI eligibility screening. The exclusion of participants with MRI contraindications or claustrophobia is standard.

The single-IRB model (Northwestern) with a Data Monitoring and Oversight Committee including clinical, imaging, genetic, epidemiologic, and patient/family perspectives provides appropriate independent oversight. The committee's annual review of safety, burden, progress, and data integrity is adequate for an observational study with no experimental intervention.

Data management provisions—separation of research and medical records, coded identifiers, password-protected electronic files, and restricted access—are standard and appropriate. The data-sharing plan (de-identified REDCap dataset available on request under a data-use agreement) is reasonable.

The 10% verification procedures for assessment scoring, case-report forms, and data entry provide quality assurance commensurate with the study's observational design. Adverse event reporting follows institutional requirements.

No material ethical concerns are identified. The genetic component, while exploratory, uses commercially available assays for common polymorphisms and does not return results to participants, which is appropriate given the uncertain clinical significance of these variants for aphasia recovery. The saliva collection (Oragene kits) is minimally invasive.

## Management: B (Good) — Confidence: C (Moderate)

The management assessment is based on the proposal narrative, which describes the team, resources, and schedule at a level appropriate for a research grant application but without the detailed administrative documentation (CVs, itemized budget, formal management plan) that would enable higher-confidence judgment.

**Strengths.** The co-PI team combines complementary expertise: Cherney in aphasia treatment and clinical research, Heinemann in rehabilitation outcomes and growth modeling. The published protocol adds Kozlowski (biostatistics/growth modeling), Domenighetti (genomics), Baliki (neuroimaging), and Kwasny (biostatistics) as co-authors, confirming that the project has the stated multi-disciplinary coverage. The track record is relevant: Heinemann and colleagues have published IGC applications in spinal cord injury (Kozlowski & Heinemann, 2013) and traumatic brain injury (Hart et al., 2014), and the team's earlier stroke rehabilitation therapy-intensity work (Bode et al., 2004) demonstrates familiarity with the clinical data sources.

The operational infrastructure is well-described: dedicated research assistants at each site for daily screening and assessment coordination, REDCap for case-report forms and central data checks, NIHSS certification and standardized training for consistency. The use of a single IRB and a Data Monitoring and Oversight Committee provides centralized governance.

The schedule (Table in §6) is appropriately detailed, covering site approvals, training, enrollment, follow-up assessments, data extraction and cleaning, analysis, and dissemination across five years. The staggered timing of assessments and analyses reflects the longitudinal design.

**Uncertainties.** The proposal does not provide a budget, cost breakdown, or formal staffing levels. Without this information, the adequacy of resources cannot be independently assessed. The proposal states that "the schedule allows extension or an additional site if accrual or retention is insufficient," which implies funding flexibility, but the basis for that flexibility is not documented. This is not unusual for a research narrative (as distinct from an administrative application), but it limits the confidence of the management judgement.

The coordination burden across three (later four) sites is non-trivial: consistent therapist logging, reliable assessment administration, video verification, and post-discharge follow-up all require sustained effort and communication. The proposal acknowledges this through its description of training, reliability checks, and centralized data management but does not detail a formal site-coordination plan (e.g., regular cross-site meetings, site-visit schedule, communication protocols). The team's experience with multi-site rehabilitation research mitigates this concern but does not eliminate it.

The genetic and imaging components introduce specialized technical dependencies (genotyping facility, MRI scanner access, computational infrastructure for image processing) that are concentrated at the lead site. The proposal states that these resources are available but does not describe backup arrangements if equipment downtime or personnel turnover affects these components.

## Recommendation: Support

The project addresses a well-defined, consequential gap in aphasia research—the absence of comprehensive, multi-domain recovery trajectories that integrate impairment, participation, and quality of life with treatment exposure and biomarkers. The design is appropriate to its stage as a prospective observational cohort study, the analytic methods are sophisticated and well-matched to the data, and the internal validation framework provides transparency about model performance. The team has relevant expertise, the multi-site recruitment plan is grounded in demonstrated clinical volumes, and the therapy-exposure measurement protocol represents a meaningful advance over billing-code-based approaches.

The principal limitations—the observational design's inability to support causal claims about treatment effects, the thin evidence base for the specific genetic variants in aphasia, the absence of a formal power analysis for Aim 2, and uncertainty about MCID thresholds for prediction evaluation—are acknowledged in the proposal or are intrinsic to the study's exploratory scope. They do not undermine the core contributions: a uniquely rich descriptive resource, well-characterized recovery trajectories across three outcome domains, and internally validated candidate predictor models that can inform future interventional research and clinical expectation-setting.

The project is scientifically worthwhile and methodologically sufficient as planned. No redesign or rescoping is required for support.

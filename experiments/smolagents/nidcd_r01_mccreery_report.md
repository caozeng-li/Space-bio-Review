# Scientific Project Review

**Proposal:** *Complex Listening Skills in School-Age Hard of Hearing Children*
**Principal Investigator:** Ryan W. McCreery
**Mechanism:** R01 (NIH/NIDCD)
**Review Date:** 2026-10-28

---

## Overall Assessment

This proposal addresses a clinically and educationally significant question: why do children who use hearing aids (HA) continue to struggle with speech understanding in noise even when amplification restores audibility? The PI and team bring deep, programmatic expertise in pediatric audiology, evidenced by a substantial corpus of published work directly relevant to each aim. The proposed framework—separating contributions of selective attention, working memory, language, and audibility to spectral resolution, perceptual weighting, and informational masking—is conceptually well-motivated and builds on the team's own preliminary data and the broader Ease of Language Understanding (ELU) framework.

The strengths are genuine: well-justified sample sizes with formal power calculations, thoughtful methodological refinements over prior pilot work (narrower carriers, level roving, individual frequency-band weighting in a feasible time window), a longitudinal design spanning 24 months, preregistration and open-data commitments, and a two-site recruitment strategy through established clinical research centers. The proposal also explicitly anticipates alternative outcomes (null mediation, stable rather than shifting weights, cognition as a general rather than masker-specific factor) and explains what each would mean for the framework—a mark of scientific maturity.

However, significant limitations affect the coherence of the design and the strength of inference attainable from several key comparisons. The primary concern is that the proposed design is fundamentally **correlational-observational** in its treatment of cognitive predictors, yet the theoretical narrative—and several stated hypotheses—implies causal architecture (mediation, directional pathways). Mediation analysis from cross-sectional or even two-wave longitudinal data, with correlated predictors measured at the same level of analysis, cannot distinguish the proposed directional model from plausible alternatives including reverse causation, reciprocal effects, or shared method variance. A second concern relates to the treatment of the Aim 2 frequency-band weighting experiment in children with hearing loss (Experiment 2b): presenting stimuli at a fixed 65 dB SPL through a hearing-aid simulator rather than at individually verified sensation levels risks conflating audibility with perceptual weighting, precisely the confound the experiment most needs to separate. A third concern is the candidate gene—although speculative—of the longitudinal design's capacity to detect developmental change within 24 months in the 6–12 year age window, given that the largest age-related differences in the preliminary data appear between 6-7 and 10-year-olds rather than within shorter developmental intervals.

These issues do not negate the project's value. The descriptive contribution—quantifying relationships among spectral resolution, individual frequency weighting, cognitive measures, and speech-in-noise performance in a large, well-characterized sample, with longitudinal follow-up—would itself be a significant resource for the field. But the inferential ambition to establish a developmental *explanation* that could directly guide individualized amplification exceeds what the design can deliver. The project is therefore better characterized as a major descriptive and hypothesis-generating investigation rather than a test of causal mechanisms.

## Scientific Value: B (Good) | Confidence: B (High)

The question—why children with mild-to-severe hearing loss continue to have difficulty understanding speech in noise despite amplification—is important on both scientific and practical grounds. The proposal estimates that nearly half of studied children lack optimally audible fittings, and the pilot data clearly demonstrate that aided audibility alone does not close the speech-in-noise gap. Identifying the sensory-cognitive architecture behind that gap could inform more individualized fitting strategies, cognitive training, or classroom accommodations.

The attainable knowledge is substantial in descriptive terms. The project would produce:
- Spectral-ripple discrimination thresholds linked to working memory and selective attention, with a direct manipulation of interstimulus interval to probe memory demands (Experiments 1a–1b), in both CNH and CHL.
- The first large-scale individual frequency-band importance functions for children with hearing loss, related to attention and language (Aim 2).
- Speech recognition in speech-shaped noise versus two-talker maskers, with cognitive mediators, in both groups (Aim 3).
- Two-year longitudinal trajectories linking baseline cognitive and sensory measures to later speech recognition (Experiments 1c, 2c, 3c).

The practical contribution is more prospective: the proposal states its goal as "a developmental explanation that can support future individualized amplification and rehabilitation." The descriptive resource would indeed support future work toward that goal, but the proposal itself does not include a translational component that tests an individualized intervention. This is appropriate for an R01 mechanism; the contribution is foundational rather than applied.

A notable gap is that the proposal does not directly address the mechanistic distinction it highlights in the rationale: whether poor spectral-ripple thresholds reflect sensory resolution, cognitive task demands, or both. Experiment 1b's manipulation of interstimulus interval is a clever approach to this question, but the design cannot fully separate these alternatives because the cognitive measures themselves are behavioral and subject to similar task demands. The longitudinal design (Experiment 1c) could, in principle, show that early cognitive measures predict later spectral resolution after controlling for earlier resolution, lending some support to a directional interpretation, but reciprocal effects remain plausible.

The proposal also does not include a direct comparison of the hearing-aid simulator condition (Aim 2b) with the personal-HA sound-field condition used elsewhere, which would be needed to interpret differences in weighting patterns across experiments. This limits the integration of findings across aims, though each aim remains independently informative.

## Innovation: B (Good) | Confidence: B (High)

The proposal makes several incremental but well-motivated advances beyond the team's prior work and the broader literature:

1. **Individual frequency-band importance in children with hearing loss.** Prior band-importance functions derive almost exclusively from adult normal-hearing listeners. The team's pilot reanalysis of 96 CNH CVC nonword data and the newer rapid estimation method (under 30 minutes) represent a genuine methodological advance. The Bosen et al. (2025) publication from the team—*Developmental Changes in Speech Frequency Weighting in Children*—provides independent confirmation that such weights shift with age in CNH (increasing at 2000 and 4000 Hz). The proposal extends this to CHL and adds longitudinal measurement, which is novel.

2. **Joint modeling of sensory, cognitive, and linguistic predictors.** While the ELU model has been applied to children with hearing loss (McCreery et al., 2019, *Frontiers in Neuroscience*), the current proposal adds spectral resolution and perceptual weighting as intermediate constructs, and manipulates interstimulus interval to vary cognitive load. This more differentiated framework is an advance over treating cognition as a single moderator of speech recognition.

3. **Two-talker versus speech-shaped noise comparison in CHL.** The team's prior work (McCreery et al., 2020, *JSLHR*) examined these masker types in CNH; extending to CHL and relating findings to selective attention measures is a logical next step.

The proposal is not, however, a conceptual departure. The individual constructs—spectral resolution, working memory, selective attention, vocabulary, audibility—and their pairwise associations with speech recognition are each documented in the literature, including by this group. The innovation lies in the simultaneous measurement, the developmental framing, and the methodological refinements rather than in a new theoretical mechanism.

The proposal's claim that selective attention specifically supports frequency weighting at 1–2 kHz while suppressing 4–8 kHz is novel and testable, but the preliminary evidence for this specific directional hypothesis is thin. The pilot data from five CNH children (Figure 5) is suggestive but cannot establish the attentional mechanism. The Bosen et al. (2025) findings that "none of the vocabulary and cognitive tasks predicted frequency importance weights after controlling for age" in their CNH sample of 42 children is directly relevant here. Even though that study used a slightly different task, the null cognitive-weight association after age adjustment raises questions about the attention-weighting hypothesis that the proposal should have addressed more explicitly.

## Rigor: C (Moderate) | Confidence: B (High)

### Strengths

The measurement approach is generally strong. Audiometric assessment with electroacoustic verification of aided SII at each visit is thorough. The cognitive battery (PEBL Flanker and Switcher, AWMA subtests, PPVT-IV, WASI Vocabulary) uses well-validated instruments with a deliberate strategy of minimizing audibility confounds through visual/nonverbal tasks where possible. The power calculations using simulated datasets are appropriate and transparent about detectable effect sizes. Preregistration, blinded scoring of a subset of speech responses, and open sharing of de-identified data and analysis code are commendable practices.

The proposal handles several methodological challenges explicitly: participants unable to tolerate 20 dB SL will be tested at lower SL with absolute level included in analysis; missingness and within-block variability in the weighting experiment will be examined against attention to assess selective attrition; and alternative stopping rules (71% correct) are planned if SNR50 tracks show poor reliability.

### Limitations

**Causal inference from mediation models.** The proposal repeatedly invokes mediation and path analysis to test "direct and mediated relationships," "whether spectral effects are direct, mediated, or both," and "whether audibility has an immediate association with recognition and an indirect association through language." In a cross-sectional design (Experiments 1a, 2a, 2b, 3a, 3b), mediation analysis cannot support causal claims because the temporal ordering of mediator and outcome is not established, and the standard assumptions (no unmeasured confounding of the mediator-outcome path, no treatment-mediator interaction affecting the outcome) are untestable. The longitudinal arms (Experiments 1c, 2c, 3c) partially address temporal ordering, but with only three waves and correlated growth in predictors and outcomes, directional inference remains challenging. The proposal acknowledges bidirectional relationships as an "alternative to be examined," which is appropriate, but the primary hypothesis language implies a directional model that the design cannot confirm.

**Fixed presentation level in Experiment 2b.** Presenting CVC words and nonwords at a fixed 65 dB SPL through a hearing-aid simulator risks that some frequency bands are inaudible for children with greater degrees of loss at those frequencies. The proposal states that "bands that are poorly audible or poorly resolved are predicted to contribute little to recognition," which makes audibility a confound rather than a finding. Using individually verified sensation levels for each band would better isolate perceptual weighting from audibility, though it would complicate the design. The hearing-aid simulator is appreciated for its experimental control, but the rationale for not individualizing sensation levels is not fully developed.

**Spectrotemporal versus spectral-only resolution.** The pilot data from 18 CHL used a *spectrotemporal* ripple task (Kirby et al., 2019, *Ear and Hearing*). The proposed task shifts to *spectral-only* ripple discrimination with narrowband carriers and level roving. While this addresses acknowledged limitations of the pilot task (inability to separate spectral from temporal information, no level rove), it also means the proposed task has less direct pilot validation in CHL. The proposal would benefit from a clearer discussion of how spectral-only ripple thresholds relate to the spectrotemporal thresholds from prior work and what different inferences each supports.

**Longitudinal sensitivity in a 24-month window.** The proposal states detectable standardized changes of d = .10 in three-predictor mixed models for Experiment 2c, which is statistically defensible with the planned sample sizes. However, whether meaningful developmental change in frequency weighting occurs within 24 months in this age range is uncertain. The Bosen et al. (2025) cross-sectional data show age-related differences between 6- and 13-year-olds but do not directly address within-child change over two years. If change is small or irregular, the longitudinal design risks being underpowered for its developmental questions, though it would still produce useful test-retest reliability data.

**Confidence in ratings.** My confidence is high (B) because the proposal is detailed and transparent about its methods, making the inferential limitations clearly identifiable. The limitations do not reflect hidden flaws but rather honest trade-offs between what is practically feasible and what would be methodologically ideal.

## Feasibility: A (Excellent) | Confidence: B (High)

The project is highly feasible as described. The two-site structure (Boys Town National Research Hospital and University of Iowa) provides access to established participant databases and clinical populations. The team has a demonstrated track record of recruiting and retaining children with hearing loss for longitudinal studies. The overall recruitment target of approximately 150 CNH and 200 CHL across five years, with allowance for ~10% attrition, appears realistic given the two-site catchment.

The experimental procedures are well within the team's technical capabilities. The spectral-ripple task, frequency-band filtering method, adaptive speech recognition procedures, and cognitive assessments are all established in the team's prior work. The newer rapid band-importance estimation (under 30 minutes, down from four hours) is a critical feasibility advance that makes the individual-differences design practical.

The schedule (Table 1) is aggressive but plausible. Aim 1 cross-sectional collection in years 1–2, Aim 2 in years 2–3, and Aim 3 in years 3–4, with longitudinal arms extending into years 3–5, creates a staggered workload that should be manageable. The total of 955 subject visits and 2,100 data-collection hours over five years is substantial but proportionate to an R01 with two experienced clinical research sites.

The primary feasibility risk is participant burden. Some children will participate in multiple experiments across visits spanning up to two years, with visits lasting 2–3 hours each. The proposal addresses this by reusing participants across aims where feasible and by noting that correlated ear weights in Experiment 2b could reduce testing to the better ear. The exclusion of children with secondary disabilities, while scientifically motivated, further restricts an already selective population. Whether families of children who meet all criteria will commit to the full longitudinal protocol is not addressed in detail but is consistent with the team's demonstrated retention in prior studies.

## Ethics and Safety: B (Good) | Confidence: B (High)

The proposal describes appropriate protections: consent and assent procedures, hardware and software limits on auditory signal levels, probe-microphone discomfort management, and institutional review under a single-IRB arrangement. The study is characterized as minimal risk, which is reasonable given that stimuli are presented at conversational levels (65 dB SPL) and at 20 dB SL for headphone-delivered stimuli. Clinically relevant findings may be shared with the child's care or education team with caregiver permission—a responsible practice.

Data management is well-specified: access controls, password-protected storage, de-identified data sharing through the Open Science Framework with reidentification restrictions, and code release through GitHub. Published reports will not identify individual children.

The exclusion of children with secondary disabilities is justified on scientific grounds (reducing heterogeneity from mechanisms unrelated to the research question) but has an equity implication: the findings may not generalize to the substantial proportion of children with hearing loss who have additional conditions. This is not an ethical violation—targeted sampling is standard in mechanistic research—but it should temper claims about clinical applicability to the broader pediatric hearing-loss population.

The proposal does not explicitly discuss whether children receive compensation for their time, nor does it address the potential for fatigue or frustration during extended testing sessions. These are minor omissions that an IRB would address but do not raise ethical concerns given the team's pediatric testing experience. No specific concerns about data security, participant welfare, or community impact are evident.

## Management: B (Good) | Confidence: C (Moderate)

The PI, Ryan W. McCreery, has an established research program in pediatric audiology with consistent NIH funding and productive collaborations across the two proposed sites. The proposal references co-investigators with relevant expertise, though without full CVs in the supplied material, the review must rely on the track record evident in the preliminary work and the publications returned by literature searches. That track record is strong: the team has published together on the core constructs of this proposal in *Ear and Hearing*, *Frontiers in Neuroscience*, *JSLHR*, and other respected venues.

The two-site structure with shared measures and protocols is a strength, providing recruitment capacity and generalizability. The division of recruitment (approximately half from each site) and the use of a single IRB simplify coordination.

The management section is brief by the standards of a full administrative application. No itemized budget, personnel plan, or detailed timeline with milestones is included in the supplied text. This limits confidence in the management rating, but the absence of such detail in the review material does not constitute evidence of inadequate management capacity. The schedule table and the logic of staggered aims provide a sufficient operational outline.

The primary management consideration is the coordination burden of nine experiments across two sites with overlapping participant pools. The proposal's strategy of shared measures and participant reuse is efficient but requires careful tracking of which children have completed which experiments at which timepoints. The team's experience with multi-site longitudinal studies mitigates this concern, though the supplied text does not detail the data-management infrastructure beyond analysis code and data sharing.

## Overall Recommendation: **Conditional Support (conditional_support)**

The project addresses an important, clinically relevant question with strong preliminary data, an experienced team, and generally well-specified methods. The descriptive contribution—a richly characterized dataset relating spectral resolution, individual frequency-band weighting, cognitive and linguistic measures, and speech recognition in multiple noise conditions, with two-year longitudinal follow-up, in both CNH and CHL—would be a significant resource for pediatric audiology.

Support is conditional on the project's inferential claims being recalibrated to match the design's capabilities. Specifically:

1. **The language of causal mediation should be replaced with a more conservative framing** appropriate to observational, largely cross-sectional data. The analyses remain valuable as tests of conditional associations and as hypothesis generation; path diagrams can describe patterns of association without implying that the design confirms directional pathways. The longitudinal arms add temporal information but do not, with three waves and correlated growth, resolve causal direction.

2. **Experiment 2b should explicitly address the audibility confound** in its fixed-level design. At minimum, the proposal should commit to reporting sensation level for each frequency band for each participant and to conducting sensitivity analyses that distinguish inaudibility from low perceptual weight. If feasible within the project, an individualized sensation-level condition or a subgroup analysis restricted to bands with verified audibility would strengthen inference substantially.

3. **The developmental claims should be tempered** in light of the limited 24-month window and the cross-sectional nature of the primary age-comparison evidence. The longitudinal data will be informative about stability versus change but may not capture the full developmental trajectory from early school age to adolescence.

These conditions do not require redesigning the experiments but rather adjusting the interpretive framework and adding specified sensitivity analyses. They are achievable within the proposed scope. If these adjustments are made, the project represents a worthwhile and supportable contribution. Without them, the gap between the causal-developmental framing and the observational-correlational design risks overclaiming, which would undermine the project's scientific value despite its genuine descriptive strengths.

# Scientific Project Review
**Proposal:** *Addressing Ethical Challenges in Networked Biorepositories*
**Principal Investigators:** Kyle B. Brothers (University of Louisville) and Aaron Goldenberg (Case Western Reserve University)
**Mechanism:** NIH R01, four-year mixed-methods study
**Review date:** 2026-10-28

---

## Overall Assessment

This proposal addresses a genuine and increasingly pressing question: as biorepositories become networked—combining biospecimens, genotyping data, and health-related data across multiple institutions—what distinctive ethical and regulatory challenges arise, and how should they be governed? The investigators bring substantial relevant experience, and the mixed-methods design combining a retrospective inventory, stakeholder interviews, longitudinal participant observation in five contrasting networks, and normative analysis with stakeholder refinement is well-conceived in outline. The five case-study networks (eMERGE, Kentucky Cancer Registry, NBSTRN, Sage Bionetworks–Synapse, Mayo Clinic Biobank) provide worthwhile variation in maturity, configuration, and domain.

However, the proposal suffers from a significant structural limitation: the observational component, while methodologically ambitious and intellectually attractive, lacks sufficient specification of how observational data will be systematically transformed into analyzable evidence that can support the normative recommendations promised in Aim 3. The logic connecting observation of meetings to identification of "challenges" and then to "recommendations" relies on interpretive steps that are described only in general terms. This does not invalidate the project, but it does mean that the central inferential chain—from what is observed to what should be recommended—carries risks that the present plan does not fully address. The project remains a worthwhile direction with a capable team. The issues identified are substantive but addressable within the proposed framework. The overall judgement is **conditional support**, contingent on strengthening the analytic pathway from observational data to normative outputs.

## Scientific Value: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

### Contribution
The proposal aims to produce three categories of knowledge: (1) a systematic characterization of the organizational configurations of US networked biorepositories and the ethical and regulatory challenges they encounter (descriptive contribution); (2) an account of how governance, oversight, and problem-solving unfold in practice, including how organizational context shapes deliberation and choice of solutions (processual/mechanistic contribution); and (3) domain-specific, generalizable recommendations for consent, privacy, security, access, governance, and oversight, refined through stakeholder engagement (normative/practical contribution).

The question is well-motivated. Precision medicine depends on large, diverse cohorts that no single institution can assemble. Networking existing repositories is an efficient strategy but creates a structured tension: ethically permissible practices at individual sites—different consent scopes, different donor control mechanisms, different return-of-results policies—must be reconciled at the network level. Prior work, including the investigators' own 2013 national survey (Henderson et al., *Genome Medicine* 2013;5:3) and IRB policy study (Goldenberg et al., *BMC Medical Ethics* 2015;16:32), established that variation exists and that 16% of surveyed US biobanks described themselves as formal or informal networks. The 2024 publication by Cadigan, Ponsaran, Rich, Timmons, Brothers, and Goldenberg (*AJOB Empirical Bioethics* 2024;16:42-51), which reports 38 interviews with representatives of 31 networked biobanks as part of "a larger study on the ethical, legal, and social implications (ELSI) of networked biobanking," appears to be an outcome of this same funded project. This is noted for completeness; the review judges the proposal as prospective and does not use that subsequent publication as evidence of independent prior work.

The translational aim—formulating practicable governance recommendations—addresses a gap identified across the literature. Existing work has examined individual biobank governance, pan-European networking under BBMRI-ERIC (Argudo-Portal and Domènech, *Life Sciences, Society and Policy* 2020;16:9), and the EHDS framework (Grech and Pace, *Frontiers in Genetics* 2026), but the US landscape of networked biorepositories, with its distinctive regulatory environment including the 2018 Common Rule revisions introducing broad consent, has not been systematically studied from an empirical ethics perspective. The proposed integration of empirical observation with normative analysis represents a potentially valuable methodological contribution to ELSI research practice.

### Limitations
The contribution is primarily descriptive and normative rather than causal. The project does not test hypotheses about what governance arrangements produce better outcomes; it characterizes, interprets, and recommends. This is appropriate for the research type and stage, but it bounds the likely impact. The recommendations will be generalizable only to the extent that the five cases capture relevant variation in US networked biorepositories. The proposal rightly acknowledges that findings "may justify replacing or adding cases," but the space to do so within the four-year timeline is limited. The exclusion of direct donor perspectives—explicitly deferred to "subsequent work"—means that the normative recommendations will rest on stakeholder and expert views, which may or may not align with donor preferences. This is a conscious scope limitation, not a flaw, but it does constrain the contribution.

## Innovation: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

### What the project adds
The project's originality lies in three design choices. First, it studies networked biorepositories as an organizational form, rather than treating biobanks as isolated entities. The distinction between locally held specimens with centralized data access, federated models, and cloud-based sharing architectures is a genuine conceptual contribution that structures the empirical work. Second, it combines retrospective characterization with prospective, longitudinal participant observation, which is unusual in ELSI research. Most empirical ethics studies rely on interviews, surveys, or document analysis; the addition of embedded observation of governance deliberations as they unfold provides access to processes that retrospective accounts cannot reliably capture. Third, the project deliberately includes developing and abandoned networking efforts alongside mature networks, avoiding the survivorship bias that would come from studying only successful organizations.

### Relationship to prior work
The investigators' prior work (the national biobank survey, the CTSA IRB policy study, and work in eMERGE, NBSTRN, and pediatric biobank consent) provides the methodological and substantive foundation. The proposal extends that work rather than replicating it. The 2024 Cadigan et al. publication on stewardship, funding, utilization, and sustainability in networked biobanking demonstrates one product of this lineage; the current proposal's broader scope across six ethical domains and inclusion of observational methods goes beyond that published analysis.

Independent of this team, the broader literature on biobank governance has advanced considerably. The BBMRI-ERIC experience in Europe (Argudo-Portal and Domènech 2020), the EHDS governance architecture (Grech and Pace 2026), the GA4GH standards framework (Rehm et al., *Cell Genomics* 2021), and empirical work on data governance challenges across research and clinical care (Kim et al., *Health Expectations* 2026) all address related questions. The present proposal's contribution is distinctive in its focus on US organizational arrangements, its combination of observational and normative methods, and its attention to the process by which networks deliberate about ethical challenges rather than only the content of resulting policies.

### Priority claims
The proposal does not make inflated priority claims. It positions itself as addressing an understudied organizational form and method. This is appropriately modest and supported by the literature.

## Rigor: C (Moderate) — Confidence: B (High)

### Strengths
The retrospective inventory and interview component (Aim 1) is well-specified. The search strategy, combining adapted Boolean searches of PubMed and NIH RePORTER, keyword searches of institutional websites, and snowball sampling from the earlier national biobank study, is systematic and appropriately broad given inconsistent terminology. The target of up to 100 interviews across years 1–3, with up to five stakeholders per network, provides reasonable breadth. The deliberate inclusion of "less powerful or dissenting stakeholders" is a thoughtful design feature that addresses a known weakness of leadership-referred sampling in organizational research.

The Framework Analysis approach is appropriate for applied qualitative research with policy implications. The use of two coders at different sites, independent coding of the same ten interviews, and a threshold of 90% agreement before distributing remaining interviews is a defensible quality-assurance strategy, though 90% is unusually high and may be difficult to achieve in practice without definitional narrowing of codes.

The inclusion of primary documents (consent documents, data-use agreements, IRB protocols) and their coding in the same thematic framework as interviews and observations is a strong design feature that enables triangulation between stated policies, reported experiences, and observed deliberations.

### Limitations
The principal rigor concern is the analytic pathway in Aim 2 (longitudinal participant observation). The proposal describes *what* will be observed—consortium meetings, advisory groups, conference calls, governance bodies, with twice-annual in-person visits and ongoing remote observation—and *how* field notes will be generated and reflexively processed (biweekly team discussions of observer influence). It does not, however, specify how observational data will be systematically analyzed to support the identification of "challenges" and their translation into recommendations. The proposal states that coded interviews, documents, and field notes will be reviewed "to identify recurrent challenges," but the method for moving from field-note narratives to coded themes to challenge identification is described at a level of generality that leaves substantial interpretive discretion. This matters because the observational data are meant to reveal *how* stakeholders identify and deliberate about ethical issues—a process claim that requires more than cataloguing the topics discussed.

The biweekly reflexive discussions, while valuable for transparency about observer effects, do not themselves constitute an analytic method. The proposal would benefit from specifying: (a) how field notes will be structured (e.g., template for recording setting, participants, topics, decisions, tensions), (b) whether a preliminary coding framework derived from Aim 1 will guide observation or whether observation will be more open-ended, (c) how observations will be compared across networks, and (d) how discrepancies between stated policies (from documents), reported practices (from interviews), and observed deliberations (from field notes) will be handled analytically.

The normative integration in Aim 3 is described as a process—identify challenges, review conceptual and legal literature, assess candidate solutions, draft reports, test with focus groups, refine through symposium—but the normative framework itself is not specified. The proposal mentions "ethical/legal standards" and "compatibility with ethical/legal standards, costs and practical trade-offs, and usefulness across different network configurations" as evaluation criteria, but which ethical principles or theories will guide assessment is left open. This is not uncommon in empirical ethics research, where normative commitments may emerge from the data, but it introduces uncertainty about how disagreements among the normative core will be resolved and how recommendations will be justified when empirical findings and ethical principles pull in different directions.

The focus-group component (up to ten sessions, each devoted to one report) is appropriately designed with attention to power differences, but the sampling frame for focus-group participants is not specified. The proposal states "purposive recruitment will select stakeholders with practical experience in that domain," but the relationship between focus-group composition and the representativeness of feedback is not addressed.

The expert symposium, while a legitimate dissemination and refinement mechanism, is a relatively thin method for testing recommendations. Large- and small-group discussions at a workshop-style event can surface concerns but cannot substitute for systematic evaluation.

## Feasibility: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

### Access and relationships
The five case-study networks have agreed to observation. This is a critical dependency, and the proposal documents it credibly. The team's existing relationships with eMERGE, NBSTRN, Mayo Clinic Biobank, Kentucky Cancer Registry, and Sage Bionetworks are a material asset. The advisory committee includes representatives from each network (Janet Olson for Mayo, Rex Chisholm for eMERGE, Michael Watson for NBSTRN, Thomas Tucker for Kentucky Cancer Registry, Megan Dorr for Sage), which provides both access and accountability. The existing relationships reduce but do not eliminate the risk that observed deliberations will be constrained by participants' awareness of researcher presence. The biweekly reflexive discussions are a reasonable mitigation.

### Team expertise
The team is well-credentialed for the proposed work. Brothers and Goldenberg have complementary expertise in bioethics, qualitative methods, and biobank governance. Jean Cadigan contributes ethnographic methods and experience from the earlier biobank inventory. Richard Sharp provides normative analysis and biobank experience. Susanne Rivera contributes regulatory and IRB expertise. Mark Rothstein contributes legal and privacy analysis. Heather Harrell contributes document and field analysis. The division of responsibilities in Table 5 is clear. The normative core (philosophical, legal/regulatory specialists) contributes throughout instrument development and interpretation, which is appropriate for integrated empirical-normative work.

### Methods
The retrospective inventory and interviews are well within the team's demonstrated capabilities. The observational component is more ambitious; the proposal acknowledges the challenge by assigning two primary contacts per case and arranging substitutes when needed. The four-year timeline (Table 7) is plausible, with overlapping aims and analysis beginning mid-project. The schedule allows for two years of observation (Q3–Q14) and approximately one year for focus groups, symposium, and finalization.

### Critical dependencies
Several dependencies are worth noting: (1) Sustained access to network deliberations over two years—if a network undergoes leadership change or reorganization, access could be disrupted. The proposal's provision for replacing or adding cases provides some flexibility. (2) Interview recruitment, particularly of "less powerful or dissenting stakeholders"—the snowball approach may not reliably identify these individuals without active effort beyond leadership referrals. (3) The quality of observational data depends on investigators' ability to attend relevant meetings; the twice-annual in-person visits are a minimum, and the proposal relies on remote observation for continuity. (4) The normative integration depends on a functioning process of deliberation within the normative core; the proposal documents the composition but not the decision-making procedure.

These dependencies are manageable within a four-year R01, particularly given the team's experience and existing relationships.

## Ethics and Safety: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

### Participant protection
The proposal addresses the standard protections for interview and focus-group research: information provision, consent, ability to decline questions or withdraw, and confidentiality through study identifiers or pseudonyms. Network names will generally be withheld in publications unless permission is obtained. Access to recordings, transcripts, and linkage information is restricted to the research team; paper records are locked and computer files password-protected. Original recordings will be destroyed after transcription and coding.

### Observational ethics
The ethical management of participant observation is more complex, and the proposal acknowledges this honestly. Explicit individual consent "may not be practicable for every transient participant," so the approach relies on transparency, repeated reminders, and appropriate authorization coordinated with network leadership and the study IRB. This is a reasonable accommodation given the nature of the observation (meetings, calls, governance bodies with changing participants) and is consistent with established practice in ethnographic and organizational research. The biweekly reflexive discussions about observer influence are an ethical as well as methodological practice.

### Risk assessment
Potential harms include discomfort, professional repercussions, and indirect identification. The use of pseudonyms and withholding of network names addresses identification risk. The focus-group design with attention to power differences addresses the risk that participants will self-censor. The exclusion of specimen donors from the study population removes risks associated with vulnerable research participants.

### Limitations
The proposal does not discuss how observation will handle moments when investigators witness ethically problematic practices or decisions. If, for example, a network makes a decision that the observing investigator believes places donor privacy at risk, the proposal does not specify whether and how the investigator should intervene. This is a genuine ethical tension in embedded research: the investigator's dual role as observer and ethics expert creates obligations that are not fully addressed by the standard confidentiality framework. This is not a decisive concern—it is a tension inherent to the method—but the proposal would be strengthened by acknowledging it and outlining a response protocol.

The proposal does not address data management for the large volume of field notes, transcripts, and documents that will be generated across two sites, though the use of a shared ATLAS.ti project and secure digital workspace implies adequate infrastructure.

## Management: B (Good) — Confidence: B (High)

### Leadership and coordination
Brothers and Goldenberg share oversight with clear division of operational responsibilities (Table 5). Goldenberg leads inventory, interviews, and focus groups; Brothers leads document analysis, observation, and policy development. This division appears balanced and plays to each PI's strengths. The co-investigator roles are defined. The advisory committee (Table 6) combines ELSI scholars (Henderson, Juengst), genomic researchers (Haines), and network representatives, providing both scientific guidance and practical access.

### Schedule
The four-year schedule (Table 7) is logically sequenced: inventory and interviews begin early (Q1–Q8 and Q3–Q10), observation runs for most of the project (Q3–Q14), analysis overlaps with data collection (Q7–Q12), and translation activities occupy the later phases (Q9–Q16). The overlap between data collection and analysis is appropriate for qualitative research and allows emerging findings to inform ongoing work.

### Resource adequacy
The proposal does not include a detailed budget, which is typical for the review format. The personnel described—two PIs, five co-investigators, research coordinators at each site, and an advisory committee—is appropriate for the scope. The use of two lead sites (Louisville and Case Western Reserve) with distributed analysis responsibilities is a strength that reduces single-institution dependency. The proposal does not specify how many research coordinators or students will support the work, which limits assessment of whether the observation schedule (two primary contacts per network, twice-annual visits, ongoing remote observation across five networks) is adequately staffed.

### Dissemination
The dissemination plan includes scholarly publications, conference presentations, direct engagement with networks (calls or webinars), and partnerships with relevant organizations and agencies. This is appropriate for the translational aim.

## Recommendation: Conditional Support

The project addresses an important question with a well-motivated design and a capable team. The descriptive contributions (characterizing network configurations and identifying ethical challenges) are well-supported by the proposed methods and are likely to be realized. The normative contribution (formulating generalizable governance recommendations) is the more ambitious and higher-risk element, and its success depends on the analytic pathway from observational data to normative output, which is currently underspecified.

The conditions for support are:

1. **Specify the analytic method for observational data.** Before initiating Aim 2, the team should articulate how field-note data will be structured, coded, and analyzed to support challenge identification and recommendation development. This should include a plan for systematic comparison across the five networks and for handling discrepancies between documented policies, interview accounts, and observed behavior.

2. **Articulate the normative framework.** The criteria by which candidate governance solutions will be evaluated—which ethical principles, which legal standards, which practical considerations, and how these are weighted—should be specified before the normative core begins its deliberative work. This need not be a rigid framework, but it should provide sufficient structure that the basis for recommendations is transparent and contestable.

3. **Develop a protocol for observer response to witnessed ethical concerns.** The team should establish, in consultation with their IRB and advisory committee, a protocol for situations in which investigators observe practices or decisions that raise material ethical concerns.

These conditions are addressable within the proposed timeline and resources and do not require redesign of the project's core activities. Satisfying them would substantially increase confidence that the observational and normative components can deliver on their stated aims.

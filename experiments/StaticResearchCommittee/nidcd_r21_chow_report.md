# Project review

## Overall assessment

This R21 proposal investigates brain activity during continuous narrative speech in children with persistent stuttering (pCWS), children who have recovered from stuttering (rCWS), and non-stuttering controls (CWNS). The central question is whether persistence is associated with anomalous activity in left-hemisphere speech-motor circuits, while recovery involves normalization of left-hemisphere processing and potentially greater right-frontal compensatory involvement. The work aims to distinguish sustained “trait” activity during fluent speech from transient “state” activity during individual stuttering moments using a mixed block/event general linear model applied to sICA-denoised fMRI data. The most valuable expected knowledge is the functional characterization of recovered children’s speech networks—a domain where structural evidence of compensatory or restorative processes exists (increased white matter volume in motor projection fibers, corpus callosum, and cerebellar peduncles) but corresponding functional evidence during overt speech is absent. Regardless of whether the findings confirm predictions from adult studies or reveal unexpected developmental patterns, the results would provide the first functional neuroimaging evidence bearing on the mechanisms of childhood recovery from stuttering.

The investigation rests on a well-validated technical foundation: the team’s sICA denoising method has been shown to recover continuous-speech activity comparable to PET in adults and has been successfully applied in the target age group (Chow et al., 2023), with preliminary data from an eight-year-old child confirming the feasibility of the storytelling paradigm. The use of a longitudinal cohort with established persistence and recovery classifications provides a strong ascertainment advantage. Access to the required scanner, equipment, and the combined expertise in fMRI methods, childhood stuttering, and speech analysis are well documented. These strengths are offset by decisive limitations in the experimental design. First, Aim 1a’s comparison of persistent CWS against controls on fluent speech activity has been substantially addressed by the team’s own published work reporting reduced left ventral premotor cortex activation in CWS during spontaneous speech. Second, the mixed block/event GLM for Aim 1b faces unresolved collinearity between spontaneous stuttering events and sustained speech blocks; the proposal does not provide design diagnostics to support the claim that these components are reliably separable, and the team’s prior work explicitly noted that insufficient events and limited statistical power prevented analysis of stuttering-moment activity. Third, Aim 2’s cross-sectional recovery comparison can characterize current group differences but—as the proposal acknowledges—cannot distinguish neural features that enabled recovery from those that are consequences of recovery or of divergent developmental trajectories. Power calculations rely on adult-derived effect sizes that may overstate sensitivity for the likely subtler recovery effects, and recruiting 20 recovered children from the existing cohort may prove difficult given published recovery rates in this cohort.

Weighing these considerations, the proposal asks an important question—what functional neural markers distinguish persistent stuttering from recovery in childhood?—that addresses a genuine gap at the intersection of the structural neuroanatomy of stuttering recovery and adult functional neuroimaging. The three-group design with a mixed block/event approach is a logical extension of the team’s prior work and would yield a useful functional characterization of recovered children’s speech networks. However, three limitations materially weaken the link between the planned comparisons and the inferences promised. Aim 1a’s core finding has been substantially pre-empted by the team’s own publication; the collinearity between spontaneous stuttering events and sustained speech blocks in Aim 1b is unresolved; and the cross-sectional recovery comparison cannot establish the developmental processes it aims to illuminate. Given that two of the three aims (1a and 1b) face significant inferential constraints, the project in its current form would provide a useful but incomplete first characterization rather than a decisive test of the hypotheses. The recommended judgement is that the proposal identifies a worthwhile direction and the team is the right one to pursue it, but the current plan requires revision to the design and framing before it can support the central claims with sufficient confidence.

**Overall conclusion: The current proposal does not yet justify support**

## Scientific value

**Rating: B; Confidence: B**

**The project addresses a clearly defined and clinically important gap: no published fMRI study has examined brain activity during overt continuous narrative speech in children while simultaneously comparing persistent stuttering, recovered stuttering, and non-stuttering control groups.** The question sits at the intersection of two established literatures—the structural neuroanatomy of childhood stuttering recovery and the functional neuroimaging of adult stuttering—where direct functional evidence in children is absent. The team’s own longitudinal cohort has provided structural findings that motivate but do not test the functional hypotheses: recovered children show increased white matter volume in motor projection fibres, left arcuate fasciculus, corpus callosum, and cerebellar peduncles, suggesting normalisation or compensation of earlier structural deficits [1]. Cortical thickness findings further indicate that children who would later persist show specifically reduced thickness in left ventral motor and premotor areas [2]. The proposal would supply the missing functional evidence.

**The most valuable attainable knowledge comes from Aim 2, which compares recovered children with both persistent and control groups.** Two competing hypotheses are articulated: normalisation of left-hemisphere processing versus compensatory right-hemisphere activity [3]. The cross-sectional comparison cannot establish which children would have shown which pattern before recovery—a limitation the proposal acknowledges [3]—but characterising the functional state of recovered children’s speech networks would provide the first functional neuroimaging evidence bearing on the mechanisms of recovery. Whether the findings confirm adult-derived predictions of reduced left perisylvian activity and greater right-frontal involvement, or reveal unexpected developmental patterns, the results would constitute a meaningful advance. This knowledge could inform whether interventions should target left-hemisphere speech-motor circuits, right-hemisphere compensatory pathways, or both.

Aim 1a (pCWS versus CWNS during fluent speech) can test whether the reduced left perisylvian activity reported in adults who stutter during continuous speech is already present in children. A positive finding would strengthen the case for a trait-like functional marker of persistence detectable in childhood; a negative finding would usefully constrain the developmental timeline. Aim 1b (stuttered versus fluent speech within pCWS) addresses a longstanding difficulty in paediatric stuttering research: the Chow et al. (2023) study explicitly noted that stuttering events could not be analysed because most children produced too few events in the scanner [4]. The proposal’s requirement of greater than 4.4% stuttering during quiet speech assessment, the use of storytelling expected to elicit approximately 300 syllables, and the event-regressor approach make a genuine attempt to address this limitation [3]. If sufficient events are obtained, the within-subject contrast could reveal whether childhood stuttering moments engage the same SMA, ventral premotor, and basal ganglia circuits reported in adults [3], or whether children show a different pattern—either finding would be informative.

**Three considerations temper the attainable knowledge.** First, Aim 1a’s core comparison has been substantially addressed by the team’s own published work (Chow et al., 2023), which found reduced left ventral premotor cortex activation in 22 CWS versus 18 controls during spontaneous speech [4]. The proposal adds a narrative storytelling task and a slightly larger sample (20 per group), but the incremental contribution of this aim over published work is modest. Second, the cross-sectional design for Aim 2 means the study characterises current group differences rather than the developmental processes leading to recovery; this produces a useful first functional description but cannot distinguish neural features that enabled recovery from those that are consequences of recovery. Third, the power calculations rely on adult-derived effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 0.7 for between-group, 0.58 for within-stuttering comparisons) that may overstate sensitivity for the likely subtler recovery effects in children [3]. These constraints do not negate the project’s value—a well-executed study would still yield a useful functional characterisation of recovered children’s speech networks where none currently exists—but they place the attainable knowledge at the level of an informative first characterisation rather than a decisive test of the recovery hypotheses.

## Innovation

**Rating: B; Confidence: B**

**The proposal’s most original contribution is the inclusion of a recovered-children group (rCWS) for functional neuroimaging during continuous narrative speech—a comparison no published fMRI study has yet reported.** The structural neuroimaging literature provides evidence that recovery involves both normalization and compensation in white matter morphology—increased volume in motor projection fibres, left arcuate fasciculus, corpus callosum, and cerebellar peduncles—but corresponding functional evidence during overt speech is absent [1]. The Neef and Chang (2024) review identifies this as a critical gap: “neural recovery patterns may give us insights into the neural basis of fluent speech production and its pathologies,” noting that existing recovery evidence is primarily structural rather than functional during speech [5]. By adding rCWS to the persistent and control groups, the proposal would provide the first functional characterization of how recovered children’s speech networks operate during continuous speech, addressing whether they resemble those of persistently stuttering children, non-stuttering children, or neither.

**The proposal further adds a mixed block/event GLM that explicitly models intermittent stuttering events on top of sustained fluent speech within the same children.** This trait-versus-state distinction has been formalized in the adult meta-analytic literature: Belyk et al. (2014) demonstrated through activation likelihood estimation that trait stuttering (fluent speech in people who stutter versus controls) and state stuttering (stuttered versus fluent speech within the same individuals) engage distinct neural patterns, with trait stuttering showing rightward lateralization and state stuttering involving motor overactivation [6]. All studies contributing to that meta-analysis examined adults. The present proposal extends this framework to childhood, the period when stuttering emerges and when most recovery occurs. The team’s prior work modelled stuttering events only as a nuisance covariate, explicitly noting that “the majority of the participants produced relatively few stuttering moments (\<10) in the scanner” and that “the statistical power for analyzing stuttering-related activity may not be sufficient” [4]. The current proposal’s requirement of more than 4.4% stuttering during quiet speech assessment, the narrative storytelling paradigm expected to elicit approximately 300 syllables, and the event-regressor approach represent a deliberate attempt to overcome this limitation [3].

**These novel elements must be weighed against what the proposal does not add beyond existing work.** Aim 1a’s comparison of persistent CWS against controls on fluent speech activity overlaps substantially with the team’s published finding of reduced left ventral premotor cortex activation in CWS during spontaneous speech (Chow et al., 2023) [4]. The proposal substitutes a Frog-story narrative task for the picture-description task used in that study and targets a marginally larger sample (20 versus 22 per group), but the core comparison and predicted anatomical locus—left perisylvian, inferior frontal, and posterior temporal regions—are the same [3]. The incremental originality of Aim 1a therefore lies in task extension and replication rather than in generating qualitatively new knowledge. Similarly, the sICA denoising method, while sophisticated, has already been validated in children of the target age range and published by the same team [3] [4]; its application here represents a capable deployment rather than methodological innovation.

The novelty of the proposal is thus concentrated in Aim 2 and, to a lesser extent, in Aim 1b’s effort to isolate stuttering-moment activity in children. The three-group design with simultaneous trait and state analysis during continuous speech is a logical and original extension of the team’s prior work that no other group is positioned to execute with comparable access to a well-characterized longitudinal cohort.

## Rigor

**Rating: C; Confidence: B**

**The experimental design includes several validated components but contains three material weaknesses that limit the inferences the project can draw from its central comparisons.** The sICA denoising pipeline, speech coding procedures, and statistical correction methods are technically sound and appropriate for the research stage. However, the overlap of Aim 1a with already-published work, the unresolved collinearity in the mixed block/event GLM for Aim 1b, and the cross-sectional nature of the recovery comparison in Aim 2 together reduce the confidence with which the specific inferences promised in the aims can be drawn from the proposed design.

**The sICA denoising method forms the methodological backbone of the proposal and is well-supported.** The technique uses spatial independent component analysis with six noise templates and three component-intensity measures—out-of-brain ratio, scattering degree and slice-wise variation—to identify and remove noise components [3]. Validation against PET, the gold standard for speech imaging with minimal motion artefact, has shown that denoised fMRI and PET yield comparable continuous-speech activity patterns in adults [3] [7]. In the team’s preliminary data, DVARS remained below approximately 1% for movements under 9 mm after denoising across 281 resting-state scans in 50 CWS and 45 CWNS aged 3–13 [3]. An eight-year-old child completed the proposed storytelling paradigm and showed expected perisylvian activation [3]. The published Chow et al. (2023) study independently confirmed that this pipeline yields interpretable speech-related activation maps in 40 children aged 5–12, with control children’s activation patterns matching adult PET-derived maps [4]. The proposal’s approach to volumes with abrupt movements beyond the denoising method’s working range—additional removal via image scrubbing—is a sensible safeguard [3].

**The speech coding procedures are similarly well-founded.** Two independent speech-language pathologists will transcribe recordings in CLAN/CHAT and mark precise onset and offset of stuttering events, with acoustic events locating repetitions and prolongations and visible articulatory movements helping locate blocks [3]. The team’s published work reports 88% inter-rater agreement on stuttering moment identification using comparable procedures [4]. The linguistic covariates—principal components of speech rate, utterance length, semantic diversity, and syntactic complexity—are a thoughtful control for between-group differences in speech output that could otherwise confound neural comparisons [3]. Blinding of analysts to group membership and the use of permutation-based multiple comparison correction are correctly specified.

**Aim 1a’s central comparison has been substantially addressed by the team’s own published work.** Chow et al. (2023) compared 22 CWS and 18 controls aged 5–12 during spontaneous speech production using the same sICA denoising pipeline and found significantly reduced left ventral premotor cortex activation in CWS [4]. The present proposal substitutes a Frog-story narrative task for the picture-description task used in that study and targets a marginally larger sample (20 per group), but the core comparison and predicted anatomical locus—left posterior temporal and inferior frontal regions [3]—are the same. The incremental knowledge gain from Aim 1a over published work is modest; the comparison serves primarily as a replication and task extension rather than as a novel investigation.

**Aim 1b faces a more fundamental design challenge.** The mixed block/event GLM aims to separate sustained fluent-speech activity (block regressors) from transient activity during individual stuttering moments (event regressors) [3]. However, stuttering events are spontaneous—they occur unpredictably within the sustained speech blocks, and their timing cannot be experimentally controlled. In a standard mixed block/event design, event timing is experimentally manipulated to achieve estimable separation of sustained and transient BOLD components. Petersen and Dubis (2012), the design reference cited by the proposal, discuss designs where events are experimentally controlled [8]. When events are spontaneous, the event regressor can become collinear with the block regressor, particularly if stuttering tends to cluster at speech onsets—a known characteristic of stuttering—where BOLD responses to speech initiation also peak. The GLM may then have difficulty attributing variance uniquely to the event versus the block.

The proposal states the model “explicitly separates transient stuttering effects from the fluent speech activity on which they occur” [3], but does not provide diagnostics to support this claim, such as variance inflation factors, design efficiency estimates for the event contrast, or simulations with realistic stuttering timing distributions. The team’s own published work acknowledged the difficulty: Chow et al. (2023) included stuttering moments as a regressor of no interest specifically because insufficient events and limited statistical power prevented their analysis [4]. The proposal addresses the power concern by requiring more than 10 stuttering instances for Aim 1b inclusion [3], which is a sensible threshold, but the collinearity problem is a structural property of how events distribute within blocks that a higher event count alone does not resolve. Without evidence that the design can separate these components, the event analysis should be regarded as exploratory, and the inferences that can be drawn from null or unexpected findings in Aim 1b are limited.

**Aim 2’s cross-sectional recovery comparison can characterize group differences but cannot establish the developmental processes it aims to illuminate.** The proposal transparently acknowledges that “this design characterizes current group differences; future longitudinal work would track the development of those differences” [3]. This is an appropriate disclaimer, and cross-sectional characterization of recovered children’s speech networks is a legitimate and valuable scientific contribution absent from the literature. The limitation is that cross-sectional group differences cannot distinguish neural features that enabled recovery from those that are consequences of recovery or of divergent developmental trajectories. The recovered group may differ from the persistent group on unmeasured variables—language ability, treatment history, family environment—that confound the neural comparison. The proposal includes covariates for severity, treatment type, IQ, SES, age, and sex [3], but these adjustments cannot substitute for within-subject longitudinal tracking of the recovery process. Structural studies from this same cohort have demonstrated that developmental trajectories, not just cross-sectional differences, differentiate persistent from recovered children [1]. The functional study proposed here would provide a first characterization but cannot reach the developmental conclusions that the structural trajectory data support.

**The power calculations introduce additional uncertainty.** The estimates use adult-derived effect sizes: Cohen’s d = 0.7 (SD = 0.65) for between-group comparisons and d = 0.58 (SD = 0.74) for within-stuttering comparisons, yielding a requirement of 15 per group at 80% power with alpha 0.05, inflated to 20 for exclusions [3]. The Chow et al. (2023) paper explicitly noted that its sample of 22 CWS and 18 controls “could be relatively small for detecting subtler effects” and that “studies with small samples sizes are more susceptible to sampling variation and error, leading to false positives and negatives” [4]. The proposed n = 20 represents only a marginal increase, and the recovery effects—comparing rCWS with pCWS—are likely to be smaller than the persistence effects on which the adult-based power calculation rests. No childhood-specific effect size estimate is provided for the recovery comparison. The proposal acknowledges this implicitly by planning to increase recruitment “to accommodate exclusion and potentially smaller recovery effects” [3], but the adult-based estimate may still overstate power for the comparisons that constitute the project’s most novel contribution.

## Feasibility

**Rating: B; Confidence: B**

**The technical pathway is well-supported by both the team’s preliminary data and independent published validation, though recruiting 20 recovered children (rCWS) from the existing longitudinal cohort presents a material feasibility risk that the proposal acknowledges but does not fully quantify.**

The core methodological challenge—acquiring interpretable fMRI data during overt continuous speech in young children—is addressed by a validated denoising pipeline. The sICA method, developed by Xu et al. (2014), separates movement- and speech-related noise from BOLD signal using six noise templates and three component-intensity measures [3]. Validation against PET has shown that denoised fMRI and PET yield comparable continuous-speech activity patterns in adults, establishing that the technique recovers genuine neural signal from artifact-contaminated data [7]. In the team’s preliminary data, DVARS remained below approximately 1% for movements under 9 mm after denoising across 281 resting-state scans in 50 CWS and 45 CWNS aged 3–13 [3]. Critically, an eight-year-old child completed the proposed storytelling paradigm and showed expected perisylvian activation, providing direct feasibility evidence for the exact task and age range [3]. The published Chow et al. (2023) study confirmed that this sICA method, combined with a framewise displacement exclusion threshold, yields interpretable speech-related activation maps in 40 children aged 5–12, with control children’s activation patterns matching adult PET-derived maps [4].

The imaging platform—a GE MR750 3T scanner with an eight-channel head coil, optical noise-cancellation microphone, and MR-compatible camera—is standard and adequate for the planned acquisition parameters [3]. The task duration of less than 15 minutes of fMRI [3] and the total scanning session of about 30 minutes, based on the team’s published procedures [4], are within accepted limits for children aged 5–11. The proposal’s fallback that children with fewer than ten in-scanner stuttering instances are excluded only from Aim 1b’s event analysis rather than from every comparison [3] sensibly preserves sample size for the between-group contrasts. The familiarization procedures—mock scanning, task practice, and the option to reschedule visits with extra training [3]—align with procedures successfully used in the team’s published work, where children practised the experimental task for 5–10 minutes and participated in a mock MRI session with an animated video-guided protocol for younger children [4].

**Recruiting the recovered group is the principal practical risk.** Published reports from this longitudinal cohort document that, in an early wave, only 11 of 36 stuttering children met recovery criteria after longitudinal follow-up [9]. The proposal targets 20 children per group, drawing from an 18-month recruitment window (months 1–18) [3]. Continued cohort expansion and additional recruitment waves since the 2018 report would increase the pool of classifiable recovered children. However, the proposal does not quantify how many recovered children are currently available in the cohort who also fall within the 5–11 age range, are monolingual English speakers without exclusionary conditions, and are willing to undergo fMRI. The Chow et al. (2023) study, which recruited *de novo* rather than from this longitudinal cohort, enrolled 48 CWS and 26 controls to yield 22 persistent CWS and 18 controls in the final analysis—an attrition rate of approximately 35–40% from enrollment to analysable data after exclusions for motion, task non-compliance, incidental findings, and insufficient diagnostic criteria [4]. If similar attrition applies here, the proposal would need to identify roughly 30 eligible recovered children to retain 20 after exclusions. The 18-month recruitment window and the existing cohort infrastructure provide reasonable mitigation, but the recovered-group recruitment remains the primary practical uncertainty for Aim 2’s statistical power. This concern is material but not disqualifying: the team’s demonstrated ability to recruit and retain comparable paediatric samples, the fallback exclusion rules, and the substantial scientific value of even a smaller recovered group’s functional characterization mean the project can be implemented as described, with the recovered-group sample size representing the main practical risk to the precision of the recovery comparison.

## Ethics and safety

**Rating: A; Confidence: B**

**The ethics and safety arrangements are proportionate, well-specified, and appropriate for the study population and procedures.** The proposal describes a coherent set of protections addressing informed consent, participant welfare during scanning, data privacy, and data sharing. No additional ethical concerns arise from the study’s design, and the arrangements align with established practices in paediatric neuroimaging research as demonstrated in the team’s published work.

Parents or guardians will provide informed consent and children verbal or written assent [3]. This dual-permission approach is standard and necessary for research involving minors. The proposal includes MRI safety screening, hearing protection, rest breaks, and pre-scan familiarization with the procedure, directly addressing the primary sources of discomfort and anxiety in paediatric scanning—loud acoustic noise, confinement, and unfamiliarity with the environment [3]. Parents may remain nearby or with a distressed child, and participation can be stopped at any time without penalty. The team’s published procedures confirm that these arrangements have been implemented successfully in their prior work: children practised the experimental task for 5–10 minutes and participated in a mock MRI session, with younger children (up to age 8) receiving an additional animated, video-guided familiarization protocol [4]. The task duration of less than 15 minutes of fMRI [3], within a total scanning session of approximately 30 minutes [4], is well within accepted tolerances for children aged 5–11. The option to reschedule a visit with extra training if a child cannot complete the task correctly [3] further reduces pressure on participants.

Data protection measures follow standard institutional practice. Audio/video recordings and imaging data will use coded identifiers rather than directly identifying information, with locked or password-protected storage and access limited to approved collaborators [3]. The separation of identifying information from research data through coding is appropriate for the sensitivity of speech recordings and structural brain images. The proposal’s plan for post-study data sharing—de-identified relevant data released under a formal data-use agreement within one year after study closeout [3]—balances the value of secondary use with participant privacy.

The study population—children aged 5–11 with and without stuttering—does not raise concerns about undue inducement or exploitation. Stuttering can affect social, emotional, and academic development [3], and research that may ultimately inform interventions carries potential benefit to the population studied, provided the burden on child participants is minimized. The proposal’s attention to familiarization, comfort, and the right to withdraw does so. The exclusion of children with diagnosed developmental or psychiatric disorders other than stuttering [3] is a scientific design choice rather than an ethical concern; it narrows the population to which findings generalize but does not raise issues of unfair exclusion from potential benefit, as this is an observational neuroimaging study with no direct therapeutic intervention.

## Management

**Rating: B; Confidence: B**

**The management arrangements are appropriate to the project’s scope and draw on complementary, well-documented expertise across the three core domains—fMRI methods, the longitudinal childhood cohort, and speech-language analysis.** The proposal describes a tripartite division of scientific leadership: Chow contributes the sICA denoising methodology and fMRI analysis pipeline, Chang provides access to and knowledge of the longitudinal cohort with established persistence and recovery classifications, and Ratner brings the speech-analysis expertise needed for reliable transcription and stuttering-event identification [3]. Each investigator has published extensively in the relevant component. Chow is first author on the 2023 study that demonstrated the feasibility of sICA-denoised speech fMRI in 22 CWS and 18 controls [4]; Chang leads the longitudinal cohort programme from which structural findings on recovery-related white matter and cortical thickness trajectories have emerged [2] [1]; and Ratner’s speech coding procedures, with two independent raters achieving 88% agreement on stuttering moment identification, have been validated in the team’s published work [4]. This division of labour is sensible: no single investigator is required to span all three domains, and the critical dependencies—cohort access, denoising code, and transcription protocols—are distributed across the team rather than concentrated in one person.

**The schedule is practical and appropriately scaled.** Months 1–18 are allocated for recruitment and imaging, with preparation of analyses running in parallel; months 12–24 for speech and fMRI analysis and preliminary presentations; and months 24–36 for manuscript preparation, submission and dissemination [3]. The overlapping timeline for recruitment, familiarization, and analysis preparation reflects the reality that analytical pipelines can be developed and tested on early-acquired data while recruitment continues. The 18-month recruitment window provides time to draw participants from the longitudinal cohort’s annual assessment cycles and to reschedule visits where children require additional training [3]. The total project duration of 36 months is appropriate for an R21 mechanism study of this scope: acquiring 60 usable paediatric fMRI datasets with the described quality-control exclusions, completing the speech transcription and coding, and performing the mixed block/event GLM analyses with appropriate sensitivity checks.

The proposal does not include detailed CVs, an itemized budget, or evidence of IRB approval. Under the review standards applied here, the absence of these administrative documents alone does not demonstrate incapacity or inadequate resourcing. The team’s extensive publication record using the same cohort, scanner platform (GE MR750 3T at the University of Michigan), and denoising pipeline provides strong circumstantial evidence of institutional access and capability [3] [4]. The required scanning, recording, and stimulus equipment is stated as available [3]. No critical single-point-of-failure dependencies are evident in the described arrangement: the scanner and cohort are institutional resources rather than investigator-dependent, the sICA denoising code has been published and validated [7], and the speech coding procedures are documented with established inter-rater reliability.

The proposal identifies future directions—functional connectivity during continuous speech, time-resolved analyses of transitions into stuttering, and longitudinal tracking from onset through persistence or recovery [3]—that fall outside the current scope but indicate an awareness of how the proposed work fits within a larger research programme. This does not affect the management judgement for the present proposal, but it supports the assessment that the team understands the logical next steps and has not over-committed the current project to aims beyond its R21 scale.

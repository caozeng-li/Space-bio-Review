# Project review

## Overall assessment

The EMPOWER project will identify which of five positive-emotion regulation skills—noticing positive events and gratitude, mindfulness, reappraisal, personal strengths and goal-setting, and acts of kindness—contribute meaningfully to the well-being of young adult cancer survivors after active treatment. Existing multicomponent interventions produce promising but variable effects across clinical populations, yet cannot disentangle which specific skills drive improvement or whether some add burden without benefit. Using the Multiphase Optimization Strategy, a full factorial experiment assigns 352 survivors to 32 combinations of the five components, providing 176 participants per component contrast. The primary outcomes are positive affect, life satisfaction, depression, and anxiety. The most valuable expected knowledge is a ranked set of component effect estimates and preliminary interaction and mediation findings that together will allow assembly of a leaner, evidence-based optimized package ready for a definitive multisite trial. This component-level evidence also addresses a broader need in positive psychology intervention science for data on which skills are active for whom.

The design is methodologically efficient for screening main effects, uses validated PROMIS and NIH Toolbox adaptive measures, and is grounded in pilot work demonstrating feasibility and acceptability of the web-based EMPOWER platform in young adult survivors. The team combines deep expertise in young-adult oncology, positive-emotion intervention research, MOST trial methodology, and eHealth delivery. Three material constraints define the boundaries of the contribution. First, the study is powered to detect main effects of d ≥ 0.31 but interactions only at d ≥ 0.62, so moderate synergistic or antagonistic combinations will likely be missed. Second, the inert-control strategy—replacing absent skills with factual journaling and reading—could attenuate estimated effects to an unquantified degree if those control activities themselves improve well-being. Third, the protocol relaxes alpha to 0.10 for optimization screening but does not specify a formal decision rule for how the four primary outcomes jointly determine component retention. The primary feasibility risk is that recruitment and retention assumptions (13% of eligible and 72% at one month) are near the upper end of pilot experience, though contingency channels and multi-wave improvements provide partial mitigation.

The full factorial design is well-matched to its screening purpose and will credibly rank the five components by their individual contributions to well-being. The acknowledged power limit for interactions and the unquantified attenuation risk from the inert control condition mean that the assembled package may omit components whose benefit depends on combination, and that the absolute effect estimates will require confirmation in a subsequent trial. These are inherent trade-offs in a MOST optimization, not design failures. The recruitment and retention assumptions are the most material implementation risks, but they are supported by pilot data with documented improvement across waves and by contingency channels. On balance, the knowledge produced would be a substantive step toward an evidence-based, scalable well-being intervention for an underserved survivor group, and the current plan provides a sufficient route to that contribution.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**A clinically important question that the factorial design can credibly address.** Young adults diagnosed with cancer face persistent distress during a life stage defined by education, career development, relationship formation, and family planning, yet they have limited access to supportive care tailored to their developmental context [1]. Existing multicomponent positive-psychology interventions, including those the investigative team has previously developed and tested (LILAC, DAHLIA, IRISS, LEAF), have produced promising but variable package-level effects on depression and positive affect across several clinical populations [1]. However, a package-level result cannot identify which specific skills are necessary, which add burden without benefit, or whether particular combinations produce synergistic or antagonistic effects [1]. The EMPOWER project directly addresses this gap by applying the Multiphase Optimization Strategy to isolate the individual and combined contributions of five emotion-regulation components [1].

**Attainable knowledge with immediate practical value.** With 176 participants assigned to each component level—achieved by pooling across the 32 conditions of the full factorial design—the study is powered to detect component main effects of d ≥ 0.31 at 80% power (α = 0.10), adequate for the stated screening purpose that seeks to retain components with effects around d = 0.50 [1]. The primary outcomes capture the full spectrum of psychological functioning that the theoretical framework demands: positive affect and life satisfaction represent the well-being dimension, while depression and anxiety represent the distress dimension [1]. Secondary outcomes—physical functioning, fatigue, satisfaction with social roles, and health behaviors—broaden the assessment to domains relevant to survivorship care [1]. The primary endpoint at one month, with follow-up tracking through six months, allows the study to distinguish immediate from sustained effects [1].

The knowledge produced will directly enable assembly of a leaner, evidence-based intervention package ready for confirmatory evaluation in a subsequent multisite trial [1]. Rather than carrying forward all five components on the assumption that a comprehensive package is necessarily superior—an assumption that existing trials cannot test—the optimized package will include only those components that demonstrate meaningful individual effects. This has practical implications for participant burden, intervention scalability, and resource efficiency in future implementation.

**Broader scientific significance.** Beyond the specific optimized package, the component effect estimates contribute to a need identified by recent systematic reviews for evidence on which specific positive-psychology skills are active, for whom, and under what conditions [2]. The five EMPOWER components are individually supported by meta-analytic evidence—gratitude interventions show pooled effects of d ≈ 0.31–0.38 on well-being [2], savoring interventions yield g = 0.51 overall [3], and positive psychology interventions collectively produce d = 0.29–0.47 for well-being outcomes [2]—but comparative evidence within a single population and design is absent. The EMPOWER factorial experiment provides that comparative evidence for a population that has been underserved in intervention research.

**Boundaries of the contribution.** The relaxed alpha level (0.10) and the acknowledged power limitation for interactions (detectable only at d ≥ 0.62) mean that the component effect estimates are screening-level, not definitive [1]. The inert-control strategy could attenuate estimated effects to an unquantified degree if those control activities themselves improve well-being [1]. These constraints are inherent to the MOST optimization phase and are explicitly acknowledged by the investigators; they define the boundaries within which the knowledge should be interpreted pending confirmatory testing [1].

## Innovation

**A clearly bounded advance that addresses a genuine gap.** The EMPOWER project does not introduce a new therapeutic technique or theoretical framework; its originality lies in the comparative structure it applies to an established set of interventions within a specific, underserved group. Prior positive-psychology trials—including the team's own LILAC, DAHLIA, IRISS, and LEAF packages—have established that multicomponent emotion-regulation interventions can improve well-being across clinical contexts [1]. However, these package-level trials share a fundamental limitation: they cannot determine which specific skills drive improvement, whether certain components add burden without benefit, or how components interact when combined [1]. The present project replaces the classical "test the whole package" paradigm with a systematic, prospective comparison of five individually supported emotion-regulation skills within a single young adult cancer survivor population, using a design that isolates their separate and combined contributions.

**What the factorial design adds beyond prior component-level evidence.** Each EMPOWER component is supported by independent evidence from studies conducted in isolation or in different populations: gratitude interventions show pooled effects of d ≈ 0.31–0.38 [2]; savoring interventions yield g = 0.51 [3]; and positive psychology interventions collectively produce d = 0.29–0.47, with gratitude-based, strength-based, and mindfulness-integrated approaches showing the strongest evidence bases [2]. This evidence is fragmented across different studies, populations, delivery formats, and control conditions. The EMPOWER factorial experiment provides common ground by testing all five components simultaneously in a single population, with identical delivery format (web-based, self-guided), identical measurement battery (PROMIS and NIH Toolbox CATs), and a single control strategy [1]. This yields comparative effect estimates not confounded by differences in study context—a contribution that cannot be obtained by aggregating across the existing literature.

**Extension of the MOST paradigm to positive emotion regulation in AYA survivorship.** The Multiphase Optimization Strategy has been applied to intervention refinement in dementia caregiving, where a recent fractional factorial trial identified mindfulness as the most consistently effective component and demonstrated synergistic interactions with self-care skills and behavioral problem management [4]; in breast cancer pain management, where a factorial protocol is testing mindful attention, decentering, and values-based components [5]; and in several health behavior contexts including weight loss and physical activity [6] [7] [8]. The EMPOWER project extends this optimization paradigm to a new domain—positive emotion skills in post-treatment AYA cancer survivors—and to a new set of candidate components.

The ASCENT pilot (Ingram et al.), with Salsman as senior author, tests four components for depression self-management in AYA survivors using a small factorial design with 16 participants [9]. That project targets depressive symptom management, whereas EMPOWER targets well-being enhancement across both positive and negative affect dimensions. The two projects address complementary clinical targets, and ASCENT's pilot scale does not overlap with EMPOWER's powered optimization scope (N = 352).

**Contribution to the trajectory toward personalized intervention.** Recent systematic reviews have identified personalized positive psychology—tailoring intervention type, dose, and delivery to individual characteristics—as a priority for the field [2]. Component-level evidence of the kind EMPOWER will produce is a prerequisite for such personalization: until it is known which skills are effective on average, and whether effects differ by age, gender, or baseline characteristics, it is impossible to match interventions to individuals. The planned moderation analyses examining age and gender [1] begin to supply this evidence.

## Rigor

**An efficient screening design with three acknowledged constraints that together limit the determinacy of the optimized package.** The EMPOWER full factorial experiment is methodologically well-suited to its primary purpose: ranking five emotion-regulation components by their individual contributions to well-being outcomes. However, three interrelated constraints—limited interaction power, unquantified control-condition attenuation, and the absence of a formal multi-outcome decision rule—mean that the assembled package may omit components whose value depends on combination or on a different outcome pattern than the one that emerges from the primary analyses. These are not design errors; they are inherent trade-offs in a screening-phase MOST trial that the investigators explicitly acknowledge.

**Design structure and measurement strengths.** The 2⁵ full factorial design is the canonical structure for the MOST optimization phase [1]. Each component is assigned to exactly 176 participants and withheld from exactly 176, with assignment balanced across all other components. This pooling—rather than treating the 32 conditions as separate arms—provides the efficiency that makes the design feasible [1]. Block randomization (size 64), administered by the study statistician after baseline assessment, protects allocation concealment. The analytic model—ANCOVA adjusting follow-up scores for baseline values with five component indicators—is appropriate for the screening question [1]. The supplementary longitudinal mixed-effects models with person-level random effects and autoregressive covariance structure provide a sensitivity check using all available time points [1].

The measurement battery is a clear strength. The four primary outcomes—positive affect and life satisfaction from the NIH Toolbox, depression and anxiety from PROMIS—are administered as computer-adaptive tests, reducing participant burden to an estimated 15–20 minutes while maintaining precision and avoiding ceiling or floor effects [1]. These capture both the positive and negative dimensions of well-being that the theoretical framework, grounded in Broaden-and-Build Theory and Revised Stress and Coping Theory, requires [1]. Secondary outcomes—physical functioning, fatigue, satisfaction with social roles, and health behaviors—broaden the assessment to survivorship-relevant domains, though with 11 participants per condition their analysis will be descriptive rather than powered [1]. The weekly assessment of primary outcomes during the five-week intervention is a methodological asset; however, the proposal does not specify a formal time-course analysis for this purpose [1].

**Constraint 1: limited interaction power.** The study is powered to detect component main effects of d ≥ 0.31 at 80% power (α = 0.10, assuming 256 participants at one month), which is adequate for the stated retention criterion of d ≈ 0.50 [1]. The detectable two-way interaction, however, is d ≥ 0.62. This means the study is insensitive to moderate synergistic or antagonistic combinations—precisely the kind observed in other MOST trials, such as the dementia caregiving study where mindfulness enhanced the benefits of self-care skills and behavioral problem management on depression [4]. A component that shows no main effect but whose benefit depends entirely on co-administration with another component would likely be missed. The stated goal of identifying "combinations with synergistic or antagonistic effects" [1] is therefore substantially underpowered. The relaxed α = 0.10—appropriate for a screening trial whose findings will undergo confirmatory testing—affects only the false-negative rate for main effects, not the power for interactions.

**Constraint 2: unquantified control-condition attenuation.** Absent components are replaced with factual journaling and reading about nutrition or stress responses, aiming to provide credible control material while maintaining engagement [1]. The proposal candidly acknowledges that "if this content itself improves well-being, estimated EMPOWER effects would be smaller." The magnitude of this attenuation is unknown: the two EMPOWER pilot waves were single-arm studies [1] and provide no evidence about the control condition's response. If the control activities produce even small improvements, genuinely useful components may fall below the retention threshold. The factorial design mitigates this somewhat—each component comparison is averaged across all configurations of the other components—but the attenuation risk remains real and uncalibrated.

**Constraint 3: absent multi-outcome decision rule.** The protocol does not specify a formal decision rule for how the four primary outcomes jointly determine component retention. With four outcomes, five components, and α = 0.10, the multiplicity risk is non-trivial even for an optimization study [1]. A component might show a meaningful effect on positive affect but not on depression, or on life satisfaction but not on anxiety. Without a pre-specified rule—for example, a composite outcome, a requirement that effects appear on at least two of four outcomes, or a correction for multiple testing—the retention decisions will necessarily involve post hoc judgment. In a screening trial, this flexibility is not disqualifying, but it introduces investigator discretion into an otherwise structured optimization procedure.

**Cumulative effect.** These three constraints are consistent with the MOST optimization phase and appropriately acknowledged. They do not prevent the study from achieving its primary aim—ranking components by main effects—but they mean the optimized package's composition should be treated as a best estimate requiring confirmatory testing, rather than as a definitive determination of which components are necessary and sufficient.

## Feasibility

**The core technical pathway is well-established; the principal risks concern recruitment and retention assumptions that are near the upper bound of pilot performance.**

The web-based intervention delivery rests on solid institutional foundations. EMPOWER is a self-guided, five-week program in which each weekly module comprises one to two days of instructional material followed by daily home practice of 20–30 minutes [1]. The Wake Forest Research Information Systems Unit hosts the platform, REDCap manages screening, consent, and survey data collection, and block randomization is administered by the study statistician after baseline assessment [1]. This technical architecture has already supported the two single-arm EMPOWER pilot waves at two academic sites [1]. The eight-week completion window accommodates interruptions common in this population without complex protocol adaptations [1], and the factorial manipulation—substituting inert journaling and reading for absent skills—is a software configuration task rather than a procedural one [1].

**Recruitment assumptions and pilot evidence.** The proposal assumes 2,850 potentially eligible survivors over 36 months from the two comprehensive cancer centers, based on approximately 550 and 400 post-treatment survivors seen annually at Wake Forest and Northwestern [1]. A 13% recruitment rate is then applied to reach the target of 352 participants. The pilot data bracket this assumption: the Northwestern wave enrolled 18 of 185 potentially eligible survivors (9.7%), while the Wake Forest wave, which benefited from multiple recruitment channels and dedicated staff, enrolled 23 of 99 contacted (23.2%) [1]. The 13% projection sits between these values, making it plausible but closer to a midpoint than a conservative estimate. If the actual rate converges toward 9.7% of eligibles, the eligible pool would need to be approximately 3,630 rather than 2,850, exceeding the projected catchment. The community partnership with Cancer Services provides a contingency channel [1], but its capacity to close a substantial gap is unspecified.

**Retention assumptions.** The 72% retention assumption for the primary one-month endpoint presents a parallel concern. The two pilot waves achieved 72% and 66.7% retention at follow-up, but these were small single-arm studies (N = 18 and 23) with simpler protocols than the 32-condition factorial experiment [1]. Self-guided digital health interventions in AYA cancer populations show a wide retention range: a recent expressive writing pilot achieved 72% six-month follow-up after iterative modifications [10], and a meta-analysis found that instructor-based delivery produced stronger effects than self-guided approaches [11]—and EMPOWER is self-guided. If retention falls to 60–65% at one month, the analytic sample would be approximately 211–229 rather than 256, increasing the detectable main effect from 0.31 to roughly 0.33–0.35—still adequate for the d ≈ 0.50 retention criterion but further eroding the already-limited interaction power. The $20 per-assessment incentive and the eight-week completion window are reasonable retention supports but do not eliminate the risk [1].

These recruitment and retention assumptions are the only feasibility elements that could materially alter the study's inferential yield. The four-year timeline phases set-up, optimization, analysis, and dissemination appropriately, though recruitment extends into Year 4, leaving limited buffer if accrual lags [1]. The technical platform, team expertise, and institutional infrastructure are all in place and have been demonstrated in the pilot work. The feasibility risk is concentrated: the study can be carried out with the stated methods and resources, provided the recruitment and retention rates do not fall substantially below the pilot-informed projections.

## Ethics and safety

**The intervention is appropriately low-risk and the ethical and data-protection arrangements are proportionate to the activity level.**

EMPOWER teaches emotion-regulation skills—noticing positive events and gratitude, mindfulness, reappraisal, strengths and goal-setting, and acts of kindness—through a self-guided, web-based platform [1]. These are behavioral and cognitive practices with no intrinsic physical risk, and the online format allows participants to remain in their usual clinical care throughout. The intervention occupies the supportive-care space between self-help and formal mental health treatment; it is not intended to manage acute psychiatric conditions, and the eligibility criteria appropriately exclude individuals with significant psychiatric histories from a self-guided skills program that lacks therapist monitoring or crisis-response capacity [1].

**Consent, data protection, and participant experience.** Potential participants complete a REDCap eligibility screener, provide informed consent, and receive unique study IDs and PINs that control questionnaire access [1]. REDCap provides secure, HIPAA-compliant data collection, and the Wake Forest Research Information Systems Unit hosts the intervention platform, giving both sites institutional IT oversight with established security protocols [1]. No deception is involved: all participants receive a credible web-based program; the experimental manipulation substitutes control content (factual journaling, reading about nutrition or stress responses) for absent active components [1].

Participation burden is modest and appropriately compensated. The five-week intervention requires 20–30 minutes of daily home practice, with up to eight weeks allowed for completion [1]. Assessments occur at baseline, weekly during the intervention, and at one week, one month, three months, and six months post-intervention [1]. The PROMIS and NIH Toolbox computer-adaptive tests typically require 15–20 minutes per administration—substantially less than fixed-length instruments [1]. Participants receive $20 per completed assessment, up to $100 total, consistent with standard practice for survey-based studies and unlikely to be coercive [1].

**Additional safeguards.** The exclusion of individuals with recurrence or multiple primary cancers [1] narrows generalizability but is a design choice for an optimization trial. Provider permission precedes contact for clinic-based recruitment [1], an appropriate safeguard that prevents unsolicited approaches to patients whose clinical status may make participation unsuitable. Community-based recruitment operates outside this gatekeeping mechanism, which is standard for studies that also recruit beyond clinical settings. Formal ethics committee approval and a data safety monitoring plan are not detailed in the supplied narrative, but these omissions reflect the proposal format; the REDCap and institutional IT infrastructure described are standard components of an IRB-approved protocol at U.S. academic medical centers, and the intervention's low-risk behavioral nature typically qualifies for expedited review with minimal monitoring requirements.

## Management

**A well-constructed team and established institutional infrastructure; the main management risk is the tight coupling of recruitment timelines to the four-year schedule.**

The investigative team combines the specific expertise domains that the project requires. John Salsman contributes young-adult oncology survivorship research, recruitment experience with this population, and outcome-measurement expertise; Judith Moskowitz contributes the positive-emotion intervention tradition from which the EMPOWER skills were developed; and Lynne Wagner brings MOST trial methodology experience [1]. The broader collaborator group covers psychosocial oncology, medical oncology, quantitative methods, survivorship, and eHealth, providing clinical and technical support without overburdening the leadership structure [1]. This configuration matches the project's dual demands: content expertise in both the population and the intervention class, combined with methodological expertise in the factorial optimization design.

**Institutional resources and schedule.** The two recruitment sites—Wake Forest Baptist Comprehensive Cancer Center and Northwestern's Robert H. Lurie Comprehensive Cancer Center—are both NCI-designated comprehensive cancer centers with existing survivorship programs, tumor registries, and EHR-based identification workflows [1]. The Wake Forest Research Information Systems Unit hosts the intervention platform, and REDCap provides the data collection infrastructure across both sites [1]. These are institutional resources rather than project-built systems, which reduces technical risk and start-up time. The pilot waves confirmed that the two-site workflow—identification, provider permission, contact, screening, consent, randomization, and assessment—functions with this specific intervention and population [1].

The four-year schedule phases the work appropriately: study start-up occupies the first two quarters, optimization recruitment runs from late Year 1 through early Year 4, data analysis overlaps in Years 3–4, and dissemination and follow-on grant preparation occupy the final period [1]. The expected products—component effect estimates, mediator/moderator findings, and an assembled intervention ready for confirmatory trial planning—are clearly specified and consistent with the MOST optimization phase [1].

**Recruitment timeline risk.** The principal management concern, elaborated in the Feasibility section, is that recruitment occupies approximately ten quarters with limited buffer if accrual falls behind the projected rate [1]. If actual enrollment converges toward the lower end of the pilot range (9.7%), the recruitment window could extend beyond the four-year grant period, compressing the analysis and dissemination phases. A recruitment shortfall would not prevent the optimization analyses—main effect estimation would remain possible with a smaller sample, albeit with reduced precision—but it would further erode interaction detection and could shift the manuscript timeline into a no-cost extension. This concern reflects the inherent challenge of recruiting young adult cancer survivors to a multi-week self-guided intervention, a challenge the team has documented candidly in its pilot reports. The multi-channel strategy and contingency partnership provide partial mitigation.

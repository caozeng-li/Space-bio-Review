# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project, Personalized Screening for Lung Cancer (PLuS), aims to determine how multimorbidity modifies the benefits and harms of low-dose CT (LDCT) lung cancer screening in routine care. The rationale is that patients at highest cancer risk, often due to smoking, also face increased competing mortality and procedural complications from related chronic diseases, potentially negating screening’s benefit [1]. The study will use a multi-site cohort of approximately 34,039 screened adults to characterize comorbidity, functional limitations, and pulmonary function. It will then estimate screening-related outcomes across these health states and feed those empirical estimates into the validated UM-LCS microsimulation model [1]. The most valuable expected knowledge is the identification of specific, evidence-based health subgroups in which lung cancer screening is effective, ineffective, or harmful, providing a quantitative basis for individualizing screening decisions currently guided only by vague health descriptions [1].

The proposal is strongly supported by its conceptual framework, the assembled multi-site real-world cohort with validated instruments like the Gagne comorbidity score and NLP for nodule identification, and an experienced, multidisciplinary team with direct access to an established CISNET microsimulation model [1]. External evidence, including an ATS research statement and a recent systematic review, unambiguously confirms the importance and novelty of the proposed work [2]. The main limitations are inferential, not logistical. The observational design can describe outcomes across health strata but cannot directly estimate net benefit without an unscreened comparison group. The analytical plan to address this relies on a microsimulation model calibrated on healthier populations, an unvalidated frailty indicator structurally missing from a key subpopulation, and an unresolved approach to competing risks. These limitations materially affect the project’s ability to produce the robust, causally interpretable estimates needed to change clinical guidelines.

The project’s high value lies in its systematic approach to a problem of first-order policy importance. The team has designed a study that leverages a unique, diverse cohort and advanced modeling to build the evidence base for personalized screening. However, the scientific impact of this project is critically dependent on the strength of the empirical evidence it can generate for Aim 2. The significant limitations in the causal inference design—particularly the lack of an unscreened comparator and the unvalidated application of key measures—create a risk that the study’s adjusted estimates will not be robust enough to reliably refine the simulation model or directly inform clinical guidelines. The value of the eventual output would be enhanced by strengthening the analytical plan to address these inferential challenges directly.

**Recommendation: conditional support.** The project’s descriptive aims (Aim 1 and descriptive components of Aim 2) are supported as proposed. The adjusted-outcome and simulation components (remaining Aim 2 and Aim 3) are supportable conditional on the analytical plan being strengthened to specify (1) the handling of competing risks in cumulative incidence estimation, and (2) the protocol for calibrating cohort-derived estimates into the UM-LCS model. These targeted, feasible adjustments can materially improve the confidence and utility of the project’s central output without altering its scope or resources.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

**Scientific value is excellent (A, confidence B).** The proposal tackles a central gap in the evidence base for lung cancer screening—the role of multimorbidity—with a systematic, multi-site plan that can produce quantitative, clinically actionable evidence [1]. Current US guidelines condition screening on being in “reasonably good health,” yet the supporting NLST enrolled a population substantially healthier than the community-eligible population. The proposal presents data showing median life expectancy of 21.2 years among NLST participants versus 14 years for a Medicare screening-eligible subgroup, with higher rates of diabetes, heart disease, and stroke in the community [1]. This mismatch creates a pressing scientific need for evidence that differentiates those likely to benefit from those in whom harms may dominate. The proposal addresses exactly this question.

**Attainable knowledge across three aims.** Aim 1 will characterize the prevalence of chronic conditions, functional limitations, and impaired pulmonary function among approximately 34,039 adults undergoing screening in three distinct health systems, with explicit attention to variation by race/ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and age [1]. This descriptive contribution is itself valuable, since the Almatrafi et al. systematic review of 57 studies found that validated comorbidity indices and frailty measures were rarely used in LCS populations [2].

Aim 2 will quantify how screening-related harms—false-positive findings, procedure-related complications, and their downstream consequences—vary across comorbidity, functional limitation, and pulmonary function strata [1]. Procedure-related complication rates in community settings are substantially higher than those observed in the NLST: Huo et al. reported rates of approximately 22% in younger populations and 24% in Medicare populations, roughly double the NLST‘s 8–10% [3]. Understanding how multimorbidity further modifies these rates is critical for informed shared decision-making.

Aim 3 will refine the UM-LCS CISNET model with cohort-derived estimates of test characteristics, biopsy and complication rates, adherence, and competing mortality, then simulate alternative screening strategies across multimorbidity subgroups to identify thresholds where the benefit-harm balance shifts [1].

**Responding to an identified evidence gap.** The combination of these three aims provides a coherent evidence product: descriptive patterns of multimorbidity, empirical estimates of how outcomes vary with health status, and model-based comparisons of lifetime strategies. The 2018 ATS research statement explicitly called for incorporating comorbidity severity and functional status into screening selection [2]. Separately, Kale et al. demonstrated the feasibility of incorporating comorbidity effects into microsimulation but acknowledged reliance on self-reported comorbidity from PLCO and an inability to determine disease severity [4]. The present proposal improves on this by using clinical data with time-varying measurement, validated NLP for nodule identification (sensitivity and specificity above 95%) [1], and a combined Charlson/Elixhauser score with strong predictive performance (c-statistic 0.860 for 30-day mortality) [5]. The expected output—subgroup-specific screening outcomes and quantitative stopping rules resembling those of an average-health reference population—would provide exactly the kind of evidence that current guidelines lack.

## Innovation

**Innovation is excellent (A, confidence B).** The proposal makes a genuine advance beyond prior work in three integrated respects: the measurement strategy, the empirical setting, and the synthesis of observational evidence with simulation modeling.

**Multi-dimensional measurement strategy.** The systematic combination of three complementary dimensions of health status within a single study is a clear advance. Prior work has typically relied on single dimensions. Howard et al. established the generalizability problem by comparing self-reported comorbidity between NLST participants and HRS respondents, but did not quantify outcome modification [6]. Caverly et al. used microsimulation with life expectancy as the key modifier but acknowledged that quantitative thresholds depended on model structure and did not incorporate detailed comorbidity-outcome associations [7]. The Kale et al. microsimulation incorporated literature-derived comorbidity effects on complications and mortality, but relied on self-reported comorbidity from PLCO and NHIS, with limited ability to determine disease severity [4].

The present proposal goes beyond all of these by simultaneously measuring the Gagne combined Charlson/Elixhauser score (validated for mortality prediction with c-statistic 0.860 for 30-day mortality) [5], a claims-based function-related indicator capturing 15 domains of functional limitation [1], and spirometry-defined pulmonary function with GOLD classification [1]. The Almatrafi et al. systematic review of 57 LCS studies confirmed that no prior study had combined validated comorbidity indices with frailty measures, and that validated tools were used in only 10% of studies [2]. The proposal directly addresses this identified gap.

**Real-world, multi-site empirical setting.** A second innovation is the empirical setting: a multi-site observational cohort spanning three geographically and demographically distinct health systems—an integrated care organization with a large, ethnically diverse population (KPSC), a state safety-net hospital (MUSC), and a VA medical center with a dedicated screening coordinator [1]. This design permits estimation of screening outcomes using real-world EHR data with time-varying comorbidity measurement, rather than relying solely on model-based extrapolation from trial populations that were substantially healthier than the community-eligible population [1]. The use of a validated NLP algorithm for pulmonary nodule identification (sensitivity 96%, specificity 92%, with an expanded version above 95% for both) [1] and Lung-RADS standardized reporting across all sites [8] supports reliable, comparable outcome ascertainment.

**Integration of observational evidence with simulation modeling.** The third innovation is the integration pathway from observational estimates to microsimulation refinement. The proposal does not simply compare outcomes across comorbidity strata; it uses those empirical estimates—screening outcomes, biopsy and complication rates, Lung-RADS adherence, and competing mortality—to refine the inputs of the UM-LCS CISNET model [1]. This creates a feedback loop between real-world evidence and decision-analytic modeling that can generate policy-relevant comparisons of alternative screening strategies across multimorbidity subgroups, including identification of thresholds where benefits and harms resemble those of a predefined average-health subgroup [1]. The extension to examine how individual preferences jointly with comorbidity affect quality-adjusted net benefit, building on the Caverly et al. preference-sensitivity framework, represents a coherent methodological extension rather than a de novo model [1] [7].

Taken together, these three elements—multi-dimensional exposure measurement, real-world multi-site outcome estimation, and empirical-to-simulation integration—represent a methodological package that goes substantively beyond existing single-dimension studies or simulation-only approaches. The innovation lies not in a single novel technique but in the systematic assembly of validated methods to address a question that multiple authoritative sources have identified as unresolved.

## Rigor

**Rigor is moderate (C, confidence B).** The design can credibly deliver the descriptive and prevalence components of the project, but its capacity to produce the causally interpretable, adjusted estimates of how multimorbidity modifies screening net benefit—the project’s central inferential goal—is materially constrained by several design features that the current analytical plan does not fully resolve.

**Strengths supporting descriptive and empirical inference.** The multi-site cohort with approximately 34,039 screened adults across KPSC, MUSC, and VA Portland provides geographic, socioeconomic, racial/ethnic, and care-delivery diversity that strengthens generalizability [1]. The use of Lung-RADS for standardized reporting of screening findings across all sites supports consistent outcome classification [8]. The NLP algorithm for nodule identification, with reported sensitivity of 96% and specificity of 92%, supports reliable capture of screening-detected abnormalities [1]. The Gagne combined Charlson/Elixhauser comorbidity score is externally validated for mortality prediction with a c-statistic of 0.860 (95% CI 0.854–0.866) for 30-day mortality [5], making it an appropriate instrument for characterizing comorbidity burden.

The discrete-time survival modeling framework, indexing time by examination number and treating comorbidity, functional limitation, and pulmonary function as time-varying covariates, correctly reflects the repeated-measures structure of screening data [1]. Power calculations are adequate for the main comorbidity comparisons: with approximately 902 incident cancers, the study can detect a 5% absolute risk difference in procedure-related complications and 1.3–1.9% differences in false-positive rates across comorbidity levels at 80% power [1].

**Absence of an unscreened comparison group.** The most consequential limitation is the observational design’s capacity to estimate how multimorbidity modifies the net benefit of screening. The design compares outcomes across comorbidity strata within a screened cohort; it has no unscreened comparison group. Without a counterfactual, the study cannot directly estimate what would have happened to similarly multimorbid patients in the absence of screening. The proposal addresses this by feeding observed estimates into the UM-LCS simulation (Aim 3), where the model provides the unscreened counterfactual. This is a valid strategy, but it shifts the burden of inference onto the model’s structural assumptions, which were calibrated to trial populations (NLST, PLCO) that were substantially healthier than the target population [1].

**Limitations of the functional limitation measure.** The Claims-based Frailty Indicator (FRI) was developed and validated in the Cardiovascular Health Study cohort (mean age 72, 84% white, AUC 0.75 for concurrent frailty phenotype prediction) [9]. Its performance in a lung cancer screening population—enriched for current and former smokers with substantial comorbidity—has not been established. An AUC of 0.75 represents moderate discrimination, and misclassification may be differential across exposure categories. Furthermore, FRI ascertainment is restricted to KPSC and VA, representing approximately 80% of the cohort; the MUSC safety-net site lacks the necessary claims and EHR indicators [1]. This structural missingness at an entire site is unlikely to be completely random, and the validity of multiple imputation under a missing-at-random assumption is difficult to verify when missingness is institutional rather than incidental.

**Unresolved competing risks.** The discrete-time survival models estimate cumulative probabilities by aggregating discrete hazards for each outcome separately [1]. In a multimorbid screening population, death from competing causes is a material concern. If patients in higher comorbidity strata die from cardiovascular or respiratory disease before experiencing a screening outcome of interest, treating death as a censoring event will overestimate the cumulative incidence of that outcome in those strata. This biases the comparison of cumulative probabilities across comorbidity groups. The proposal does not describe how competing risks will be handled in cumulative incidence estimation. Competing-risk methods—cumulative incidence functions with Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards or cause-specific hazards—would be more appropriate. The recent demonstration that Fine-Gray models can yield substantively different estimates from cause-specific analyses when competing mortality is high [10] underscores the importance of this methodological choice.

**Propensity score application to non-treatment exposures.** The propensity score approach is applied in a context where the “exposure”—multimorbidity level—is not a treatment to which patients are assigned. The validity of this adaptation depends on whether the variables predicting multimorbidity level also predict screening outcomes, and whether unmeasured confounders such as healthcare utilization intensity and provider behavior are adequately captured. G-methods are often preferred for time-varying confounding in such settings [11]. The proposal’s approach is defensible but limits the causal interpretation of adjusted estimates.

These limitations are individually manageable; collectively, they reduce confidence that Aim 2’s adjusted estimates will be sufficiently robust to serve as the sole empirical inputs for the Aim 3 microsimulation refinements that are intended to drive clinical decision-making.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility is good (B, confidence B).** The core technical pathway rests on established data infrastructure, validated instruments, and a team with directly relevant prior experience. Two material limitations—incomplete functional-limitation ascertainment and an unspecified microsimulation calibration protocol—are manageable risks that do not prevent delivery of the stated aims.

**Established data infrastructure and instruments.** The Watch the Spot common data model already defines 13 groups of elements covering eligibility, demographics, smoking, nodules, utilization, comorbidity, and vital status; the team has prior experience combining EHR and claims across institutions using this infrastructure [1]. The NLP algorithm for pulmonary nodule identification in radiology reports has demonstrated 96% sensitivity and 92% specificity, with an expanded version capturing laterality, lobe, size, attenuation, edge, and calcification reportedly above 95% for both [1]. These performance characteristics are adequate for identifying screening-detected nodules from unstructured text across all three sites. The exposure instruments are externally validated: the Gagne combined Charlson/Elixhauser comorbidity score achieved a c-statistic of 0.860 for 30-day mortality prediction [5], and the Segal claims-based frailty indicator, while developed in a different population (Cardiovascular Health Study, AUC 0.75), is a published instrument with established predictive validity for hospitalization, disability, and death [9]. The UM-LCS microsimulation is an established CISNET model that has been used in comparative screening analyses, including the Caverly et al. preference-sensitivity study that the proposal explicitly builds upon [1] [7].

**Realistic data access and coordinating structure.** Retrospective data covering 2016–2019 are already available at project start, reducing dependence on prospective accrual. All three sites have integrated LDCT screening into routine care with distinct but complementary identification mechanisms [1]. The data coordinating center at KPSC, led by Gould and Shen with additional statistical input from Fan, provides centralized oversight for capture, mapping, transfer, and Aims 1–2 analyses [1]. Sources include structured EHR exports, NLP, ICD-9/10 and CPT/HCPCS codes, radiology and pathology reports, tumor registries, mortality data, census linkage for area-level socioeconomic measures, and Medicare linkage to capture care outside participating systems [1].

**Two manageable limitations.** First, functional limitation indicators (FRI) are available only at KPSC and VA, representing approximately 80% of the cohort; MUSC lacks the necessary claims and EHR indicators [1]. The proposal plans multiple imputation with ten datasets and inverse-probability weighting sensitivity analyses [1]. These are standard approaches under a missing-at-random assumption, but FRI missingness at MUSC is structural—the entire site lacks the data—and MUSC serves a distinct safety-net population with potentially different functional limitation patterns. The proposal does not discuss whether the imputation model can validly bridge this structural gap, and FRI is a central exposure for all three aims. This constrains the functional limitation analyses but does not prevent them, since comorbidity and pulmonary function measures remain available across all sites.

Second, the proposal states that cohort-derived estimates will “refine” UM-LCS model parameters but does not specify the calibration or updating protocol [1]. Translating observational estimates into microsimulation inputs requires methodological choices—whether to replace, average, or Bayesian-update existing trial-based parameters, and how to handle parameter correlations. The UM-LCS team’s prior experience with similar calibration work, and the model’s established structure and documentation of data sources [1], provide a reasonable basis for expecting this to be surmountable, though the lack of specification is a moderate concern given that Aim 3 is the culminating aim.

**Realistic timeline and adequate power.** The five-year timeline is well-structured: regulatory approvals in the first half of Year 1, retrospective and prospective cohort collection through Year 3, Aims 1–2 analysis through Year 4, simulation refinement spanning Years 1–5, and manuscript preparation concentrated in Years 3–5 [1]. The staggered design permits parallel work on the observational cohort and the microsimulation. The power calculations are adequate for main comparisons (5% detectable risk difference in complications; 1.3–1.9% for false positives) [1]. The internal inconsistency between approximately 1,902 early-stage patients [1] and 902 incident cancers in the power calculation [1] appears to be a presentational error rather than a feasibility defect; the 2% cancer rate underlying the power analysis is plausible for a screening cohort.

In sum, the technical pathway and resources are adequate to deliver all three aims. The structural limitation in FRI coverage will constrain but not prevent the functional limitation analyses, and the simulation calibration protocol warrants further specification, but neither renders the project infeasible.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety are excellent (A, confidence A).** The study involves secondary analysis of existing clinical and administrative data without direct participant contact, so it presents negligible physical risk. The data security framework described is robust, proportionate to the sensitivity of the information, and aligned with standard institutional and federal requirements.

**Negligible physical risk.** The project will draw on electronic health records, claims, cancer registries, and mortality records from three health systems [1]. No experimental intervention, specimen collection, or direct interaction with patients is proposed. Physical risks and the ethical complexities that accompany interventional or qualitative research are therefore absent. The principal residual concern is privacy, and the plan addresses that concern with appropriate technical and procedural safeguards.

**Proportionate data protection.** Each participating institution will maintain a secure patient database. Names, medical record numbers, and dates of birth will be replaced with unique study identifiers and stored separately from the analytic dataset [1]. This standard de-identification practice reduces the risk of re-identification while retaining the capacity to link records across data sources. The coordinating center at KPSC will operate a HIPAA-compliant common data structure, providing a consistent institutional framework for protected health information across sites. Data transfer from MUSC and VA Portland to KPSC will use REDCap with encrypted transmission and authenticated, authorized access [1]. These are mature, widely adopted technologies with established security profiles. The centralized repository and the tiered access model—separating identifiers from study data and restricting analytic access to authorized team members—follow the principle of data minimization.

**Regulatory and equity considerations.** The proposal states that regulatory approvals, including IRB review, will be obtained at each study site [1]. The use of retrospective, de-identified records for secondary research is a context in which waivers of consent are commonly granted under the Common Rule, provided the research presents no more than minimal risk. The study population—adults aged 55–80 undergoing LDCT screening—includes groups historically underrepresented in screening research, including Black and Hispanic populations, patients in safety-net settings, and veterans [1]. Research on how multimorbidity modifies screening benefit in these populations can support more equitable screening decisions and reduce disparities driven by one-size-fits-all eligibility criteria. This potential to benefit populations with high comorbidity prevalence reinforces the study’s ethical standing.

No material ethical or safety concern is identified in the supplied materials.

## Management

**Management is good (B, confidence B).** The multidisciplinary team covers all necessary domains with demonstrated prior collaboration, the coordinating structure is appropriate, and the five-year schedule is well-organized. The provided materials do not include detailed CVs or a budget, which limits independent verification of capacity but does not constitute evidence of incapacity.

**Team expertise and prior collaboration.** Braithwaite leads with a background in epidemiology and cancer screening outcomes [1]. Gould contributes thoracic oncology and pulmonary nodule management; Silvestri adds thoracic surgery and screening program expertise. Both Gould and Silvestri are recognized authorities in lung cancer screening quality, as evidenced by their co-authorship on the 2025 American College of Chest Physicians/American Thoracic Society policy update on high-quality LCS [12]. The simulation component draws on the CISNET modeling group, whose UM-LCS model has been used in comparative screening analyses and the Caverly et al. preference-sensitivity study [1]. Statistical leadership for the data coordinating center rests with Gould and Shen, with additional input from Fan [1]. Patient advocates are integrated for interpretation and dissemination, supporting the project’s commitment to patient-centered decision support [1].

**Centralized coordination and practical governance.** KPSC serves as the data coordinating center, overseeing capture, mapping, transfer, management, and analyses for Aims 1 and 2 [1]. The three contributing sites—KPSC, MUSC, and VA Portland—each extract data from their own EHR and claims systems using a common data dictionary implemented with available institutional tools [1]. The Watch the Spot common data model provides a pre-existing vocabulary of 13 element groups that has been used in prior multi-site work by this team [1]. Monthly conference calls and annual in-person meetings provide regular coordination touchpoints [1]. This structure is proportionate to a three-site study with a single analytic hub and does not require elaborate governance.

**Well-sequenced five-year schedule.** The timeline is realistic and well-organized [1]. Regulatory approvals and subcontracts occupy the first half of Year 1. Retrospective and prospective cohort collection runs through Year 3, with database pooling, linkage, and Aims 1–2 analysis extending through Year 4. The simulation refinement for Aim 3 begins in the second half of Year 1 and continues through Year 5, permitted by the availability of retrospective data (2016–2019) at project start and the model’s existing structure. Manuscript preparation is concentrated in Years 3–5. The staggered design allows parallel work on observational analyses and simulation, reducing idle periods.

**Documented limitations and expected products.** The proposal text does not include investigator CVs or an itemized budget, which limits independent verification of individual track records and resource adequacy. However, the described roles align with published expertise, and the proposed activities—data extraction, statistical analysis, and simulation modeling—are supported by the documented infrastructure and instruments. The expected products are clearly specified: a harmonized clinical cohort, subgroup-specific estimates of screening harms and benefits, refined simulation models, and evidence supporting screening discussions and future guideline development [1]. These map directly to the three aims and the five-year timeline, providing a clear accountability framework for project management.

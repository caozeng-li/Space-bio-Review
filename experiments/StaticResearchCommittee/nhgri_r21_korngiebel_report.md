# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project aims to determine patient preferences and needs for the electronic return of genetic test results through an EHR patient portal and to translate those findings into iteratively tested, patient-facing report prototypes. The planned work is structured as a sequential, mixed-methods investigation: qualitative interviews with 80 participants to explore concern thresholds and delivery-mode preferences across different genetic and non-genetic test results (Aims 1–2), followed by the user-centered design and three-round cognitive usability testing of six to eight electronic prototypes with 84 portal users and non-users (Aim 3). The most valuable expected knowledge is the set of patient-validated prototype templates—including both Epic-constrained and platform-agnostic "blue-sky" designs—and a concurrently refined ethical Points to Consider framework, which together will provide actionable, empirically grounded guidance for health systems implementing genomic medicine under information-blocking rules.

The proposal is supported by a well-motivated practical problem, a coherent integration of qualitative bioethics with user-centered design, strong engagement with an underserved safety-net population, and a capable investigative team embedded in a major academic health system. These strengths principally underpin the judgement. The main limitations are the modest increment in novelty for the interview-based needs-assessment aims beyond the PI's already-published 2022 study, and a sample-size rationale for Aim 3 that overstates the problem-discovery rate supported by its cited reference. These constraints limit the definitiveness of the comparative claims but do not fundamentally prevent the study from generating useful formative design knowledge.

Weighing the supported value against the identified limitations, the project represents a coherent and worthwhile formative investigation. The key contribution from the prototype development phase is likely to yield concrete, testable design knowledge that extends the field beyond the needs-assessment stage. This benefit offsets the modest novelty in the interview aims and the manageable overstatement of the usability sample-size rationale, which limits precision but not the core inference. The trade-off results in a plan that is sufficient for its stated R21 goals, with the clear understanding that subsequent implementation and clinical outcome studies will be needed to build on these findings.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**Scientific value: good.** The project asks a well-motivated and genuinely practical question: how should patient-facing electronic reports for genetic test results be designed to meet patients' information needs, respect their preferences for delivery mode, and serve populations with differing levels of portal access and health literacy? The question's urgency is well established by the 21st Century Cures Act mandate for rapid electronic release of clinical results, and by emerging evidence that unmediated genetic-result release has caused distress, misinterpretation, and in rare cases clinically consequential misunderstandings. Lowe et al. (2025) documented genetic counselors reporting patient panic and, in one instance, a family nearly pursuing an unnecessary radical nephrectomy after misinterpreting a variant of uncertain significance released before counseling [1]. Yet the same survey also identified benefits: earlier reassurance, patient empowerment, and more efficient workflows [1]. The proposal positions its work at precisely this tension between transparency and the risk of harm from unmediated delivery.

The attainable knowledge falls into three connected categories. First, Aims 1 and 2 will produce qualitative evidence on the thresholds at which different genetic results should be accompanied or preceded by personal contact, what content patients consider essential, and how these preferences vary across result types—extending the PI's prior published findings by separating the genetic-versus-non-genetic comparison (Aim 1) from within-category comparisons across four genetic result types (Aim 2) [2]. Second, and most distinctively, Aim 3 will produce six to eight iteratively tested electronic report prototypes, spanning both Epic-constrained and unconstrained blue-sky formats, with usability evidence from 42 portal users and 42 non-users [2]. These prototypes provide concrete, testable design knowledge that moves beyond general recommendations toward implementable templates [2]. Third, the Points to Consider ethical framework will be refined throughout through empirical input, yielding an evidence-informed structure organized around beneficence, autonomy, and justice that ties design choices to principled justifications [2].

The value of these outputs is partly moderated by the prior-work landscape. The PI's 2022 interview study with 59 patients already identified patient preferences for summary coversheets, clinician involvement in all electronic returns, smartphone-friendly design, clear next-steps language, and explicit encouragement to contact providers [3]. That study also applied a three-tier concern framework and compared genetic with non-genetic result experiences within a single interview protocol [3]. The new Aims refine this architecture—adding a hemochromatosis category, deliberately sampling negative-result recipients, and separating the comparative questions into distinct aims—but the conceptual advance is incremental [2]. The proposal's distinctive scientific contribution therefore rests primarily on Aim 3: the translation of needs-assessment findings into interactive prototypes, tested across three iterative rounds with a user/non-user comparison that prior studies have not undertaken. That integrated pipeline, and the deliberate inclusion of a safety-net clinic population through Harborview [2], gives the work practical relevance that a needs-assessment study alone would not achieve.

Given the R21 mechanism's exploratory character, the attainable descriptive knowledge—patient-validated report templates and an evidence-informed ethical framework—represents a useful advance, even if subsequent clinical and implementation studies will be needed to evaluate effects on patient understanding, engagement, and health outcomes [2].

## Innovation

**Innovation: moderate.** The project's most original contribution is the translation of qualitative needs-assessment findings into interactively testable genetic-result report prototypes that span both Epic-constrained and platform-agnostic blue-sky formats, evaluated in three iterative usability rounds with a deliberate comparison between portal users and non-users. This integrated pipeline extends beyond any single study currently in the literature. The incremental advance over the PI's already-published work is much smaller for the interview-based needs-assessment aims, which refine rather than substantially reconceptualize the prior 2022 findings.

**Aims 1 and 2: incremental refinement of prior needs-assessment work.** The genealogical relationship between the present proposal and Korngiebel and West (2022) is clear and constrains the novelty attributable to Aims 1 and 2. That earlier study already recruited 59 eCare users who had received genetic test results, applied a three-tier concern framework encompassing pharmacogenetic, blood-coagulation, alpha-thalassemia, and heritable cancer-risk results, and simultaneously explored comparisons with non-genetic result experiences within a single interview protocol [3]. Its findings—that patients wanted simple summary coversheets alongside detailed reports, clinician involvement in all electronic returns, smartphone-friendly design, clear next-steps language, and explicit encouragement to contact providers—directly anticipated the domains the current Aims 1 and 2 propose to investigate [3].

The new proposal reconfigures this architecture: the genetic-versus-non-genetic comparison moves into a separate Aim 1, the between-category comparison is expanded to four explicitly named categories in Aim 2, and hemochromatosis is introduced as a moderate-concern category absent from the 2022 framework [2]. These are genuine refinements that permit deeper exploration of within-patient comparisons in Aim 1 and a structured sampling of negative-result experiences in Aim 2. But the underlying question—what do patients need and prefer, and how do those preferences vary by result type?—remains the same, and the conceptual machinery of concern thresholds mapping onto delivery-mode preferences is inherited directly from the prior study.

**Aim 3: a distinctive methodological contribution.** Where the proposal makes a more distinctive move is in Aim 3. No prior study of which evidence is available has combined qualitative needs assessment with multi-round usability testing of genetic-result prototypes that systematically varies both the design format (Epic-style versus blue-sky) and the participant's portal experience (users versus non-users) across three design iterations. The Trieu et al. (2025) MyCancerGene study developed a digital genetic health portal with extensive patient and provider input, but focused on a single cancer-genetics platform and did not compare constrained and unconstrained design formats or examine usability through successive prototype generations [4]. Pollard et al. (2023) developed a hereditary-cancer portal for family communication with 25 pre-design interviews and subsequent user feedback, but with a smaller testing sample and without the methodological contrast between EHR-embedded and blue-sky designs [5].

The inclusion of 42 non-users in Aim 3 addresses an equity limitation that the PI's own 2022 study identified as a gap. That study noted that its participant cohort was largely female and White, that all participants were fluent English speakers who had used the patient portal, and that underrepresented groups may tend to be non-users [3]. The deliberate recruitment of non-users through Harborview's safety-net Family Medicine Clinic [2] converts this post-hoc limitation into a prospective design feature, permitting the study to examine whether electronic return can substitute for personal delivery or should remain supplementary from the perspective of patients who have neither developed portal habits nor been primed by existing result-presentation conventions [2].

**Ethical framework.** The Points to Consider framework is useful but not strongly original in its initial formulation. Its three organizing principles—beneficence/nonmaleficence, respect for autonomy, and justice and equity [2]—are the familiar pillars of bioethical analysis. The framework's innovation will depend on whether the empirical findings from Aims 1 through 3 add specific, actionable content that goes beyond the questions any thoughtful designer might ask. The proposal appropriately commits to refining the framework iteratively [2], and if executed well, the resulting document could provide a practically useful, evidence-informed ethics tool. At this pre-execution stage, however, the framework represents a structured approach rather than a conceptual advance.

The moderate rating reflects the weighted contribution across the three aims. The prototype-development and user/non-user comparison in Aim 3 is genuinely original for this field. The refinement of the needs-assessment architecture in Aims 1 and 2 is real but incremental, and the initial ethical framework is methodical rather than conceptually novel.

## Rigor

**Rigor: good.** The overall design is appropriately specified for an exploratory, formative investigation and supports the intended inferences about patient preferences and prototype usability, though the Aim 3 sample-size rationale overstates the quantitative precision supported by its cited source.

**Aim 1 design: within-subjects comparison of genetic and non-genetic results.** The qualitative core of Aims 1 and 2 follows well-established procedures. Aim 1 uses a within-subjects design—interviewing the same 40 participants about both their genetic and non-genetic result experiences—which is well suited to eliciting perceived thresholds and contrasts [2]. The 40-participant target comfortably exceeds established saturation benchmarks: Guest, Bunce, and Johnson (2006) found saturation within 12 interviews, with 92% of codes identified by that point [6]. The proposal commits to continuing recruitment until diversity targets are met and the team judges that major themes have reached saturation, up to the planned maxima [2], providing a principled rather than mechanical stopping rule.

**Aim 2 design: four-category comparison within genetic results.** Aim 2 samples 10 participants in each of four result categories—pharmacogenomic, hemochromatosis, inherited colorectal-cancer risk, and negative results—providing conceptually grounded variation in concern and complexity [2]. The per-category sample of 10 is sufficient to generate descriptive themes within categories and suggest contrasts. However, Hagaman and Wutich (2017) indicate that 20–40 interviews may be needed to identify cross-cutting metathemes with confidence [7], so between-category comparisons will be suggestive rather than definitive. This constraint is appropriate to an R21 study that explicitly treats the categories as revisable starting points to be tested and refined through participants' accounts [2].

**Qualitative analysis procedures.** The analytical approach is rigorous by qualitative standards. Directed content and thematic analysis will be led by Korngiebel and Robins, using dual independent coders who will periodically clarify coding and resolve disagreements by consensus [2]. Atlas.ti will support transcript organization, coding, and codebook development. Team and advisory board review of results, with the ethical framework refined in light of emerging findings, provides a structured credibility check [2].

**Aim 3: iterative usability testing with user/non-user comparison.** The iterative usability design is logically structured: generative findings from Aims 1 and 2 inform prototype creation, followed by three rounds of cognitive usability testing with revision between rounds [2]. The inclusion of 42 eCare users and 42 non-users, equally divided across rounds with no participant repeating [2], permits the study to examine whether electronic return can substitute for personal delivery or should remain supplementary [2]. Counterbalancing prototype presentation order and the think-aloud cognitive interview method follow standard usability engineering practice [2].

**Overstated sample-size rationale for Aim 3.** The sample-size rationale, however, contains a material overstatement. The proposal claims a minimum problem-discovery rate above 98% and a mean of 99.6% for each participant type—conditions that, per Faulkner (2003), would require approximately 40 users per type cumulatively without design changes [8]. Since prototypes are revised between rounds, problem discovery is not cumulative across rounds for the same interface, because design changes between rounds alter the interface under test. With 14 participants per type per round, the expected mean discovery rate per round interpolates closer to 97%, with a plausible minimum around 90%.

This overstatement does not undermine the design's fundamental adequacy for its formative purpose. Fourteen participants per type per round substantially exceeds the conventional five-user benchmark, the three-round iterative structure ensures major problems are identified and remedied before the next round, and external consultant heuristic walkthroughs after round 2 add independent expert evaluation [2]. The quantitative precision claims for problem discovery should simply not be accepted at face value; the design can nevertheless deliver the intended formative inference about prototype usability with reasonable confidence.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility: good.** The project's largely qualitative methods, modest technical demands, and strong institutional embedding at UW Medicine make the planned work realistically achievable within the two-year R21 timeframe. The principal feasibility considerations are recruitment logistics across three sequential aims, prototype development capacity, and participant safety—all of which are adequately addressed in the proposal.

**Sequential dependency and schedule.** The sequential dependency among aims is the central operational constraint: Aim 1 analysis informs Aim 2 protocols, and both Aims 1 and 2 together supply the generative research for Aim 3 prototype creation [2]. The schedule allocates approximately two quarters each for Aims 1 and 2 data collection and analysis, followed by four quarters for prototype development and three iterative testing rounds [2]. This is tight but logically coherent for a formative study whose prototypes are intended to be low-fidelity enough to permit rapid revision between rounds. The saturation-based stopping rule—recruitment continues until diversity targets are met and major themes stabilize, up to the planned maxima [2]—provides useful flexibility: if themes stabilize early, schedule slack can be redirected to later phases; if not, the full sample can be pursued without redesign.

**Recruitment infrastructure.** Recruitment draws on substantial institutional resources. UW Medicine's clinical and translational informatics services, the eCare team, Laboratory Medicine, and Medical Genetics are all committed to participant identification [2]. The 12-month recency requirement for Aim 2 results [2] constrains the eligible pool but ensures adequate recall, and UW Medicine's size as a large academic health system makes retrospective identification of patients across the four result categories credible. The explicit provision to prioritize underrepresented groups through targeted informatics searches, direct clinic approaches, and same-day data collection [2] addresses the foreseeable challenge that minority patients may be disproportionately non-users. Harborview's Family Medicine Clinic provides access to a safety-net population with lower portal enrollment and greater minority representation—important specifically for recruiting the 42 Aim 3 non-users [2].

**Team expertise and prototype development.** Personnel and expertise are well matched to the work. Korngiebel has documented training in formative research, electronic prototyping, and usability testing; Robins contributes extensive qualitative methods experience; Fullerton provides genomic bioethics; and Amendola adds genetic counseling and clinical sequencing expertise [2]. The advisory board includes clinical genetics (Jarvik), laboratory medicine informatics (Shirts), ambulatory IT (Fellner), safety-net family medicine (Chen), and external biomedical informatics (Peterson) [2]. External prototype consultants from institutions with EHR-mediated genomics experience (Welch, Williams) supplement the local expertise and will conduct heuristic walkthroughs after round 2 [2].

The prototype development pathway is described at a level sufficient to judge feasibility: interview findings will inform content and visual ideas, which will be rendered as six to eight interactive prototypes spanning Epic-style and blue-sky formats [2]. The proposal does not specify the prototyping software, but the prototypes are described as interactive on a laptop [2] and the PI has relevant training. This unspecified detail does not constitute a critical gap. A graduate assistant is allocated to prototype revision between rounds under Korngiebel's supervision [2]—a task for which a capable master's or doctoral student in health informatics, human-computer interaction, or a related field would be adequate.

**Scope limitations.** The English-only restriction [2] is a scope limitation rather than a feasibility obstacle; it is appropriately framed as the first step before subsequent multilingual work, given that the eCare portal is currently English-only at UW Medicine. The project's minimal-risk profile and clearly specified safety provisions, discussed under ethics and safety, further support the feasibility judgement. The main residual risk—recruitment pace for Aim 2's narrow result-category strata with the 12-month window—is mitigated by the committed institutional resources and does not threaten the project's core deliverability.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety: good.** The project is minimal-risk research, and the ethical and safety provisions are proportionate, complete, and appropriately tailored to the study's qualitative interview and usability-testing design.

**Risk identification and mitigation.** The proposal identifies four material risks: discomfort discussing inherited risk or sensitive health experiences, concern about audio recording, potential recognition by others during waiting-room recruitment, and inadvertent disclosure of information about relatives [2]. Each is addressed with a concrete mitigation. Participants may stop the interview or decline recording at any time and will be directed to assistance if distressed. Recruitment materials and consent discussions will explain all procedures, confidentiality protections, voluntary participation, and the absence of direct clinical benefit [2]. Participants will be explicitly reminded not to provide identifying information about third parties; if such details are supplied, they will be removed from recordings and transcripts [2]. The recognition risk during clinic-based recruitment is inherent to the setting and is managed through the same confidentiality framework rather than eliminated—a reasonable accommodation given that Harborview's Family Medicine Clinic is essential for reaching non-users and underrepresented groups [2].

**Regulatory pathway.** The plan to seek a HIPAA waiver for access to information used in identifying eligible patients and an IRB waiver of written consent documentation [2] is well justified for a retrospective identification and interview study, where written consent forms would themselves create unnecessary identifying links. The waiver-of-documentation approach does not compromise the consent process, which remains substantive: the consent discussion covers procedures, confidentiality, voluntary nature, risks, and the absence of benefit [2].

**Data management.** Data management provisions are clearly specified and proportionate. Transcripts will be de-identified; information required for incentive administration will be stored separately; study data will reside on secure servers or password-protected computers; physical materials will be locked with access restricted to the study team; and all data will be destroyed at study close [2]. These measures collectively address the main confidentiality concern—that genetic and health-related interview content could be linked to identifiable individuals—without over-engineering a solution for what is a study that generates de-identified transcripts rather than clinical data.

**Context-specific considerations.** Two features strengthen the assessment. First, the study does not generate incidental clinical findings and does not enroll children [2]; these are correctly characterized rather than overlooked. Second, the English-only recruitment [2] is an acknowledged scope limitation rather than an ethical blind spot, with later multilingual work explicitly anticipated. No ethical violation or inadequacy arises from conducting formative work in a single language when the patient portal itself is currently English-only at UW Medicine. The combination of substantive consent procedures, explicit risk acknowledgement with specific mitigations, a defensible regulatory waiver strategy, and standard secure-data practices constitutes a complete and appropriate ethics and safety plan for the proposed activities.

## Management

**Management: good.** The project's management plan is proportionate, logically structured, and supported by a well-qualified team whose collective expertise covers the domains required for each aim. The two-year schedule respects the sequential dependencies among aims, the advisory and consulting arrangements provide appropriate oversight and external perspective, and the allocation of responsibilities is clearly specified.

**Core team and responsibilities.** The investigative team is compact but covers the necessary disciplinary range. Korngiebel leads with documented training in formative research, electronic prototyping, and usability testing—the three methodological pillars of the project [2]. Robins contributes extensive qualitative methods experience, essential for the directed content and thematic analysis planned for Aims 1 and 2 [2]. Fullerton provides genomic bioethics expertise that directly informs the Points to Consider framework and its iterative refinement [2]. Amendola adds clinical genetic-counseling and sequencing experience, grounding the work in the practical realities of result disclosure [2]. A graduate assistant is allocated to prototype revision between usability rounds under Korngiebel's supervision [2]—a task appropriately scoped for a supervised trainee.

**Advisory structure.** The advisory structure is unusually well developed for an R21 project. The advisory board includes clinical genetics (Jarvik), laboratory medicine informatics (Shirts), ambulatory IT representing the eCare platform (Fellner), safety-net family medicine at Harborview (Chen), and external biomedical informatics from Vanderbilt (Peterson) [2]. The board's twice-yearly meetings and as-needed consultation [2] provide a structured but flexible mechanism for addressing issues that cross the clinical–informatics–equity boundary. Two external prototype consultants (Welch from MUSC, Williams from Geisinger) bring independent expertise from other institutions with EHR-mediated genomics programs and will conduct heuristic walkthroughs after Aim 3 round 2 [2]. A separate mentoring committee (Burke, Tarczy-Hornoch, Carline) provides bioethics and biomedical informatics oversight [2]. The distinction between advisory board, prototype consultants, and mentoring committee is sensible: each group has a defined scope that does not duplicate the others, and none creates a competing approval pathway that could delay the work.

**Schedule logic.** The two-year schedule sequences the three aims in a logical dependency chain: Aim 1 interviews and analysis occupy quarters 1–3, Aim 2 interviews and analysis quarters 3–5, prototype creation and round 1 testing quarters 5–6, and the remaining two iterative rounds with external review quarters 6–8 [2]. The overlap between Aim 1 analysis and Aim 2 data collection is efficient without risking protocol incoherence, because Aim 2 interview guides will be piloted and informed by emerging Aim 1 findings [2]. The schedule's tightness for the prototype phase—approximately two quarters for initial prototype creation and testing, then one quarter each for the remaining two rounds—is realistic for the low-fidelity, iteratively revisable prototypes described [2]. The built-in piloting of interview protocols before each aim's data collection [2] and of the usability protocol [2] represents a practical quality-assurance measure that supports schedule adherence by catching problems early.

The proposal does not specify the prototyping software platform or the graduate assistant's exact qualifications. For an R21 narrative, these are within normal limits: the prototyping task is to create interactive mock-ups navigable on a laptop [2], a capability well within current tools and the PI's documented training, and the graduate assistant role is supervised and limited to revision rather than independent design. The management plan as documented provides the coordination structure, expertise, schedule logic, and oversight mechanisms to deliver the three aims within the proposed two-year period.

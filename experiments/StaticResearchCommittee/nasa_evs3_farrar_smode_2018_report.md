# Project review

## Overall assessment

The S-MODE (Submesoscale Ocean Dynamics and Vertical Transport) proposal investigates the hypothesis that kilometer-scale ocean fronts and eddies make important contributions to the vertical exchange of heat, carbon, nutrients, and other climate and biological variables in the upper ocean. The team proposes a coordinated airborne and oceanographic field campaign offshore of San Francisco, combining direct in situ measurements from a research vessel, Wave Gliders, Seagliders, surface drifters, and Lagrangian floats with wide-swath airborne remote sensing of surface currents, winds, sea surface temperature, ocean color, and directional wave spectra. The planned work involves a two-week pilot campaign followed by two 25-day intensive operations periods in spring and fall, capturing contrasting mixed-layer depths, forcing, and stratification.

The investigation has four science goals. Goals 1 and 3 constitute the threshold mission: quantifying the three-dimensional structure of submesoscale features responsible for vertical exchange, and relating remotely sensed surface currents to the subsurface currents that actually drive transport. Goals 2 and 4, supported by the baseline mission’s additional sampling and seasonal contrast, test air-sea interaction mechanisms at these scales and, most exploratorily, attempt to construct a remote-sensing proxy for vertical biogeochemical flux using surface current divergence and remotely sensed tracers [1].

The most valuable expected knowledge is a benchmark observational dataset that can discriminate between competing high-resolution model predictions of submesoscale dynamics and improve the interpretation of surface measurements from aircraft and satellites. This directly addresses a critical gap between a rich theoretical and modeling literature and the scarcity of systematic observations at the relevant scales. Simulations produce abundant submesoscale structure yet disagree on fundamental properties: the relative prominence of fronts versus eddies, the seasonal contrast, and the magnitude of air-sea flux modification [1]. The McWilliams (2016) review confirmed that “so far little has been measured for SMCs because of the sampling difficulties” and identified numerous compelling unresolved issues. S-MODE’s coordinated airborne and in situ measurements would produce the first dataset that simultaneously resolves surface current structure from airborne DopplerScatt, subsurface density and velocity from a nested in situ array, and direct vertical velocity from Lagrangian floats at submesoscale fronts, with the remote-sensing context needed to connect these measurements to satellite observables.

The experimental design is a major strength. The nested sampling strategy, with DopplerScatt providing synoptic surface current context over a \~75 × 300 km domain and in situ assets concentrating on individual features, is well-matched to the problem [1]. The instruments are mature (TRL ≥ 6), with prior flight heritage and demonstrated performance that exceeds the science requirements with comfortable margins [1]. The management structure is well-defined, the timeline includes prudent contingency, and the risk register addresses the most probable operational threats.

The principal limitations are scientifically inherent rather than design flaws: the effective gradient accuracy of DopplerScatt in marginal wind conditions, where external analysis suggests divergence estimation may require wind speeds above 6 m s⁻¹ for useful accuracy [2]; the acknowledged challenge of separating submesoscale balanced motions from internal waves, for which the proposal asserts that sustained sampling suffices but does not specify the analysis methods; and the statistical robustness of the exploratory flux proxy in Goal 4 given the limited number of feature realizations. These are genuine scientific uncertainties that reflect the difficulty of the problem, and the proposal treats them with appropriate honesty. The threshold/baseline structure provides a prudent descope option that protects the core descriptive and methodological contributions of Goals 1 and 3.

Weighing the strengths and limitations, the current proposal presents a compelling plan that is well-supported and fit for purpose. The high scientific value of the question and the originality of the multi-platform approach are matched by a rigorous design that credibly addresses its core goals, and an implementation plan that is realistic given the demonstrated instrument maturity and team experience. **The current proposal is suitable for support.**

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**S-MODE addresses a well-posed and scientifically important question that sits at the intersection of physical oceanography, air-sea interaction, and satellite remote sensing, with the potential to deliver a benchmark observational dataset of lasting value.**

The central hypothesis — that kilometer-scale submesoscale fronts and eddies make important contributions to the vertical exchange of heat, carbon, nutrients, and other climate and biological variables in the upper ocean — is strongly motivated by the extensive high-resolution modeling literature. Simulations produce abundant submesoscale structure, yet they disagree on fundamental properties: the relative prominence of fronts versus eddies, the seasonal contrast in submesoscale activity, and the magnitude of air-sea flux modification at these scales [1]. These disagreements reflect the sensitivity of model predictions to numerical damping, grid spacing, boundary-layer turbulence parameterizations, and air-sea exchange formulations at grid spacings approaching 100 m, where explicitly simulated structures overlap scales represented by turbulence closures [1]. The proposal correctly identifies that “competing simulations disagree about how submesoscale activity responds” to seasonal changes in mixed-layer depth, winds, and heat flux [1], motivating observations that can test the structure, evolution, and exchange processes directly.

The knowledge S-MODE promises to generate is both descriptive and practical. For Goals 1 and 3, which constitute the threshold mission, the investigation would produce the first dataset that simultaneously resolves surface current structure from airborne DopplerScatt, subsurface density and velocity from a nested in situ array, and direct vertical velocity from Lagrangian floats at submesoscale fronts, with the remote-sensing context needed to connect these measurements to satellite observables [1]. Prior campaigns each captured important pieces — the D’Asaro et al. (2018) demonstration of convergence with float-measured vertical velocities of 1–2 cm s⁻¹ at a single frontal feature [3], the LatMix two-vessel survey that obtained the first in situ estimates of the full velocity gradient tensor at O(1 km) [4], and earlier multi-platform studies showing that coordinated SST, drifter, ship, and float observations could resolve a frontal eddy and its subduction [1] — but none combined the wide-swath airborne current mapping, the nested autonomous platform array, and the multi-season coverage proposed here. This observational gap was explicitly noted in the McWilliams (2016) review.

The practical value of the investigation extends beyond the campaign. Goal 3 directly addresses how radar-measured surface currents, which sample the top few centimeters, relate to the currents within and beneath the surface boundary layer that actually drive vertical transport [1]. This relationship is essential for interpreting measurements from current-measuring satellites, including the planned NASA/CNES SWOT mission, and the proposed comparison — using dense drifter arrays bridging the radar footprint to upper-60-cm currents and ADCP profiles extending to deeper layers — would produce transferable knowledge for the satellite oceanography community. The benchmark dataset, by co-locating radar currents, drifter currents, ADCP profiles, CTD surveys, optical measurements, and direct vertical velocity, would enable model validation at a level not previously possible.

Even the most exploratory goal, Goal 4, carries significant potential value: if the approach of constructing a vertical flux proxy from surface current divergence and remotely sensed tracers proves viable even under a restricted set of conditions, it would open a new avenue for estimating biogeochemical fluxes from satellite measurements [1]. The proposal’s honest characterization of this as “the most exploratory goal” and its plan to test “statistical stability, convergence, and sensitivity to instrumental noise” [1] reflect an appropriate balance of ambition and caution. The threshold/baseline structure ensures that the core descriptive and methodological contributions of Goals 1 and 3 are protected even if the more ambitious flux proxy development faces challenges, which is prudent given the genuine scientific uncertainties involved.

## Innovation

**S-MODE is genuinely original in its coordinated, multi-platform approach to observing submesoscale dynamics; its combination of wide-swath airborne current mapping with a nested in situ array has no precedent at the proposal date.**

The originality resides primarily in the integration of measurement capabilities that have previously been demonstrated only in isolation. Several prior studies each captured important but partial pieces of the submesoscale puzzle. The D’Asaro et al. (2018) Gulf of Mexico campaign demonstrated that a Lagrangian float could measure sustained vertical velocities of 1–2 cm s⁻¹ at a submesoscale front, with independent confirmation from the float’s upward-looking ADCP [3]. This confirmed one prediction of submesoscale-resolving models: that vertical velocities at fronts can reach O(10⁻² m s⁻¹), far larger than the 10–100 m day⁻¹ typically inferred by indirect methods [3]. However, that study covered a single \~20 × 20 km region over approximately one week, without the airborne remote-sensing context that would enable generalization across multiple features and conditions. The LatMix experiment obtained the first in situ estimates of the full velocity gradient tensor at O(1 km) using synchronous two-ship ADCP sampling, yielding near-surface vorticity distributions in excellent agreement with model predictions for active submesoscale turbulence [4]. Yet LatMix similarly lacked the synoptic spatial coverage provided by airborne current mapping.

What S-MODE adds is the simultaneous deployment of all these elements within a single, strategically designed field program. DopplerScatt provides daily, wide-swath (\~25 km per pass) maps of surface current vectors, wind vectors, and their gradients — vorticity, divergence, and strain — over a \~50 × 100 km region at \~2 km effective resolution [1]. These airborne maps serve as the targeting tool for concentrated in situ operations but also constitute a scientific product in their own right: the first systematic airborne Doppler scatterometer observations of submesoscale current structure. The nested in situ array — Wave Gliders in a 1 km square measuring horizontal divergence, Lagrangian floats measuring direct vertical velocity, Seagliders providing mesoscale context to 1000 m, and a research vessel repeating \~5 km surveys around tracked floats — simultaneously resolves the three-dimensional density, velocity, and optical structure of individual features [1]. No previous campaign has combined these measurement types with the spatial coverage, temporal repetition, and seasonal contrast proposed here.

The investigation is also methodologically original in its attempt to construct and validate a remote-sensing proxy for vertical biogeochemical flux. Goal 4 proposes to use surface current divergence derived from DopplerScatt, multiplied by an in situ-calibrated vertical length scale, as an estimate of vertical velocity, and then to form a flux covariance with remotely sensed chlorophyll or particulate organic carbon [1]. This is a creative, if exploratory, idea. The proposal is appropriately cautious about its prospects — it is described as “the most exploratory goal” — but the very attempt to develop a testable relationship between a radar-derived quantity (divergence) and a biogeochemically relevant quantity (vertical flux of carbon or chlorophyll) represents a genuine methodological contribution, whether or not the proxy proves robust across all conditions.

The seasonal contrast between spring and fall campaigns adds a further dimension of originality. Submesoscale-resolving models disagree about the seasonal cycle of activity [1], and the proposal’s decision to sample under both shallow and deep mixed-layer conditions provides explicit experimental leverage on this model-discrimination question. This is not merely an extension of sampling duration — it is a targeted test of a specific model uncertainty, enabled by the two-season design.

## Rigor

**The S-MODE experimental design is carefully constructed and well-matched to its central hypothesis; the nested multi-platform strategy, complementary vertical velocity methods, and explicit treatment of measurement requirements together provide a credible route to quantifying the three-dimensional structure of submesoscale features. The principal limitations are genuine scientific challenges that the proposal acknowledges with appropriate honesty rather than design defects.**

### Nested sampling architecture

The nested sampling architecture is the design’s strongest feature and is logically structured to address the problem at its inherent scales. Aircraft define the mesoscale-to-submesoscale context over a \~75 × 300 km domain, with daily DopplerScatt maps of surface currents and winds at \~2 km effective resolution guiding in situ assets to concentrate on individual fronts and eddies [1]. Four Seagliders traversing racetrack patterns to 1000 m provide mesoscale context at approximately 3 km resolution every two days [1]. At the finest scale, four Wave Gliders in a nominal 1 km square measure horizontal divergence through the upper 70 m, while the ship repeats \~5 km surveys around a tracked Lagrangian float every 1–2 hours, obtaining nominal 1 km horizontal sampling and profiles to about 100 m [1]. This tiered design — from the 75 × 300 km aircraft domain down to the 1 km Wave Glider array — builds explicitly on demonstrated capabilities from earlier campaigns, including the LatMix two-vessel survey and the D’Asaro et al. (2018) float demonstration [4] [3].

The use of two independent methods for estimating vertical velocity — direct measurement by Lagrangian floats and inference from Wave Glider horizontal divergence — is a particular strength that provides internal cross-validation [1]. The Lagrangian float approach has demonstrated accuracy of 0.001 m s⁻¹, well below the required 0.003 m s⁻¹, and the float ADCP profile accuracy of 0.002 m s⁻¹ similarly satisfies the requirement [1] [3]. The divergence-based method integrates measured horizontal divergence from the 1 km Wave Glider square to estimate vertical velocity, with a required precision of 2 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹ that the traceability matrix shows is met — though not exceeded — by glider velocity accuracy of 0.02 m s⁻¹ over 1 km [1]. These complementary measurements address the problem from different spatial perspectives (point-following versus array-averaged), providing a more complete description than either method alone.

### DopplerScatt gradient detection and wind-speed dependence

DopplerScatt requires wind speeds above 2–3 m s⁻¹ for meaningful radar returns [5], and external analysis suggests that divergence estimation from Doppler scatterometry may require wind speeds above 6 m s⁻¹ for useful accuracy [2]. The proposal’s risk register acknowledges the risk of winds below 3 m s⁻¹ and mitigates it through seasonal scheduling and 200% campaign-duration margin [1], but the more stringent 6 m s⁻¹ threshold for divergence estimation is not addressed. Since Goal 1 requires current gradient accuracy of 4 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹ and Goal 4 depends on divergence-based flux estimation, low-wind periods during a 14–25 day IOP could limit the data available for these analyses. The Wineteer et al. (2020) validation work underscores the challenge: comparisons against independent current measurements show agreement better than 10 cm s⁻¹ but with direction differences at lower wind speeds [5].

### Separation of balanced motions from internal waves

The proposal explicitly recognizes that submesoscale motions overlap in scale with inertial currents and internal gravity waves, and that “a snapshot of small-scale variability is therefore insufficient to attribute an observed vertical velocity to submesoscale circulation” [1]. The proposed solution — sustained sampling over days — is a sound principle, and the modeling plan includes a control Lagrangian float outside the main frontal feature to provide context for internal-wave and tidal motions, as well as numerical simulations with tidal forcing [1]. However, the proposal does not specify the quantitative analysis methods by which balanced submesoscale motions will be separated from internal waves at the same spatial scales. The Pinkel (2008) study demonstrates that vortical and internal-wave contributions to shear can have distinct wavenumber-frequency signatures [6], but extracting this separation from a 14–25 day time series with intermittent spatial coverage is challenging. The proposal’s confidence that 10 days of daily sampling suffices “to separate dynamical processes” [1] is stated as an assertion rather than a justified conclusion from the planned observing-system simulation experiments.

### Statistical basis for Goal 4

Goal 4 is inherently exploratory. The proposed flux proxy — using surface current divergence D multiplied by an inferred vertical length scale h to estimate vertical velocity, then forming a flux covariance with remotely sensed tracers — involves a multi-step inference chain. The length scale h must be calibrated from in situ vertical velocity and radar divergence, and separately assessed in numerical models [1]. With three feature realizations in the threshold mission and six per season in the baseline [1], the statistical basis for establishing robust proxy relationships across varying frontal types, stratification, and forcing conditions remains uncertain. The proposal’s plan to test “statistical stability, convergence, and sensitivity to instrumental noise explicitly” [1] is the appropriate treatment for an idea at this stage, rather than a claim of definitive flux quantification.

### Role of models

The modeling plan provides partial mitigation for these uncertainties. Observing-system simulation experiments before the campaigns, using nested simulations from 2 km to 30 m non-hydrostatic resolution with both ROMS and MITgcm as complementary frameworks, will test sampling patterns, array dimensions, and potential aliasing [1]. This is an appropriate use of models to optimize the observational design before deployment and to predict expected signals against which measurements can be compared. However, the proposal acknowledges that “existing models only partially represent” the two-way coupling between turbulence and fronts at grid spacings approaching 100 m [1], which limits their utility as independent validators for the very processes the observations aim to constrain.

## Feasibility

**The S-MODE implementation plan is thorough, realistic, and well-supported by demonstrated instrument performance and institutional capability. All airborne instruments are mature, with substantial flight heritage and performance margins that comfortably exceed the science requirements. The baseline mission is achievable with the proposed resources; the threshold provides a prudent descope option that protects the core objectives.**

### Instrument readiness and performance margins

The instrument readiness is a decisive strength. All airborne payloads have prior flight heritage in their proposed configurations: DopplerScatt demonstrated wide-swath current and wind retrievals in the 2017 Gulf of Mexico campaign; PRISM has operated on the ER-2; MASS has participated in 16 campaigns over seven years; and MOSES has been used in two Gulf of Mexico campaigns [1]. The oceanographic team has extensive experience with Seagliders, Lagrangian floats, instrumented Wave Gliders, and underway CTD surveys [1]. The science traceability matrix [1] and detailed instrument performance table [1] establish that measured performance meets or exceeds requirements across all critical parameters: DopplerScatt achieves 0.0215 m s⁻¹ over 5 km against a 0.09 m s⁻¹ requirement; MOSES delivers 0.01 K noise-equivalent temperature difference against a 0.05 K requirement; and MASS lidar provides 2–4 cm per pulse against a 10 cm requirement.

The aircraft accommodation analysis [1] confirms positive margins on all parameters for all three payloads. The DopplerScatt/MOSES package on the King Air has 55% mass margin and 287% power margin; MASS on the Twin Otter has 127% mass margin and 1,300% power margin; and PRISM on the ER-2 has 33% duration margin and 387% power margin. The tightest margins are MASS duration (13%) and speed (35%) on the Twin Otter, but both remain positive and the slower platform speed is explicitly accounted for in the flight planning through a fast-sampling mode that allocates about two hours for MASS coverage of the intensive operations region [1]. Alternative aircraft are identified for each payload if the preferred platform is unavailable [1].

### Timeline and schedule contingency

The five-year timeline (July 2019–June 2024) is realistic given the campaign schedule: a two-week pilot in April 2020, followed by two 25-day intensive operations periods in April and September 2021, with backup dates providing substantial schedule flexibility [1]. The approximately one-year gap between pilot and first main campaign is explicitly allocated for calibration, algorithm improvement, and correction of interoperability problems [1], which is prudent and well-justified. The final two years (2022–2024) are dedicated to scientific analysis and publication [1]. The requested 79 ship days on an ocean-class UNOLS vessel appear adequate for the planned operations including transit, mobilization, and demobilization [1].

### Risk management and residual uncertainties

The risk register [1] identifies five operational risks with pre- and post-mitigation ratings. Weather risk (fog, clouds, rain, or winds below 3 m s⁻¹) carries the highest likelihood (25–60%) and moderate mission consequence, mitigated to low residual likelihood through seasonal scheduling and 200% campaign-duration margin. Instrument anomaly carries significant mission risk but at low likelihood (\<5%). The most consequential risk is airborne instrument performance (C5: operations failure or budget overrun), rated at 5–25% likelihood before mitigation, reduced to \<5% through ground testing, test flights, and advance procurement of replacement components. This reflects the reality that a major failure of the unique DopplerScatt system would fundamentally compromise the mission, but the mature TRL of all instruments makes this a low-probability event.

One gap in the risk register is the scenario where submesoscale activity in the California Current is anomalously weak during the campaign windows. The proposal notes that front prevalence is greatest in July–October and lowest in March–April [1]; the April and September campaigns are chosen to provide contrasting conditions, and satellite SST and ocean color will be used for pre-campaign assessment. However, no explicit threshold is stated for what constitutes sufficient submesoscale activity to proceed. This is a scientific uncertainty rather than an implementation defect — the 200% duration margin provides operational buffer — but it warrants acknowledgment as a residual concern affecting the expected number of feature realizations.

## Ethics and safety

**The S-MODE investigation raises no fundamental ethical concerns, and its environmental and operational risks are well-managed through reliance on established platform operating procedures and appropriate mitigation measures; the residual risk is low and proportionate to the scientific aims.**

The investigation does not involve human participants, animal subjects, or sensitive community engagement. The work consists entirely of physical oceanographic measurements from aircraft, a research vessel, and autonomous ocean platforms, together with seawater sampling for biogeochemical analysis. The ethical dimensions are therefore limited to environmental impact and operational safety, both of which the proposal addresses through standard and appropriate arrangements.

On environmental impact, the most direct consideration is the deployment of approximately 100 surface drifters per intensive operations period, together with smaller numbers during the pilot campaign [1]. The proposal specifies that these are CARTHE-style biodegradable drifters designed to degrade in the marine environment [1]. The drifter bodies are constructed from materials that break down over weeks to months, substantially reducing the persistent marine debris concern associated with conventional plastic-hulled drifters. This choice reflects a considered design decision appropriate to the deployment scale. The Lagrangian floats, Wave Gliders, and Seagliders are recovered at the end of each deployment, and any unrecovered platform would represent an isolated loss rather than systematic disposal. The seawater sampling program involves collection of water samples for chlorophyll-a, particulate and total organic carbon, oxygen, and nutrient analysis [1] — standard oceanographic measurements involving small sample volumes and no introduction of foreign substances to the ocean. Aircraft operations involve standard aviation fuel consumption and emissions, governed by NASA flight operating requirements [1].

Operational safety is addressed through the established governance frameworks of the platforms involved. The research vessel will operate under UNOLS safety standards, which include comprehensive protocols for shipboard operations, person-overboard prevention and recovery, and emergency response [1]. Aircraft operations will follow NASA flight safety review processes, with the NASA Airborne Science Program providing the oversight framework for the King Air, ER-2, and Twin Otter flights [1]. Early air-traffic coordination is planned to manage airspace closure risk [1], which carries both safety and mission implications. The use of mature, well-characterized platforms and instruments with substantial flight heritage — all have flown previously in their proposed configurations [1] — reduces the likelihood of novel safety issues arising from unproven technology.

The proposal does not contain a dedicated safety section or a formal hazard analysis in its scientific narrative. However, this is consistent with the separation between a scientific proposal and the administrative safety documentation that would be developed during the project’s confirmation and readiness reviews — milestones explicitly included in the implementation timeline [1]. The quarterly risk reviews [1] provide a mechanism for ongoing safety monitoring throughout the field campaigns. Given the established nature of all platforms and instruments, and the reliance on institutional safety frameworks (NASA, UNOLS) with demonstrated track records, the absence of a standalone safety analysis in the scientific narrative does not indicate a material safety deficiency.

## Management

**The management structure is well-conceived for a multi-platform field campaign of this complexity, with clearly defined leadership roles, a credible staffing plan, and a realistic timeline that includes prudent contingency; the principal limitation is the lean size of the dedicated team relative to the breadth of the data to be analyzed, which the proposal partially mitigates through institutional depth and staggered postdoctoral appointments.**

The management architecture divides responsibility into three coherent domains under an experienced PI and a dedicated Science Investigation Manager. The PI (J. Thomas Farrar, WHOI) provides overall scientific leadership; Ernesto Rodriguez (JPL) leads aircraft measurements; Andrey Shcherbina (UW/APL) leads in situ observations; and Eric D’Asaro (UW/APL) leads science analysis and modeling, with Paul Matthias serving as Science Investigation Manager and NASA Ames ESPO providing field-deployment support [1]. This structure aligns authority with the natural divisions of the work — airborne remote sensing, oceanographic field operations, and synthesis — and places individuals with deep, directly relevant expertise at each node. The institutional depth supporting this team is substantial: WHOI, JPL, Scripps Institution of Oceanography, and the University of Washington Applied Physics Laboratory together represent one of the most capable collections of oceanographic and remote-sensing expertise available for a campaign of this type.

The staffing plan proposes six postdoctoral researchers on two-year appointments, with staggered start dates aligned to distinct analysis tasks: early modeling and campaign optimization, then air-sea interaction, Wave Glider and wave data analysis, and integrated frontal structure [1]. This is a lean team for a five-year investigation that will generate data from three aircraft, a research vessel, four Wave Gliders, five Seagliders, Lagrangian floats, and approximately 100 surface drifters across three field deployments. The institutional laboratories — JPL’s science data systems for PRISM and DopplerScatt processing, the established workflows at UW/APL for float and glider data, and SIO’s MASS data pipeline — provide the production infrastructure that the lean postdoctoral team complements rather than replaces. Two science workshops per year during the main analysis phase [1] provide a coordination mechanism for the geographically distributed team.

The timeline is well-structured and realistic. The five-year program allocates approximately nine months for pre-pilot preparation and modeling, a two-week pilot in April 2020 with a September backup, a full year for pilot data processing and algorithm refinement, two 25-day intensive operations periods in April and September 2021 with backup dates in the following seasons, and two full years for scientific analysis and publication [1]. The one-year gap between pilot and first main campaign is explicitly justified for calibration, algorithm improvement, and correction of interoperability problems [1]. This sequencing demonstrates an understanding that complex multi-platform campaigns require iterative refinement, not a single uninterrupted deployment sequence. A six-month overall shift preserves the seasonal comparison [1].

The data management plan specifies deliverables for each instrument group, with Level-2 data available to the team and public through the designated NASA archive (PO.DAAC) within approximately six months of each campaign, and later reprocessing scheduled through 2023 [1]. This distributed responsibility model is appropriate for a campaign with diverse instrument types, though it places a coordination burden on the Science Investigation Manager to ensure cross-compatibility of formats, metadata, and quality flags. The PI and Science Investigation Manager oversee delivery, and each group is responsible for its products and for making them available to other investigators [1].

The proposal does not contain a detailed budget or itemized personnel breakdown in the supplied materials; the management judgement at this level does not require that information. The scientific narrative provides sufficient evidence — defined roles, credible staffing, institutional support, a phased timeline with contingency, and a data delivery schedule — to assess whether the management arrangements support delivery of the stated aims. They do: the structure is appropriate to the campaign’s complexity, the leadership has directly relevant expertise, the timeline accommodates the sequential dependencies between pilot and main campaigns, and the data management plan ensures that the benchmark dataset will be accessible to the broader community. The lean postdoctoral team represents a limitation on the depth and breadth of analysis that can be completed within the funded period, but the staggered appointments and the institutional processing infrastructure provide a realistic basis for delivering the core scientific products.

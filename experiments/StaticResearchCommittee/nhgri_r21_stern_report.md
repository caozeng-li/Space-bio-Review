# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project investigates who was recommended for eugenic sterilization in nine California state institutions between 1921 and 1952, how the demographic and institutional patterns changed over time, and how patients and their families responded. It joins approximately 15,000 individual-level recommendation records with census-derived institutional and catchment populations, aims to produce the first population-based estimates of sterilization risk by ethnicity, gender, age and institution, and includes a qualitative analysis of consent practices, institutional language and patient resistance. The most valuable expected knowledge is a systematic, multi-institutional portrait of a program that accounted for roughly one-quarter of all documented eugenic sterilizations in the United States, together with empirically grounded connections to questions about reproductive control, institutional vulnerability and the ethical uses of genetic information.

The plan rests on an already-built REDCap database with 212 variables, completed IRB approvals and an informative pilot analysis at one institution that confirmed feasibility and yielded adjusted odds ratios consistent with the project's central hypotheses. Census microdata are available, the surname-based ethnic classification is explicit, and the ethical and security arrangements are thorough. Material limitations include imprecise person-time denominators from interpolated decennial census snapshots, unquantified misclassification associated with Spanish-surname identification in a California setting where Filipino-Latino surname overlap exists, missing nativity information on a substantial fraction of records, and no pre-specified sensitivity analyses for these measurement challenges. The qualitative component is well-scoped as an interpretive complement but lacks a stated analytic framework and a mechanism for formally integrating its findings with the quantitative results.

The combination of a well-preserved historical dataset, a supported analytic framework, and a societally significant question justifies the project despite unresolved constraints on measurement precision and qualitative integration. The proposed work would make a valuable descriptive and comparative contribution even if the rate estimates remain approximate and the qualitative interpretation stays close to the documents. With the data infrastructure already in place and ethical protections robust, the remaining limitations principally affect the sharpness of the quantitative inferences rather than the project's overall feasibility or importance. The current plan merits support.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**This project addresses a historically consequential and empirically under-documented question with a dataset of unusual completeness, promising descriptive, comparative, and interpretive knowledge that would advance scholarship at the intersection of historical demography, public health, and the ethical, legal, and social implications (ELSI) of genetics.** The scientific value rests on three distinct products.

First, the project will produce a systematic descriptive epidemiology covering approximately 15,000 sterilization recommendations from nine California institutions over three decades, drawing on an already-built database with 212 variables capturing demographics, diagnosis, institutional context, and recorded institutional judgments about behavior [1]. At the time of the proposal, no comparable individual-level resource existed for any US state at this scale, and the California program accounted for roughly one-quarter of all documented eugenic sterilizations nationally [1].

Second, the study will generate population-based rate estimates that link recommendation records to census-derived institutional and catchment populations, using Poisson regression to test associations with gender, age, ethnicity, and other characteristics [1]. The two-stage framework—examining disproportionate institutionalization separately from disproportionate selection for sterilization once institutionalized—is conceptually appropriate for understanding coercive institutional processes [1]. Prior quantitative work on eugenic sterilization, notably Price and Darity's 2010 county-level analysis of North Carolina, had demonstrated the feasibility of demographic analysis but relied on aggregated data without individual denominators. The California proposal's framework, which constructs institutional population denominators and compares rates within the institutionalized population, represents a conceptual advance.

Third, the qualitative analysis of recommendation forms, correspondence, and protest letters distinguishes institutional claims from the documented responses of patients and families [1]. This preservation of the distinction between recorded institutional judgments and an individual's own responses is essential for valid historical interpretation and for connecting demographic patterns to lived experience.

**The project's ELSI relevance gives it applied significance beyond historical description.** The central questions—who was targeted for reproductive control, how institutional routines and diagnostic categories were used to justify procedures, and how people resisted—engage directly with contemporary debates about disability, reproductive justice, and the ethical governance of genetic and reproductive technologies [1].

The pilot analysis at Pacific Colony already demonstrated that institutional diagnostic categories were applied unequally: among recommendation records, 56.9% carried the historical label "Moron" compared with approximately 35% in the broader institutional population, a pattern that invites systematic investigation across all nine institutions [1]. The pilot also showed large unadjusted differences in the recording of institutional allegations by Spanish-surname status, with 33.8% of Spanish-surnamed individuals flagged for "sexual delinquency" versus 22.0% of those without Spanish surnames, and 15.0% versus 7.5% flagged for petty crime [1]. These preliminary findings are the institutions' own classifications, not the researchers' judgments, and their systematic extension to a multi-institutional, multi-decade database would constitute a substantial addition to the historical record.

**The attainable knowledge is valuable even if the resulting quantitative estimates remain exploratory rather than confirmatory**, a characterization that follows from acknowledged measurement constraints in denominator construction and ethnic classification rather than from any deficiency in the question itself. The qualitative work is appropriately scoped as a complementary historical-interpretive component, and the explicit separation of the companion digital archive (Aim 4) from the core analytical plan protects the scientific deliverables from dependence on a parallel project [1].

The value is tempered by modest theoretical specificity: the proposal does not articulate sharply what new conceptual or sociological insights would follow from confirming the pilot patterns at scale, nor does it pre-specify the comparisons that would discriminate among competing historical interpretations. These limitations affect the sharpness of the contribution but do not negate it. A descriptive and comparative analysis of this scope, built on a dataset that is itself a significant scholarly achievement, would make a meaningful addition to knowledge.

## Innovation

**The proposal brings a novel combination of epidemiologic methods and systematic historical analysis to a dataset that, at the time, had not been subjected to population-based quantitative investigation.** Its originality lies in the conjunction of data, analytic framework, and disciplinary approach, rather than in any single methodological invention.

### Two-stage analytic framework

The most distinctive contribution is the conceptual framework that separates disproportionate institutionalization from disproportionate selection for sterilization once institutionalized. For California, the existing scholarship was predominantly qualitative and historical—Stern's own *Eugenic Nation* (2005), Lira and Stern's work on Mexican American resistance (2014), and institutional histories cited in the references—without formal epidemiological methods or population denominators [1]. The proposed project would have been the first to bring census microdata and regression-based rate estimation to any state's eugenic sterilization records at the individual level, constructing census-derived institutional population denominators and comparing sterilization recommendation rates within the institutionalized population, with the further step of examining institutionalization rates relative to catchment-area census aggregates [1].

### Scale and data structure

The scale and structure of the data amplify the originality. The approximately 15,000 recommendation records span nine institutions of differing function—psychiatric hospitals and homes for the feebleminded—across three decades, with 212 coded variables [1]. The sources are heterogeneous: diagnosis is available only for 1935–1952 records, census racial classifications differ across the 1920, 1930, and 1940 enumerations, and 1950 data are available only at the tract level [1]. The proposal's plan to construct comparable variables from these discrepant sources, using a consistent Spanish-surname identification procedure across both the recommendation and census datasets, represents a practical methodological advance over accepting the original institutional categories as given [1].

### Integration of quantitative and qualitative analysis

The integration of quantitative and qualitative analysis at this scale is uncommon in the historical demography of eugenics. The qualitative component goes beyond thematic illustration: it targets systematic coding of consent practices, refusal letters, and diagnostic terminology changes across the full corpus of accompanying correspondence and forms, preserving the distinction between institutional claims and patient responses [1]. The approximately 50 handwritten patient protest letters from Patton State Hospital represent a distinctive source for understanding resistance [1]. The proposal's approach to analyzing these documents alongside the demographic patterns—rather than treating narratives as mere illustration of statistical findings—is methodologically appropriate.

### Limitations in framing

Limitations in the framing of the contribution should be acknowledged. The proposal describes the work as exploratory and descriptive, which is suitable for the R21 mechanism, but does not articulate sharply what new theoretical or historical claims would follow from confirming the Pacific Colony pilot findings at scale. The pilot already demonstrated elevated adjusted odds of sterilization recommendation for Spanish-surnamed patients (OR 2.07), those under 18 (OR 3.21), and females (OR 1.27) [1]. Extending these analyses to eight additional institutions and additional census years would add precision, institutional variation, and temporal trends, yet the proposal does not specify hypotheses about why patterns might differ across institutions, nor identify the specific comparisons that would test competing historical interpretations. The qualitative analysis is described as thematic coding without a specified analytic framework. These limitations reduce the conceptual sharpness of the intended contribution but do not erase its originality. The knowledge to be gained—the first population-based, multi-institutional, quantitative-qualitative portrait of the demographic operation of any US state's eugenic sterilization program—would be a genuinely new addition to the literature.

## Rigor

**The research design can produce informative descriptive epidemiology and meaningful rate comparisons across institutions and demographic groups, but several measurement constraints mean the resulting quantitative estimates are better characterized as exploratory and hypothesis-generating than confirmatory.** The qualitative component is appropriately scoped as a historical-interpretive complement, though the absence of a specified analytic framework and a mechanism for integrating its findings with the quantitative results limits the overall inferential coherence.

### Central quantitative design and pilot evidence

The central quantitative design links approximately 15,000 sterilization recommendation records with census-derived institutional population denominators and uses Poisson regression to estimate incidence rate ratios for demographic characteristics including Spanish surname, gender, age, and nativity [1]. This architecture is conceptually appropriate for the available data structure. The Pacific Colony pilot demonstrates feasibility: comparing 1,003 recommendation records from 1935–1944 with 1,068 institution residents from the 1940 census, the team produced both unadjusted and adjusted estimates, reporting an adjusted odds ratio of 2.07 (95% CI 1.65–2.60) for Spanish surname and 3.21 (2.67–3.86) for age under 18 [1]. The shift from the pilot's logistic regression to the full proposal's Poisson regression across institutions and time is a sensible methodological progression.

### Denominator precision

Three measurement constraints warrant attention. First, the person-time denominators rely on decennial census snapshots (1920, 1930, 1940) with intercensal years linearly interpolated and post-1940 counts extended forward [2]. This approach assumes smooth population changes that may not reflect admission surges, releases, or deaths, introducing unquantified measurement error. Neither the proposal nor the published work from the funded project pre-specifies a sensitivity analysis for this interpolation, which could affect rate estimates in institutions with fluctuating populations.

### Spanish-surname identification

Second, Spanish-surname identification using Census Bureau lists is a defensible proxy when self-reported ethnicity is unavailable, but published validation studies in the same 1920–1940 California census data document material limitations. Surname-based classification of Filipino individuals achieves specificity exceeding 0.99 but sensitivity as low as 0.10 in 1920 [3]. A subsequent methods paper from the team explicitly notes that Filipino surnames overlap with Spanish surnames due to shared colonial history, and that the Spanish surname list excludes Filipino names to reduce false positives—which in turn misses Latinos whose surnames were also recorded among Filipinos [3]. General validation literature further documents that surname-based identification is less sensitive for women, due to name changes at marriage, and for more acculturated individuals [3]. The proposal acknowledges that historical classifications differ across sources [1] and plans to construct comparable variables, but does not present a plan to bound the effects of surname misclassification on rate ratio estimates through sensitivity analysis.

### Missing data

Third, missing data create a material gap. Published work from the funded project reports that 4,282 of 17,813 records (24%) were missing nativity information, requiring separate sensitivity analyses with lower and upper bound estimates [2]. The proposal itself does not pre-specify how missing data will be handled for the planned analyses. Given that missingness may be non-random—foreign-born patients may have been less likely to have birthplace recorded—this omission affects the interpretability of nativity-based comparisons. The data availability table further documents that diagnosis is absent from 1921–1934 recommendation records and from all census sources, so population-based rate comparisons will be adjusted for a limited set of covariates (age, gender, nativity, surname) that are consistently available across sources [1].

### Qualitative component

The qualitative component analyzes recommendation forms, interdepartmental correspondence, patient communications, and protest letters, with plans to manually code themes from consent and refusal documents and trace diagnostic terminology changes over time [1]. The approximately 50 handwritten patient protest letters from Patton are a distinctive source, and the proposal appropriately distinguishes institutional claims from patient responses [1].

The qualitative work is described as thematic coding without a specified analytic framework—grounded theory, content analysis, or narrative analysis are not invoked—and without a stated plan for formally integrating qualitative findings with the quantitative results. For a historical-interpretive project of this kind, this is a material limitation rather than a disqualifying one; the qualitative analysis will still yield valuable evidence about consent practices and resistance. The design would be strengthened by specifying how the two analytic streams—the demographic associations and the close reading of documents—will inform each other to produce synthetic conclusions.

## Feasibility

**The project is substantially de-risked by completed preliminary work, and its technical demands are within the capability of the assembled team.** The principal uncertainty is whether investigator effort and project funding are proportionate to the 24-month scope, because specific resource documentation is absent from the proposal; the uncertainty affects the precision of the feasibility judgement rather than indicating that the work cannot be carried out.

### Completed infrastructure and pilot evidence

The most decisive factor supporting feasibility is the prior construction of the core data infrastructure. The team has already located, digitised, and de‑identified approximately 15,000 historical recommendation records, obtained approvals from the California Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects and the University of Michigan biomedical IRB, and built a HIPAA‑compliant REDCap database containing 212 coded variables [1]. Data entry for all records is scheduled for completion before the main analytical phase begins [1].

A pilot analysis at Pacific Colony has already linked recommendation records with 1940 census institutional counts, produced unadjusted and adjusted comparisons, and reported stable estimates consistent with the project's central hypotheses—an adjusted odds ratio of 2.07 (95% CI 1.65–2.60) for Spanish surname and 3.21 (2.67–3.86) for age under 18 [1]. These results confirm that the record‑to‑census linkage is technically achievable and that the analytical tools can be applied to the available data structure.

### Data access and methods

Access to the necessary external data is confirmed through the University of Michigan's Institute for Social Research, which provides individual‑level census microdata for 1920, 1930, and 1940, supplemented by tract‑level 1950 reports and institutional annual or biennial reports [1]. The Spanish‑surname identification method draws on established Census Bureau lists with manual review for misspellings [1]; this approach is explicit, replicable, and appropriate for the historical sources. The data availability table documents which variables can be constructed from each source, and the investigators explicitly plan to construct comparable rather than assume interchangeable categories [1]. No additional data collection or primary fieldwork is required—the project is purely analytical, working with existing digitised records and publicly available census data.

### Team, schedule, and resource documentation

The personnel arrangement pairs Stern's historical expertise with Kardia and Harlow's epidemiologic and quantitative expertise [1], and the institutional environment supplies the secure‑computing infrastructure already in use. The 24‑month schedule is appropriately structured with overlapping phases: Year 1 covers census companion dataset creation, descriptive statistics, initial hypothesis testing, and qualitative coding; Year 2 expands hypothesis testing, examines institutional variation, develops the consent and refusal analysis, and prepares publications [1]. Quantitative and qualitative work proceed in parallel so that emerging findings can guide contextual investigation [1]. The two‑year timeline is ambitious given the scope—nine institutions, multiple census years, qualitative coding of correspondence—but the data are already digitised and structured, and none of the analytical methods requires novel development.

What limits the feasibility assessment is the absence of an itemised budget, specific FTE allocations for the three investigators and any supporting staff, and contingency plans for delays in census data linkage, missing‑data handling, or qualitative coding. The proposal mentions a project manager in the resource‑sharing plan [1] but does not specify that person's role during the analytical phase. These omissions prevent a definitive judgement about whether resources match the planned activities, but they do not indicate that the activities cannot be performed. The R21 mechanism is designed for exploratory work by established investigators in well‑resourced institutions, and the tasks described—analysis of an existing database, census data linkage, and thematic coding of documents—are not resource‑intensive. The work is of a kind that the named team can plausibly complete within the proposed interval.

## Ethics and safety

**The ethical and data-security arrangements are thorough, appropriately tailored to the sensitivity of the historical records, and proportionate to a retrospective study without direct participant contact.** The research raises no material concerns about physical safety, environmental harm, or direct risk to living subjects; the principal ethical obligations concern the protection of identifiable information about vulnerable historical populations and the responsible handling of records that document coercive medical procedures. The proposal meets these obligations with explicit institutional approvals, layered access controls, and a considered data-sharing plan.

### Regulatory approvals and data protection

The study is retrospective, working exclusively with historical documents created between 1921 and 1952, without any contact with the people represented or their descendants. The files include records of adults and children—recorded ages approximately 7–58—and of people with intellectual or psychiatric diagnoses, making the population a sensitive one even in a historical context [1]. The project has obtained approval from both the California Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects and the University of Michigan biomedical IRB [1]; these dual approvals, spanning the jurisdiction where the records originated and the institution conducting the analysis, provide proportionate independent oversight.

The data-protection architecture operates on multiple layers. Team members must be included on the IRB protocol and complete research-responsibility, confidentiality, and REDCap training before gaining access to any data [1]. Digitized source materials are accessed through a password-protected server; the analytical dataset is extracted into HIPAA-compliant REDCap instruments that are themselves access-controlled. Crucially, any identifiers used during data entry are excluded from exported data and reports, so that the working analytical file contains no direct identifiers. Source copies of the digitized records are stored on a separate password-protected drive kept in a locked safe, and any physical transport of records is specified to use a secure carrier [1]. This segregated, access-controlled arrangement is well-suited to the sensitivity of the source files and represents good practice.

### Data sharing and dissemination

The resource-sharing plan provides for release of suitable data after acceptance of the principal findings, with identifiers removed and disclosure risk minimized [1]. Requests and distributions are to be coordinated and documented by the project manager, using institutional data-use agreements as appropriate. The plan explicitly conditions data sharing on the privacy restrictions governing the historical records, which is appropriate given that the source files contain identifiable information about individuals, including children, who were subjected to coercive state action.

The companion digital archive (Aim 4) is subject to the same IRB and confidentiality requirements, with the proposal stating that individual identification will not be disclosed through any research outputs [1]. Because the archive is a parallel project with separate humanities support [1], its ethical governance falls partly outside the scope of this specific plan, but the stated commitment to confidentiality and the IRB oversight provide reasonable assurance that the dissemination activities will be managed responsibly.

No material risks to the research team, to communities, or to the environment are identified in the proposal, and none emerge from the activities described. The absence of laboratory or field work, animal subjects, or direct human participation means the risk profile is confined to informational privacy. The arrangements described are proportionate and thorough.

## Management

**The project's management arrangements are well matched to its analytical character: responsibilities are distributed among investigators with complementary expertise, the schedule is appropriately phased, and the institutional infrastructure is already in place.** The principal limitation is the absence of documented personnel effort levels and a named operational coordinator, which restricts the confidence with which resource proportionality can be confirmed but does not indicate a likely failure of delivery.

### Investigator roles and track record

The division of responsibilities draws on a coherent combination of disciplinary strengths. Stern brings established historical expertise on California eugenics, having published the foundational monograph *Eugenic Nation* (2005) and co-authored the Lira and Stern (2014) study of Mexican American resistance that the pilot analysis extends [1]. Kardia and Harlow contribute epidemiologic and quantitative expertise grounded in the University of Michigan School of Public Health, and the proposal explicitly names them as collaborators with defined roles in the analytical work [1]. This three-investigator structure provides redundancy across the historical, quantitative, and population-health dimensions of the project, reducing the risk that any single analytical stream depends on a single person. The team has already demonstrated its capacity to work together on this dataset through the construction of the REDCap instrument, the IRB approvals, and the Pacific Colony pilot analysis [1].

### Schedule and task phasing

The 24-month schedule, with a preparatory data-completion phase preceding the analytical period, assigns overlapping rather than strictly sequential activities. Year 1 creates the census companion dataset, produces descriptive statistics, begins hypothesis testing, and initiates qualitative coding; Year 2 expands hypothesis testing, examines institutional variation, develops the consent and refusal analysis, and prepares publications [1]. Quantitative and qualitative work proceed in parallel so that emerging findings can inform contextual investigation [1]. The two-year timeline is ambitious for nine institutions, multiple census years, and systematic qualitative coding of correspondence, but the ambition is tempered by the fact that the data are already digitised and structured. None of the analytical methods requires novel software development or primary data collection; the tasks are document coding, census linkage, and regression analysis using existing tools in an established computing environment.

### Remaining uncertainties

Several management details remain unspecified. The proposal does not document specific FTE allocations for the three investigators or for any supporting research assistants, data-entry personnel, or the project manager mentioned in the resource-sharing plan [1]. An itemised budget is not included, nor are contingency plans for delays in census data linkage or for the handling of missing data during analysis. For an R21 proposal—a mechanism designed for exploratory work by established investigators—this level of administrative detail is not unusual, and the track record of the team and the institutional resources of the University of Michigan School of Public Health provide reasonable assurance that the core activities can be staffed. The tasks described are not resource-intensive, and the parallel separation of the companion digital archive under separate humanities support [1] protects the core research deliverables from dependence on an external project whose own management is outside this plan's scope.

The coordinating structure—Stern as PI with Kardia and Harlow as named collaborators, supported by the existing data-entry and secure-computing environment—is sufficient for the work proposed. The absence of a named day-to-day project manager is a gap, given the scale of the qualitative coding and the data-sharing coordination responsibilities described, but it does not appear to create a critical dependency that would prevent the aims from being achieved.

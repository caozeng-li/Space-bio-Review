# Project review

## Overall assessment

This four-year mixed-methods project investigates the ethical and regulatory challenges that arise when biorepositories organize into networks, consortia, or federated arrangements. Rather than studying individual biobanks in isolation, it takes the network as its primary object of analysis, examining how organizational configurations shape consent, donor control, investigator access, privacy, return of results, and governance. The work combines a systematic inventory of U.S. networked biorepositories, semi-structured interviews with up to 100 stakeholders, longitudinal participant observation of governance in five contrasting networks, and normative analysis refined through stakeholder focus groups and an expert symposium. The most valuable expected knowledge is a systematic characterization of how network governance actually operates—what challenges stakeholders encounter, how they deliberate about them, and why different organizations adopt different solutions—together with domain-specific, generalizable recommendations grounded in both empirical evidence and ethical reasoning.

The proposal is strongly supported by the investigators' prior national survey of 456 U.S. biobanks and in-depth case studies of biobank management, which identified networks as a neglected but critical direction for research. The existing ELSI literature at the time was dominated by conceptual policy arguments and document-based policy comparisons; no prior study had conducted a multi-method, multi-site empirical investigation of networked biorepository governance. The five proposed observational cases—eMERGE, Kentucky Cancer Registry, NBSTRN, Sage Bionetworks–Synapse, and Mayo Clinic Biobank—provide genuine variation in maturity and configuration, and network leaders have agreed to observation, a critical precondition.

Three principal limitations materially affect the judgement. First, the five preselected cases, while well chosen, may cluster around networks with which the team has prior relationships, and the observation intensity of two in-person visits per year may miss governance processes that unfold between visits. Second, the project excludes direct donor perspectives by design, meaning recommendations about donor interests will rest on stakeholder perceptions rather than direct evidence. Third, the normative integration method is under-specified: the proposal does not articulate its theoretical position on how empirical findings relate to normative conclusions, though the team's composition provides reasonable assurance this can be developed during the project. The absence of a detailed budget in the supplied materials limits the evidence for assessing resource adequacy, though the management plan is otherwise clear and well-structured.

The proposal's strengths decisively outweigh its limitations. The research question is timely and important, the empirical contribution is original, and the design—triangulating retrospective characterization, prospective observation, and integrated normative analysis—is well-suited to the stated aims. The limitations are mostly matters of degree and scope: the preselected cases may not exhaust the range of network governance practices, the observation intensity is constrained by practical logistics, and the normative integration method deserves fuller specification. None of these threaten the central contribution, and the proposal's built-in flexibility—allowing case substitution, using focus groups to test recommendations, and incorporating an expert symposium—provides reasonable mitigation pathways. The current plan is sufficient to produce a systematic, empirically grounded account of governance challenges in networked biorepositories and defensible, context-sensitive recommendations. **Support is warranted.**

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**The proposal addresses an important and understudied question, and the knowledge it can produce is both academically significant and practically useful.**

The study's central question—how the ethical and regulatory challenges familiar from single-site biobanking are transformed when biorepositories organize into networks—is timely and well-motivated. Precision medicine increasingly requires large, diverse cohorts accessible through networked arrangements rather than isolated collections. The proposal's background establishes that 16% of U.S. biobanks already describe themselves as part of a network, consortia, or federated arrangement, yet the existing ELSI literature had not systematically examined the governance implications of this organizational form [1] [2]. Prior work was dominated by conceptual policy arguments (Kaye's proposal for "e-governance" [3]), document-based comparisons of formal access policies (the BioSHaRE-EU analysis of five European biobanks [4]), and studies focused on individual repository governance. No study had conducted the multi-method, multi-site empirical investigation of networked biorepository governance that this proposal undertakes.

The expected knowledge is both descriptive and prescriptive. Descriptively, the project will deliver a systematic characterization of U.S. networked biorepository configurations; an empirically grounded account of which ethical and regulatory challenges network stakeholders actually encounter; how these challenges are identified, deliberated upon, and resolved (or not) in practice; and why different organizations adopt different solutions under varying conditions of maturity, configuration, and resources. Prescriptively, it will produce domain-specific, generalizable governance recommendations—covering consent, donor control, investigator access, privacy and data security, return of results, and governance and oversight [1]—that are grounded in both the empirical findings and normative reasoning, refined through stakeholder focus groups and an expert symposium [1].

These six domains are well justified by the background literature and cover the principal loci of ethical tension that network arrangements create. Local consent terms may conflict with network-wide access, donor stewardship obligations may differ across sites, and return of results may require coordination across multiple institutional actors with different relationships to the donor [1]. A client investigator requesting broad access may encounter a donor institution that sees itself as a steward with restrictive obligations, while a result found at a remote site may require cooperation among investigators, central data managers, local clinicians, and the original donors.

The practical contribution addresses a genuine need. Many institutions are now building or joining biorepository networks, and the regulatory environment is shifting with genomic data-sharing policies and anticipated Common Rule changes [1]. Practicable, empirically informed governance guidance for such networks does not exist. The project's outputs—including adaptable governance models or best practices, disseminated directly to networks and through partnerships with relevant organizations and agencies [1]—constitute a useful capability beyond scholarly publication.

Two limitations affect the scope of attainable knowledge. First, the study excludes direct donor perspectives by design, reserving them for subsequent work [1]. This is a defensible scoping decision for a project that studies organizational governance among professionals; community advisory members who represent donor priorities are included as a partial proxy [1]. The consequence is that recommendations about donor control and consent will rest on stakeholder perceptions of donor interests rather than direct evidence of donor preferences—a gap the proposal correctly identifies as requiring separate investigation. Second, generalizability depends on the range of network configurations actually captured. The five cases are all U.S.-based and have academic or public-health affiliations; commercial or international networks are not represented. The provision for case substitution based on Aim 1 findings [1] provides partial mitigation, though the constraints of a four-year project with embedded observation mean the typology will be illustrative rather than exhaustive. Neither limitation undermines the central contribution: a systematic, empirically grounded account of U.S. networked biorepository governance that advances substantially beyond the existing literature and generates recommendations with practical utility.

## Innovation

**The project makes a significant and multi-dimensional original contribution by shifting the unit of analysis from the individual biobank to the network as an organizational form, and by combining retrospective characterization with prospective, longitudinal observation of governance as it unfolds.**

The originality operates at several levels, each representing a genuine advance beyond the existing ELSI literature. Most fundamentally, the proposal redefines its object of study. The investigators' prior national survey of 456 U.S. biobanks [2] established that 16% of repositories already described themselves as part of a network, consortium, or federated arrangement—a category respondents interpreted broadly to encompass registries, cooperative groups, multi-site studies, and consortia [2]. That prior work, together with in-depth case studies of six biobanks that identified "neglected ethical issues in biobank management" [5], concluded that networks constituted a critical direction for future research [2]. The current proposal picks up precisely where that work concluded, making the network—rather than the individual repository—its primary analytical unit. This conceptual reframing matters because ethical challenges that are manageable within a single institution become structurally different when authority, data, and specimens are distributed across multiple sites with distinct governance traditions, regulatory environments, and community relationships.

The combination of methods is novel in this domain. The existing literature at the time was dominated by conceptual policy arguments and document-based policy comparisons. Kaye (2011) proposed digital "e-governance" systems to bridge nationally based regulatory frameworks [3]. The BioSHaRE-EU analysis compared formal access policies across five European biobanks and found differences in application processes, ethics committee requirements, and material transfer agreements, but this was a document-based comparison rather than an investigation of everyday governance [4]. The eMERGE community engagement report (Lemke et al., 2010) described engagement activities at six U.S. biobanks but did not systematically examine governance or ethical problem-solving [6]. The Meir et al. (2011) article on "the human face of biobank networks" offered practical observations from biobankers' perspectives but was not a systematic empirical study [7]. No prior study had combined a systematic landscape inventory, retrospective interviews, and—most distinctively—longitudinal participant observation of governance deliberations in real time.

The prospective observation component (Aim 2) is particularly innovative. Studying governance as it happens, rather than reconstructing it from post hoc accounts, allows the project to capture the deliberative processes through which ethical challenges are identified, framed, and resolved—or not resolved—by network stakeholders. The five provisional cases span mature study-based pooling (eMERGE), mature state-registry linkage (Kentucky Cancer Registry), new state-held biosamples with de-identified phenotype data (NBSTRN), new cloud-based data sharing (Sage Bionetworks–Synapse), and a developing system-wide repository (Mayo Clinic Biobank) [1]. This variation in maturity and configuration is deliberate: the project does not study only successful, mature organizations but also includes developing networks and, as the proposal notes, the intention to examine an abandoned networking effort [1]—a rare and instructive case type that reveals failure modes invisible in studies restricted to thriving organizations.

Finally, the project integrates empirical findings with normative analysis in a structured sequence—empirical challenges identified from coded data, candidate solutions evaluated against ethical and legal standards and practical trade-offs, recommendations pressure-tested through focus groups and an expert symposium [1]. While individual components of this approach appear in the empirical bioethics literature, their application to networked biorepository governance—a domain where empirical description and normative prescription have largely proceeded on separate tracks—represents a methodological contribution in its own right.

## Rigor

**The mixed-methods design is well-conceived for the intended inferences, though three aspects of the design constrain the strength of some conclusions without undermining the central contribution.**

### Design strengths and triangulation strategy

The proposal triangulates three distinct evidential sources: retrospective interviews with up to 100 stakeholders across diverse network roles, longitudinal participant observation of governance in five contrasting networks, and primary-document analysis of consent forms, data-use agreements, and IRB protocols [1]. This multi-level design directly addresses a known limitation of single-method biobanking ethics research: the gap between stated policies and lived practice. The coding of interviews, field notes, and primary documents within a single shared ATLAS.ti project using a common thematic framework [1] enables systematic comparison across data types.

Several design features meet accepted standards for rigorous qualitative and empirical bioethics research. The interview sampling strategy deliberately seeks less powerful or dissenting stakeholders through snowball sampling and public organizational information, rather than relying solely on leadership referrals [1]—a specific response to the well-documented risk of elite-filtered accounts in organizational research. The Framework Analysis plan includes independent dual coding of the first ten transcripts by coders at each lead site, with a ≥90% agreement threshold before distributing remaining interviews [1]; this is stringent by qualitative standards. The seven interview domains (consent, donor control, return of results, client access, privacy, oversight, governance) [1] are well-specified, and the 60-minute semi-structured format with nondirective follow-up is standard and appropriate [1].

The observation protocol reflects awareness of ethnographic standards. Field notes are recorded immediately after sessions. Biweekly team discussions explicitly examine how the researchers' presence may have influenced deliberation [1]—a reflexive practice that surfaces rather than suppresses observer effects. The five provisional cases vary usefully in maturity (mature, new, in development) and configuration (study-based pooling, state-registry linkage, cloud-based sharing, planned system-wide repository) [1]; Table 4 [1] specifies which observation settings are accessible in each network, acknowledging variation transparently.

### Limitations that constrain the strength of inferences

Three aspects of the design warrant attention, though none is fatal to the central contribution.

**Observation intensity.** Two in-person visits per year per case, supplemented by remote observation of conference calls and meetings [1], represents a substantial fieldwork commitment. However, governance deliberations—particularly those involving conflict or sensitive trade-offs—may occur in informal interactions between scheduled events or emerge and resolve within time windows shorter than the interval between visits. The biweekly reflexive review partially mitigates this by ensuring ongoing attention to what is being captured and what might be missed, but the reliance on remote observation and informant accounts for inter-visit periods means some governance dynamics will be reconstructed rather than directly observed.

**Case representativeness.** All five networks involve organizations represented on the advisory committee or with which the investigators have existing collaborations [1]. This guarantees access—often the hardest condition to secure in organizational ethnography—but networks willing to host embedded observers may differ systematically from those that refuse, and the observed governance practices may reflect relationships of trust that do not generalize to networks without such pre-existing ties. The proposal acknowledges this by stating that Aim 1 findings may justify replacing or adding cases [1]; however, the practical constraints of access negotiation and the four-year timeline, with observation beginning in Q3 [1], mean substantial case substitution after fieldwork begins is improbable.

**Normative integration specification.** The proposal describes a defensible sequence—empirical findings identify recurrent challenges and practical contexts; the normative core evaluates candidate solutions against compatibility with ethical/legal standards, costs and trade-offs, and cross-configuration usefulness; team deliberation integrates domain-specific reports [1]—but does not articulate its theoretical position on how empirical and normative claims are related, nor how the is–ought gap will be navigated. The Ives et al. (2018) consensus standards for empirical bioethics recommend that the method of integration be "explained and justified, including details of what is integrated with what, how and by whom" [8] and that "the theoretical position on integration" be "made clear and explicit" [8]. The proposal identifies the ethical domains it will address and plans to use empirical data to inform normative analysis, broadly consistent with Standards 4–6 of Ives et al. [8], but the absence of an explicit integration framework means a reviewer cannot assess from the proposal alone whether the normative work will meet the standards of transparency, consistency, and rigor that the field has identified as essential [8]. The team's composition—including Richard Sharp (normative analysis and biobank experience), Mark Rothstein (legal and privacy analysis), and Eric Juengst on the advisory committee (philosophical and research ethics) [1]—provides reasonable assurance that this can be developed during the project's early phases, when the normative core is intended to contribute "throughout instrument development and interpretation" [1]. This is a matter of specification rather than a design failure.

On balance, the design is proportionate to the knowledge sought. It will not produce population-level estimates of challenge frequency or causal evidence about which governance arrangements produce better outcomes—nor does it claim to. What it can produce—systematic characterization of network configurations, grounded accounts of how stakeholders identify and deliberate about ethical challenges, and context-sensitive recommendations tested against stakeholder and expert judgment—is valuable within the mixed-methods, empirical bioethics paradigm, and the triangulation across data sources provides multiple vantage points that reduce reliance on any single evidentiary base.

## Feasibility

**The technical pathway is well-supported by the team's prior work and secured access, though the absence of budget detail in the supplied materials limits full assessment of resource adequacy.**

### Demonstrated capabilities and secured preconditions

The multi-component design is ambitious in scope but grounded in demonstrated capabilities. The investigators' prior national survey of 456 U.S. biobanks, which achieved a 72% response rate using a similar multi-faceted search strategy [1] [2], provides direct evidence that the inventory component (Aim 1a) is feasible. The adapted Boolean searches of PubMed and NIH RePORTER, supplemented by keyword searches of Google, AAMC member institutions, and CTSA sites [1], are appropriate for capturing the inconsistent terminology used to describe networked arrangements. The inventory's deliberate inclusion of small and informal arrangements, with verification through key contacts to confirm network existence and identify appropriate interviewees, strengthens coverage and is well within the team's established methods [1].

The interview plan of up to 100 semi-structured interviews across years 1–3 is demanding but achievable for a multi-investigator R01 with two lead sites. External precedent confirms that qualitative interview studies with biobanking stakeholders are feasible, though typically at smaller scale: Moodley and Singh (2016) conducted 21 in-depth interviews with researchers across three South African provinces [9]; Wyld et al. (2014) interviewed 25 participants including 21 medical practitioners in the Australian hospital biobanking context [10]. The larger sample here is justified by the goal of capturing variation across diverse network configurations and stakeholder roles—scientific and administrative leaders, operations managers, informaticists, ethics advisers, IRB members, community advisers, and client researchers [1]. The multi-site team structure, with investigators at both lead institutions conducting interviews and a shared ATLAS.ti project for distributed coding [1], makes this workload manageable.

The longitudinal observation component is the most logistically demanding and also the most innovative element. The critical precondition—network leaders' agreement to observation of relevant activities—is explicitly stated [1]. Two in-person visits per site per year, supplemented by remote observation of conference calls, advisory boards, and governance meetings [1], represents a substantial but achievable fieldwork commitment across four years. The designated investigator–research coordinator pair for each case, with substitutes arranged when needed [1], provides continuity that reduces the risk of observational gaps. The biweekly reflexive review process [1] integrates ongoing fieldwork into the team's analytical workflow.

The translational sequence—normative analysis informed by empirical findings, up to ten focus groups testing domain-specific recommendations, and an expert symposium for final refinement [1]—is logically ordered within the later project quarters (Q9–Q16) [1]. Focus groups of this number, with purposive recruitment, pre-circulated draft reports, and attention to power differences [1], are standard in qualitative health research and feasible within the stated timeline.

### The budget gap

The principal limitation for feasibility assessment is the absence of a detailed budget or resource justification in the supplied proposal materials. The planned activities—100 interviews with professional transcription, two annual site visits to five geographically distributed networks, up to ten focus groups, and a national/international expert symposium—carry substantial costs for travel, transcription, focus group logistics, and symposium organization. Without budget documentation, it is not possible to confirm that financial resources match the stated scope. This is a gap in the review record rather than a demonstrated defect; the original R01 application may contain a budget section not captured in the parsed text. The management plan, with its clear division of labor and advisory committee that includes representatives from four of the five case networks [1], provides reasonable assurance that the technical pathway can be executed, conditional on adequate resourcing.

## Ethics and safety

**The protections are well-specified and proportionate for a study of adult professionals and stakeholders; residual risks are inherent to the observational design and are managed transparently.**

### Participant profile and risk landscape

The study does not involve specimen donors or clinically vulnerable populations. Participants are adult professionals and stakeholders—scientific and administrative leaders, operations managers, informaticists, ethics advisers, IRB members, community advisers, donor-study investigators, and client researchers [1]. This participant profile shapes the risk landscape: the primary potential harms are professional (repercussions for expressing dissenting views about one's own network's governance), reputational (indirect identification of individuals or networks from detailed organizational descriptions), and psychological (discomfort during interviews or focus groups about ethically sensitive topics). The proposal identifies these harms explicitly and addresses them through a coherent set of safeguards [1].

### Interview and focus-group protections

Interview and focus-group participants receive information about the study, opportunities to ask questions, and consent procedures. They may decline specific questions, stop recording, or withdraw from participation [1]. These are standard and appropriate provisions for qualitative research with professional stakeholders. The decision not to offer participant incentives [1] is reasonable: in studies of organizational governance, incentives can raise concerns about undue influence on professionals who already have institutionally mediated relationships with the researchers.

The focus group design attends to the specific risk that power differences among participants may suppress candid discussion or create fear of reprisals. The proposal states that groups will be composed with attention to these power differences so that participants can speak freely, and that arrangements will encourage free expression [1]. This is a recognized challenge in stakeholder research where participants may occupy different hierarchical positions within the same network or across networks with competitive relationships. While the proposal does not detail the specific compositional strategies it will employ—such as whether managers and junior staff will be assigned to separate groups—the explicit attention to the issue is appropriate.

### Observation-setting ethics

The observation setting presents the most complex ethical challenge. Unlike interviews, where participation is discrete and consent can be individually negotiated, participant observation occurs in changing groups—consortium meetings, advisory boards, conference calls—where attending members vary across sessions. Explicit individual consent for every transient participant may be impracticable. The proposal addresses this through transparency (informing meeting participants about the researchers' role), repeated reminders, and coordination with network leadership and the study IRB to establish appropriate authorization [1]. This approach is consistent with established practice in ethnographic observation of organizational governance and represents a pragmatic, proportionate response to a genuine logistical constraint.

### Confidentiality architecture

Confidentiality protections are well-specified across multiple data types. Transcripts and reports will use study identifiers or pseudonyms. Network names will generally be withheld in publications unless explicit permission is obtained [1]—an important protection given that detailed organizational descriptions, even without naming, may permit knowledgeable readers to identify specific networks. Access to recordings, transcripts, and linkage information is restricted to the research team. Physical records are locked and computer files password-protected. Original recordings will be destroyed once transcription and coding are complete, limiting the window during which re-identification from audio data is possible [1].

### Residual risks

Three residual risks warrant acknowledgment, though all are inherent to the research design and are managed proportionately. First, pseudonymization mitigates but cannot eliminate the risk of professional repercussions for stakeholders who express dissenting views; the pool of governance participants within specialized biorepository networks is small and potentially identifiable to informed readers, even with network names withheld. The provision that network names will be disclosed only with permission provides a check, but the tension between rich organizational description and confidentiality is structural. Second, observer effects on governance deliberations—the researchers' presence altering the phenomena under study—represent both an ethical and a methodological concern. The biweekly reflexive review process [1] is designed to surface and document these effects rather than deny them, which is methodologically sound but does not eliminate the underlying dynamic. Third, indirect network identification from detailed organizational descriptions, even under pseudonyms, is a risk the proposal appropriately acknowledges and proposes to manage through permission-seeking. These residual risks are proportionate to the knowledge to be gained, and the proposal's transparency about them—rather than their denial—is a strength of the ethics section.

## Management

**The leadership structure, division of responsibilities, team expertise, and advisory arrangements are clearly specified and proportionate to the project's demands; the schedule is logically sequenced and allows appropriate overlap across aims.**

### Leadership and team expertise

Brothers and Goldenberg share overall oversight with complementary, well-defined leads: Goldenberg directs the network inventory, stakeholder interviews, and focus groups; Brothers leads primary-document analysis, longitudinal observation, and policy development [1]. This division maps coherently onto the three aims—retrospective characterization under Goldenberg, prospective observation and translational work under Brothers—while ensuring that each principal investigator carries a substantial and distinct portfolio.

The co-investigator team covers the essential disciplinary range for an empirical bioethics study of this kind. Jean Cadigan contributes ethnographic methods and co-authored the prior national biobank survey [2] and in-depth case studies [5], directly relevant to both the inventory and observation components. Richard Sharp provides normative analysis and biobank experience, anchoring the translational aim. Susanne Rivera brings regulatory and IRB expertise, consistent with the prior CTSA IRB survey on which she is a co-author [11]. Mark Rothstein covers legal and privacy analysis, and Heather Harrell takes primary responsibility for document and field analysis [1]. The normative core—philosophical and legal/regulatory specialists—is integrated throughout instrument development and interpretation, not merely at the final recommendation stage [1].

The leadership responsibilities are specified at the component level, as shown in the proposal's organization (see Table 5, [1]):

### Advisory committee

The advisory committee is a material asset. It includes representatives from four of the five provisional case networks: Janet Olson (Mayo Clinic Biobank), Rex Chisholm (eMERGE), Michael Watson (NBSTRN), and Megan Dorr (Sage Bionetworks), together with Thomas Tucker (Kentucky Cancer Registry) [1]. This secures both practical access—the critical precondition for the observation component—and ongoing methodological guidance from networks that are also the objects of study. The additional ELSI expertise (Gail Henderson, Eric Juengst) and genomic research perspective (Jonathan Haines) [1] provide independent scholarly oversight beyond the case networks' operational interests.

### Schedule

The four-year schedule (Table 7, [1]) is coherent and realistic. Network search spans Q1–Q8, allowing time to identify and characterize networks before interviews conclude in Q10. Final case selection by Q1–Q2, before observation begins in Q3, is appropriately early. Observation runs Q3–Q14, overlapping substantially with transcription and qualitative analysis (Q7–Q12), which enables the iterative relationship between fieldwork and analysis that qualitative research requires. Key challenges are identified in Q7–Q10, preliminary recommendations prepared in Q9–Q12, and the translational sequence—focus groups (Q11–Q14), expert symposium (Q13–Q14), and final dissemination (Q13–Q16)—occupies the later phases without compressing the empirical work. The two-week team review cycle for observations [1] is built into the schedule, not treated as an afterthought.

### The budget gap

The principal limitation for management assessment is the absence of budget detail in the supplied materials. The planned activities—up to 100 interviews, twice-yearly site visits to five geographically distributed networks, up to ten focus groups, and an international symposium—carry substantial costs for travel, professional transcription, focus group logistics, and event organization. Without a resource justification, it is not possible to confirm that staffing and financial resources match the stated scope. This is a gap in the evidence available to the reviewer rather than a demonstrated defect; the original R01 application may contain a budget section not present in the parsed text. The management plan, with its clear allocation of responsibilities and the demonstrated prior productivity of this investigator group across the preparatory studies [1], provides reasonable confidence that the work can be executed if adequately resourced.

# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project investigates how inflammatory signals, particularly IL6, disrupt the epigenetic machinery that maintains regulatory T cell function in inflammatory bowel disease. The central hypothesis is that the histone methyltransferase EZH2 collaborates with FOXP3 to repress inflammatory gene networks in Tregs, and that IL6‑activated JAK2 signaling phosphorylates EZH2 to impair this repression. The work proceeds through three linked aims: mapping the FOXP3–EZH2 interacting domains and testing their requirement for target‑gene repression and Treg suppressive function; identifying IL6‑dependent phosphorylation events on EZH2, with a focus on the SET‑domain residue Y641, and connecting these modifications to changes in enzymatic activity and transcription; and testing whether Tregs engineered to resist this pathway—either through IL6‑receptor deletion or through EZH2 modifications that preserve activity—provide more effective treatment of established intestinal inflammation in mouse colitis models than wild‑type Tregs. The most valuable expected knowledge is a mechanistic account of how an inflammatory cytokine impairs Treg epigenetic maintenance through a specific chromatin‑modification pathway, with the potential to inform cell‑therapy strategies that retain suppressive function in an inflamed intestine without requiring systemic cytokine blockade.

The proposal draws on substantial preliminary evidence from the Faubion laboratory, most notably the conditional EZH2‑deficient mouse model, which demonstrates that loss of the EZH2 SET domain in Tregs produces severe spontaneous inflammation, impaired suppression, and failure to prevent transfer colitis. Proximity ligation data support FOXP3–EZH2 association in primary Tregs, and mass‑spectrometric evidence identifies Y641 as a candidate IL6‑dependent phosphorylation site. The experimental design for Aim 1 is methodically sound, and the use of an established‑colitis treatment model in Aim 3 addresses the clinically relevant scenario where inflammation is already active.

Several material limitations affect the judgement. The central mechanistic claim that JAK2 directly phosphorylates EZH2 at Y641 rests on very weak computational predictions; the published literature demonstrates that JAK2‑mediated Y641 phosphorylation promotes EZH2 degradation rather than catalytic impairment. The Y641F and Y641E constructs chosen as phosphorylation‑resistant and phosphomimetic controls carry well‑characterised intrinsic activity changes unrelated to phosphorylation, compromising their interpretive value. The treatment experiments in Aim 3 use four animals per group, which provides limited power to distinguish moderate but biologically meaningful differences among multiple Treg variants. The TALEN editing experiments in primary Tregs lack published efficiency data for this specific application and carry high technical risk.

The proposal's main strength is the importance of its scientific question and the strong preliminary genetic evidence that EZH2 is required for Treg homeostasis and colitis prevention. These elements support the overall direction and give confidence that useful information will emerge from Aim 1 and the receptor‑deficient Treg comparisons in Aim 3. Against this must be weighed the central mechanistic weakness in Aim 2: the hypothesis that JAK2 directly phosphorylates EZH2 at Y641 to impair catalytic activity is poorly supported by the computational predictions and is complicated by the published literature showing that this phosphorylation event promotes degradation rather than catalytic inhibition. The Y641F and Y641E controls carry intrinsic activity changes that confound the interpretation of the rescue experiments in Aim 3, and the small group sizes limit the ability to discriminate among the several Treg variants planned. While the project can contribute valuable knowledge about FOXP3–EZH2 interactions and the effect of IL6 signaling on EZH2 function, the specific pathway model that organises the three aims—IL6→JAK2→EZH2 Y641 phosphorylation→impaired suppression—is not adequately supported by the current design. The proposal is therefore a worthwhile direction with substantive problems that affect its central claims, rather than a fatally flawed or a fully convincing plan.

**Recommendation: Current proposal insufficient for support (revise).** The project identifies a worthwhile direction, but substantive weaknesses in the current plan affect its central claims. Refocusing Aim 2 as an unbiased identification of IL6‑dependent EZH2 modifications, with direct measurement of both catalytic activity and protein stability, would strengthen the mechanistic foundation. Replacing or supplementing the Y641F/Y641E constructs with phosphorylation‑site mutants that do not carry intrinsic gain‑of‑function activity, and increasing group sizes in the colitis treatment experiments or explicitly tempering the conclusions that can be drawn from small samples, would address the main design weaknesses. The domain‑mapping in Aim 1 and the receptor‑deficient Treg comparisons in Aim 3 can proceed largely as proposed and would yield useful knowledge regardless of the specific Y641 phosphorylation hypothesis.

**Overall conclusion: The current proposal does not yet justify support**

## Scientific value

**Good (B).** The question this project asks is well motivated and scientifically important: how does the inflammatory environment—specifically IL6—disrupt the epigenetic machinery that maintains Treg suppressive function, and can interrupting this pathway preserve Treg activity during active intestinal inflammation? This question arises directly from a known obstacle in IBD therapeutics. FOXP3‑positive cells are present within inflamed lesions, yet their regulatory activity appears compromised and some cells produce inflammatory cytokines [1]. Treg‑based therapies therefore face the fundamental problem that the cells they deploy may lose function in the very milieu they are meant to control. Understanding the molecular basis of this dysfunction, and whether it can be overcome by cell‑intrinsic interventions that spare IL6 responses in other tissues, has clear translational relevance [1].

The attainable knowledge spans several levels of mechanism. The project is expected to define the FOXP3–EZH2 interacting domains required for target‑gene repression, characterise how IL6‑dependent signalling modifies EZH2 enzymatic activity, and test whether Tregs engineered to resist this pathway provide more effective treatment of established colitis than wild‑type Tregs. The work connects protein‑association data, chromatin‑occupancy measurements, transcriptional readouts, and functional suppression assays to in vivo treatment models, aiming to produce an integrated account of how an inflammatory cytokine impairs Treg epigenetic maintenance through a specific chromatin‑modification pathway [1].

The preliminary genetic evidence from the conditional EZH2‑deficient mouse strengthens confidence that useful knowledge will emerge. Loss of the EZH2 SET domain under FOXP3 promoter control produces a severe inflammatory phenotype: approximately 80% of FOXP3ΔEZH2 animals died by 35 days in a dataset of 107 mice, and survivors at eight weeks were highly susceptible to a normally subclinical 1% DSS challenge assessed by blinded histology in 16 mice [1]. Mutant Tregs produced less IL10 and more IFNγ and IL17, and suppressed responder‑cell proliferation poorly [1]. RNA sequencing of CD4‑positive cells from Crohn’s disease resections identified 379 of 457 differentially expressed EZH2 target genes as upregulated, linking the chromatin target pathway to human disease [1]. Adoptive transfer of EZH2‑deficient Tregs failed to protect RAG‑null recipients [1]. These data establish that EZH2 is required for Treg homeostasis and colitis prevention, providing a strong foundation for the mechanistic dissection proposed.

The proposal’s contribution must be assessed against existing work showing that EZH2 is critical for Treg maintenance (DuPage et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2015) and that FOXP3‑bound chromatin recruits EZH2 for repression upon Treg activation (Arvey et al., 2014). The project extends this foundation by asking how inflammatory signalling disrupts the FOXP3–EZH2 axis, which is a distinct and clinically relevant question not answered by those studies.

The scientific value is tempered by two considerations. The recognition that Y641F—a gain‑of‑function mutation—improves Treg function in autoimmunity is already published (Peeters et al., 2024), so that component of Aim 3 is partly confirmatory. The colitis treatment experiments use four animals per group, which provides limited power to detect moderate but biologically meaningful differences among the several planned Treg variants. For these reasons the attainable knowledge is valuable but not definitive on the therapeutic question; the overall scientific value is rated good rather than excellent.

## Innovation

**Good (B).** The proposal makes a genuine and timely contribution by asking how an inflammatory signal—specifically IL6—disrupts the epigenetic machinery that maintains Treg suppressive function, and whether intervening at this node can preserve suppression during active intestinal inflammation. The central framing of a cytokine‑to‑chromatin pathway, linking extracellular IL6 through JAK2 to a post‑translational modification on EZH2 that impairs FOXP3‑dependent gene repression, adds mechanistic specificity beyond the established requirement for EZH2 in Treg maintenance [1]. This is a conceptually attractive integration of signalling and epigenetics that distinguishes the project from general Treg biology or descriptive catalogues of inflammatory effects.

The originality must however be calibrated against several prior findings. The requirement for EZH2 in Treg homeostasis and suppression was established independently by DuPage et al. (2015, Immunity) and Yang et al. (2015, Scientific Reports). The cooperation between FOXP3 and EZH2 in gene repression, including FOXP3‑dependent EZH2 recruitment at activation‑responsive genes, was demonstrated by Arvey et al. (2014, Nature Immunology). The proposal’s Aim 1—mapping FOXP3–EZH2 interacting domains and testing their contribution to repression and suppression—extends this foundation but is largely confirmatory in character. The domain‑mapping experiments would resolve whether the association is direct or mediated through EED or SUZ12 [1], which is a legitimate open question, but the knowledge gained is incremental relative to existing co‑immunoprecipitation and chromatin data.

Aim 2 contains the proposal’s strongest claim to novelty: that IL6‑activated JAK2 phosphorylates EZH2 at Y641 in the SET domain, thereby reducing histone methyltransferase activity and weakening FOXP3–EZH2‑dependent repression. This hypothesis is specific, testable, and connects an established cytokine pathway to a particular chromatin‑modification event. The proposal’s preliminary evidence—IL6‑dependent reduction in EZH2‑associated H3K27 methyltransferase activity that is blocked by a JAK2 inhibitor, and mass‑spectrometric identification of Y641 phosphorylation after IL6 treatment—provides a plausible entry point [1].

However, the computational support for Y641 as a JAK2 substrate is very weak: the NetPhos2.0 score is 0.014 and the GPS3.0 score is 1, compared with scores of 0.992 and 8–10 for predicted MAPK sites at S610 and S639 [1]. This does not disprove the hypothesis, but it means the specific JAK2‑to‑Y641 claim is a high‑risk bet rather than a well‑supported prediction. The published literature adds a further complication: JAK2‑mediated phosphorylation of EZH2 at Y641 (Y646 in human) is documented, but its reported functional consequence is β‑TrCP‑mediated EZH2 degradation rather than catalytic impairment [2]. The proposal would thus contribute a genuinely novel finding if it demonstrates that phosphorylation at this site alters catalytic activity in Tregs, but the existing evidence base favours a different mechanism and the experimental controls selected complicate the interpretation.

Aim 3 applies Y641F—a phosphorylation‑resistant substitution—and Y641E—a phosphomimetic—to test whether maintaining EZH2 activity improves Treg treatment of established colitis, compared with IL6Rα‑deficient Tregs [1]. The demonstration that Y641F enhances Treg function has, however, already been published: Peeters et al. (2024, Cell Reports) from the DuPage laboratory showed that EZH2 Y641F expression in Tregs increases Foxp3 stability, promotes effector differentiation, and accelerates remission from experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis. The proposal applies this finding to a different disease context (colitis rather than EAE) and adds a comparison with Y641E and receptor‑deficient Tregs, which provides an incremental extension. The therapeutic framing—preserving Treg‑intrinsic suppression while sparing systemic IL6 signalling [1]—is conceptually attractive but is a rationale rather than a novel discovery.

Taken together, the proposal’s innovation lies principally in its mechanistic hypothesis connecting IL6 signalling to a specific EZH2 phosphorylation event, and in the integrated experimental pipeline that tests this connection from domain mapping through to an established‑colitis treatment model. The work adds to a mature foundation of published findings on EZH2 in Tregs, and elements of the downstream functional testing are partially preceded by recent work from another laboratory. The contribution is genuine and well‑motivated, but the innovative step is incremental rather than transformative, warranting a rating of good rather than excellent.

## Rigor

**Moderate (C).** The experimental design is capable of delivering useful knowledge from Aim 1 and from selected comparisons within Aim 3, but the central mechanistic hypothesis that organises the three aims—that IL6‑activated JAK2 directly phosphorylates EZH2 at Y641, thereby reducing methyltransferase activity and weakening Treg suppression—rests on evidence whose strength, direction, and expected functional consequence are inconsistent with the published literature, and the controls chosen to test it carry intrinsic activity changes that confound interpretation. This propagates into the treatment‑model rescue experiments in Aim 3. The judgement reflects a design that can answer some of the questions it poses but whose central inferential chain is materially weakened at its most specific step.

### Aim 1: methodically sound

The strongest elements lie in Aim 1. Co‑immunoprecipitation of FLAG‑EZH2 and HIS‑FOXP3 deletion constructs, supplemented by proximity‑ligation confirmation in a cellular context, is an appropriate strategy for mapping interacting regions; the laboratory reports established expression and immunoprecipitation methods, and the domain‑mapping work is supported by published FOXP3–EZH2 association data from activated Tregs [1]. Connecting the interaction data to target‑gene repression through RORA and IRF4 promoter–luciferase reporters, chromatin immunoprecipitation for H3K27me3 occupancy, and reconstitution of conditional EZH2‑deficient Tregs with wild‑type or interaction‑defective constructs provides a logical link from protein association to chromatin state to transcription [1]. The inclusion of alternative pathways—EED or SUZ12 depletion if the interaction proves indirect, and examination of EZH2‑associated long non‑coding RNAs if FOXP3 is not required for EZH2 targeting—provides reasonable contingency [1]. The in vitro suppression assay with titrated Treg‑to‑responder ratios and the in vivo transfer‑colitis prevention model with tamoxifen‑inducible deletion are well‑established approaches in the laboratory’s prior work [1]. These experiments can answer whether specific FOXP3–EZH2 interacting domains are required for target‑gene repression and Treg function.

### Aim 2: central mechanistic claim weakly supported

Aim 2 is where the design encounters its principal difficulties. The hypothesis that IL6‑activated JAK2 directly phosphorylates EZH2 at Y641 to impair methyltransferase activity is the specific mechanistic claim that distinguishes this project from more general investigations of IL6 effects on Tregs. Four lines of evidence weaken the support for this claim.

**Computational predictions.** The computational predictions for Y641 as a phosphorylation site are extremely low: the NetPhos2.0 score is 0.014 (essentially at background) and the GPS3.0 score for JAK2 is 1, compared with scores of 0.992 and 8–10 for MAPK sites at S610 and S639 [1]. These predictions do not rule out Y641 phosphorylation, but they give little positive support for JAK2 as the direct kinase.

**Published functional consequence.** A published review of EZH2 post‑translational modifications (Li et al., 2020) documents JAK2‑mediated phosphorylation of EZH2 at Y641 in mouse (Y646 in human) but reports that the functional consequence is β‑TrCP‑mediated EZH2 degradation, not catalytic impairment [2]. The proposal’s own preliminary data show that IL6 reduces EZH2‑associated H3K27 methyltransferase activity and that a JAK2 inhibitor blocks this effect [1], but these measurements do not distinguish between a catalytic defect and reduced EZH2 protein levels. The experimental design does not incorporate systematic measurements of EZH2 protein stability alongside the planned H3K27 methyltransferase assays, which would be needed to distinguish between these alternatives.

**Problematic Y641F control.** The Y641F substitution chosen as a phosphorylation‑resistant control is a well‑characterised gain‑of‑function mutation recurrent in follicular lymphoma and diffuse large B‑cell lymphoma that increases H3K27me3 levels and is found in approximately 25% of follicular lymphoma cases [3] [4] [5] [6]. It is not simply a phospho‑null substitution. If Y641F Tregs behave differently from wild‑type Tregs in suppression assays or colitis treatment, the difference cannot be attributed specifically to the absence of phosphorylation, because Y641F has intrinsic gain‑of‑function effects on methyltransferase activity that operate independently of any phosphorylation event. The proposal acknowledges that “Y641F has been associated with lymphoproliferative conditions” and commits to investigating long‑term function and toxicity [1], but this does not address the interpretive confound.

**Crude Y641E phosphomimetic.** The Y641E phosphomimetic control is a relatively crude mimic of phosphotyrosine; a preliminary observation that Y641E has lower activity than wild‑type EZH2 in an EZH2‑deficient cell system [1] does not establish that this reduction recapitulates the effect of IL6‑induced phosphorylation in Tregs. The proposal also cites Kim et al. (2013) as evidence that “Phosphorylation of EZH2 activates STAT3 signaling” [1], but that study concerns Akt‑mediated S21 phosphorylation in glioblastoma stem‑like cells, and its relevance to the proposed IL6‑JAK2‑Y641 pathway in Tregs is not established [7].

### Aim 3: strengths in the treatment model, weakened by propagated uncertainty

Aim 3 includes an important strength: the use of an established‑colitis treatment model in which Tregs are transferred four weeks after effector cells, rather than only a prevention model in which Tregs are co‑transferred with effectors. This addresses the clinically relevant scenario where inflammation is already active and where Tregs are known to be less effective [1]. The inclusion of IL6Rα‑mutant effector cells to distinguish Treg‑intrinsic defects from effector‑cell resistance to suppression is a thoughtful control [1]. The receptor‑deficient Treg comparisons with wild‑type Tregs can be interpreted cleanly regardless of the specific Y641 phosphorylation mechanism.

The EZH2‑modified Treg rescue experiments in Aim 3, however, depend on the interpretation established in Aim 2. Comparing FOXP3ΔEZH2 Tregs reconstituted with wild‑type EZH2, Y641E, or Y641F at titrated doses of 100,000–300,000 cells in the established‑colitis setting [1] will not cleanly test whether Y641 phosphorylation impairs Treg function, because both control constructs have intrinsic activity differences unrelated to phosphorylation. The power calculation—four RAG‑null recipients per treatment group, repeated three times, providing 90% power for a difference of 1.25 standard deviations under one‑way ANOVA [1]—describes the design’s ability to detect a large effect across groups. With four animals per group in a single replicate, pairwise comparisons among the several Treg variants planned have very limited sensitivity; moderate differences in disease activity or histology scores that are biologically meaningful may not be detected.

The chain of inference across the three aims is logically structured, but the central mechanistic claim is not adequately supported by the proposed measurements. The design can answer whether IL6 signalling affects EZH2 activity and whether receptor‑deficient Tregs improve colitis treatment; these outcomes would be valuable. It cannot, with the current controls, determine whether the specific pathway IL6→JAK2→EZH2 Y641 phosphorylation→impaired suppression is correct. This warrants a moderate rigour rating.

## Feasibility

**Good (B).** The methods and resources required for Aims 1 and 2 are well established in the Faubion laboratory, and the conditional mouse models, biochemical assays, and institutional infrastructure provide a credible foundation for delivering the core mechanistic work. The principal feasibility concerns are the small group sizes in the colitis treatment experiments in Aim 3, the high technical risk of TALEN editing in primary Tregs, and the sequential dependency of the three aims within a five‑year schedule.

### Established methods and resources

The core experimental methods—co‑immunoprecipitation, proximity ligation, luciferase reporters, chromatin immunoprecipitation, mass spectrometry, inhibitor pharmacology, and adenoviral transduction—are techniques that the laboratory has used in its published work on KLF‑dependent FOXP3 regulation and in the preliminary data presented in the proposal [1]. The conditional EZH2‑deficient mouse model is already characterised, with survival data from 107 mice and functional data from in vitro suppression assays and transfer‑colitis experiments [1].

The reported technical efficiencies—approximately 20% nucleofection in primary Tregs and greater than 75% adenoviral transduction [1]—are realistic. The nucleofection figure is consistent with published experience: a recent optimised protocol confirms that nucleofection compromises Treg viability and that specialised culture conditions (high IL‑2, HEPES buffer, avoidance of centrifugation) are required for adequate expansion [8]. The 20% efficiency is low, and for experiments requiring reconstitution of EZH2‑deficient Tregs with multiple mutant constructs, the starting cell number needed to yield sufficient transduced cells after selection will be substantial, particularly given that Tregs represent approximately 5–10% of CD4‑positive cells [8]. This is manageable for the biochemical experiments in Aims 1 and 2 but becomes a practical constraint for the in vivo treatment experiments, where large numbers of purified, reconstituted Tregs must be generated.

### Small group sizes in the colitis treatment model

The colitis treatment experiments in Aim 3 present the most significant feasibility concern. The design uses four RAG‑null recipients per treatment group, repeated three times [1]. With four animals per group in a single replicate, between‑group comparisons rest on very small samples. The stated power rationale—90% power for a difference of 1.25 standard deviations under one‑way ANOVA—describes the ability to detect a large effect across multiple groups, but does not address the pairwise comparisons among the several Treg variants planned in Aim 3 (wild‑type, IL6Rα‑deficient, and FOXP3ΔEZH2 Tregs reconstituted with wild‑type EZH2, Y641E, or Y641F, across titrated doses) [1]. Small but biologically meaningful differences in disease activity or histology scores may not be detectable, and pooling across three replicates (effective n = 12 per group) improves power but introduces batch effects that the analysis must address. A more explicit treatment of how pairwise comparisons will be interpreted, and whether the design can distinguish a partial improvement from no improvement, would strengthen confidence.

### TALEN editing in primary Tregs

The TALEN editing of IL6Rα in primary mouse Tregs carries high technical risk. The proposal relies on a collaboration with the institutional genetics core, but no efficiency data are provided for this specific application [1]. The published literature contains minimal direct precedent for TALEN‑mediated knockout in primary mouse Tregs; most TALEN work in primary haematopoietic cells has focused on human T cells. The proposal mitigates this dependence by testing the receptor‑deficient genetic model in parallel, so the TALEN experiments are supplementary rather than essential to the central comparison [1].

### Interdependent aims and timeline

The five‑year schedule allocates roughly years 1–2 to Aim 1, years 2–3 to Aim 2, and years 3–5 to Aim 3 [1]. The three aims are strongly interdependent: Aim 1 must identify the FOXP3–EZH2 interacting domains before Aim 2 can interpret which phosphorylation events matter for complex assembly, and Aim 2 must establish which EZH2 modifications alter activity before Aim 3 can select the correct mutants for in vivo testing. The alternative pathways described—EED/SUZ12 depletion if the interaction is indirect, examination of long noncoding RNAs if FOXP3 is not required for targeting [1]—provide reasonable contingency, but the proposal does not describe explicit go/no‑go milestones that would trigger these alternatives on a defined timeline. Delays in early aims would cascade through the entire project with limited buffer.

The institutional resource base—flow cytometry, cell sorting, confocal imaging, sequencing cores, proteomics, mouse genetics, and biochemical instrumentation—is appropriate for the proposed work [1]. The PI's expertise in intestinal immunology and Treg biology, combined with the preliminary data on conditional EZH2 deficiency, supports the feasibility of Aims 1 and 2. The concerns identified above do not make the project unachievable, but they reduce confidence that all three aims can be completed to the depth proposed within the stated five‑year period. The feasibility is therefore rated good rather than excellent.

## Ethics and safety

**Good (B).** The animal welfare framework is appropriate for murine colitis models and meets the standards expected for this type of research, but the transfer of T cells carrying a known oncogenic mutation into lymphopenic recipients requires a more explicit safety‑monitoring plan than the generic humane endpoints described.

### Animal welfare provisions

The proposal specifies that all animal work is subject to Mayo Clinic IACUC review within an accredited centralized facility with veterinary oversight [1]. Study animals will be weighed and examined daily, and predefined endpoints—loss of more than 20% of starting body weight or a moribund appearance—will trigger euthanasia. Analgesic support after adoptive transfer and approved euthanasia procedures are described. These provisions are appropriate for the colitis models employed (DSS exposure, T cell transfer into RAG‑null recipients, and tamoxifen‑inducible deletion protocols) and align with standard practice for murine IBD research.

The human tissue component is limited to previously collected CD4‑positive cell expression data from Crohn’s disease resections and healthy ileal biopsies, used as preliminary scientific context and not as part of prospective recruitment [1]. No new human subjects research is proposed, so the ethical considerations concentrate on the animal experiments.

### Oncogenic risk of Y641F‑modified Tregs in lymphopenic recipients

The principal safety concern involves the planned use of Y641F‑modified Tregs in adoptive transfer experiments. As discussed under Rigor, Y641F is a well‑characterised gain‑of‑function mutation in the EZH2 SET domain that is recurrent in follicular lymphoma (approximately 25% of cases) and diffuse large B‑cell lymphoma, where it drives aberrant H3K27me3 profiles and alters transcriptional responses [3] [4] [5]. The proposal intends to introduce this mutation into Tregs via adenoviral reconstitution of FOXP3ΔEZH2 T cells and transfer them into lymphopenic RAG‑null recipients for colitis treatment experiments at doses of 100,000–300,000 cells [1]. RAG‑null mice lack adaptive immunity, creating an environment in which transferred cells can expand with minimal immunological restraint.

The proposal acknowledges that “Y641F has been associated with lymphoproliferative conditions in other settings” and states an intention to “investigate long‑term function and toxicity,” mentioning rescue experiments in mice lacking functional Tregs as a possible approach [1]. However, the details of safety monitoring specific to the oncogenic risk are not specified. The standard humane endpoints—20% body‑weight loss and moribund appearance—are designed to detect severe colitis or graft‑versus‑host‑like pathology but may not be configured to detect subclinical lymphoproliferation or clonal expansion of the transferred cells before they cause overt illness. The planned observation period, specific criteria for detecting lymphoproliferative changes (such as splenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, or abnormal circulating cell populations), and the threshold for terminating experiments on safety grounds are not described.

This risk must be placed in perspective. The colitis treatment experiments are relatively short‑term (recipients evaluated at approximately six to ten weeks), which limits the time available for clonal evolution and malignant transformation. The Y641F mutation alone is not sufficient for lymphomagenesis; human lymphomas with this mutation carry additional genetic alterations, and the cell number transferred is modest. The IACUC framework provides institutional oversight that can address emerging concerns during the study. Nonetheless, the hazard is not hypothetical—cells carrying an oncogenic driver mutation are being deliberately introduced into an immunocompromised host—and the monitoring plan as described does not provide proportionate safeguards. Explicitly adding endpoints for lymphoproliferation (such as scheduled palpation for lymphadenopathy, terminal assessment of spleen weight, and histological examination of lymphoid organs if any animal shows unexpected illness) would bring the safety arrangements in line with the level of risk. This is a manageable deficiency rather than a fundamental ethical obstacle, but it prevents a higher rating.

## Management

**Good (B).** The PI has established expertise in the intestinal immunology, Treg biology, and chromatin‑modification techniques that the project requires, as demonstrated by the published work on KLF‑dependent FOXP3 regulation and the substantial preliminary data on conditional EZH2 deficiency [1]. The five‑year structure sequences domain mapping, mechanistic biochemistry, and in vivo treatment experiments in a logical order, and the alternative pathways described—EED or SUZ12 depletion if the FOXP3–EZH2 interaction proves indirect, examination of long noncoding RNAs if FOXP3 is not required for EZH2 targeting [1]—provide reasonable scientific contingency. Institutional cores for flow cytometry, cell sorting, confocal imaging, sequencing, proteomics, and mouse genetics are available and appropriate for the work [1].

Two features of the management plan warrant attention. First, the proposal does not specify explicit timeline‑based milestones or go/no‑go criteria that would trigger the alternative pathways on a defined schedule. The five‑year timeline allocates roughly years 1–2 to Aim 1, years 2–3 to Aim 2, and years 3–5 to Aim 3 [1]. The three aims are strongly interdependent: Aim 1 must identify the interacting domains before Aim 2 can properly interpret which phosphorylation events affect complex assembly, and Aim 2 must establish the functional consequences of EZH2 modifications before Aim 3 can select the correct mutant constructs for in vivo testing. Delays in early aims would cascade through the project with limited buffer, and without explicit milestones the decision to switch to an alternative pathway relies on ad hoc judgement. The estimated mouse usage of 2,139 animals over five years, including approximately 150 wild‑type, 100 IL6R‑mutant, and 100 EZH2‑deficient donor animals per year [1], represents a substantial breeding and husbandry operation whose coordination with the biochemical and treatment experiments requires careful scheduling that is not detailed.

Second, the TALEN editing of IL6Rα in primary mouse Tregs in Aim 3 depends on a collaboration with the institutional genetics core [1]. No letter of support, preliminary data, or efficiency estimate for this specific application in primary mouse Tregs is provided. The published literature contains minimal direct precedent for TALEN‑mediated knockout in this cell type, and the absence of documentation leaves the readiness of this collaboration uncertain. The dependence is mitigated because the receptor‑deficient genetic model is tested in parallel, making the TALEN experiments supplementary rather than essential to the central comparison [1]. Nonetheless, the management risk is that effort and time are invested in an approach whose feasibility for this cell type has not been demonstrated.

These gaps do not indicate incapacity. The PI's track record, the institutional resource base, and the logical sequencing of aims form a credible management framework. The absence of explicit decision milestones and the undocumented TALEN collaboration are remediable weaknesses that prevent a higher confidence judgement, but the plan as described is sufficient to support delivery of Aims 1 and 2 and the receptor‑deficient Treg comparisons within Aim 3.

# Project review

## Overall assessment

The PRECISE project aims to test whether a telephone patient navigation program, targeted using a prediction model, improves colonoscopy completion among safety-net patients who have a positive fecal immunochemical test (FIT) and are at elevated risk of non-adherence. Conducted at Sea Mar Community Health Centers in Washington State, the study first externally validates an existing adherence prediction model and then randomizes patients with moderate or low predicted adherence to centralized telephone navigation or usual care. The most valuable expected knowledge is evidence on the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of prediction-informed navigation, as well as practical insights into embedding such a program within routine clinic workflows in a large federally qualified health center.

The proposal is supported by the investigative team's deep experience with pragmatic CRC screening research and an established partnership with a high-need safety-net health system. The patient-randomized design, transparent power calculation for the primary outcome of one-year colonoscopy completion, and plan for blinded outcome ascertainment provide a methodologically sound framework. The principal limitation is that the prediction model's discrimination is modest (c-statistic approximately 0.65), which reduces the precision of risk stratification and limits statistical power for detecting effect modification. The interaction and moderator analyses are therefore underpowered for all but very large effects, constraining the study's ability to conclusively identify which patients benefit most. The implementation plan depends on sustained clinic leadership support, navigator continuity, and timely documentation from external GI facilities, though these are manageable risks given the team's relationship with the partner organization.

The PRECISE study makes a valuable contribution by testing a practical method for directing scarce navigation resources to safety-net patients at highest risk of non-adherence. The design is well powered for the primary effectiveness question and the implementation plan is credible. The decisive trade-off is whether the prediction model's modest discrimination—an acknowledged limitation—prevents the study from meeting its stated targeting goal. This limitation constrains what can be learned about differential treatment effects but does not undermine the trial's capacity to deliver valid evidence on the average effect of navigation in the lower-adherence population. On balance, the integrated design, methodologically sound primary analysis, and feasible execution warrant support despite inherent limitations in available predictive information.

**Rating:** B — **Confidence:** B

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

The PRECISE study addresses a highly consequential practical question whose answer would directly inform the organization of colorectal cancer screening follow-up in safety-net settings. The core scientific value lies in determining whether a prediction model can usefully direct scarce patient navigation resources to those most likely to forgo post-FIT colonoscopy, and whether such navigation—delivered by trained clinic staff embedded in routine workflows rather than by a parallel research service—improves completion rates in that targeted population.

**The problem is well-documented and concentrated in the study population.** Prior work in the team's own STOP CRC program found only 54% colonoscopy completion within one year after a positive FIT, despite 87% of patients being referred [1]. This finding is consistent with rates of 43–52% reported by others in community health centers [2] [3]. The consequences of delayed or missed follow-up are established: Meester et al. (2016) demonstrated that increasing time to colonoscopy after positive FIT is associated with elevated colorectal cancer incidence and mortality [1]. The question of whether and how to target navigation therefore carries direct clinical and public health significance, not merely methodological interest.

**The integrated design is the principal source of scientific value.** Rather than testing navigation alone or validating a prediction model alone, PRECISE combines three components that mutually reinforce each other: external validation of an adherence prediction model, a pragmatic patient-randomized trial of centralized telephone navigation, and formal testing of whether predicted adherence modifies the treatment effect [1]. The enrichment design—restricting randomization to patients with moderate or low predicted adherence while observing the highest-adherence quintile separately [1]—is well-motivated by the theoretical framework of Hayward et al. (2006), who demonstrated that multivariable risk-stratified analysis can substantially improve power to detect treatment-effect heterogeneity compared with conventional one-at-a-time subgroup analysis [4].

Prior navigation trials tested average effects without risk stratification. Green et al. (2014) found a non-significant 10.1-percentage-point difference (91.0% vs 80.8%, P=0.10) in a system where usual care already included an EHR registry and physician reminders, with the authors noting that high baseline follow-up and small sample size limited power [5]. The NH CRCSP program achieved 96% completion but delivered navigation alongside dedicated colonoscopy funding rather than embedding it in existing clinical workflows [6]. PRECISE's design, by contrast, directly addresses resource targeting—a question that neither prior study was designed to answer.

**The attainable knowledge is substantial but realistically bounded.** The study can credibly answer whether centralized telephone navigation improves colonoscopy completion in the enriched lower-adherence population, provide externally validated evidence on cost-effectiveness from the health-plan perspective across adherence strata, and generate qualitative insights into barriers and experiences from both patient and provider perspectives [1]. The economic evaluation adds practical value by estimating incremental cost-effectiveness per additional colonoscopy, adenoma detected, and cancer detected, using costs relevant to routine implementation rather than research conditions [1].

The acknowledged limitation is that the prediction model's modest discrimination—a bootstrap-corrected c-statistic of 0.65 in the development sample [1]—constrains the precision of risk stratification and reduces statistical power for detecting treatment-effect modification. This means the study can detect only large interaction effects, and more moderate but still policy-relevant differences in navigation benefit across strata would go undetected [1]. The practical consequence is that the study will provide more definitive evidence about the average effect of navigation in lower-adherence patients than about precisely how to target it. This limitation is inherent in the available predictive information and is candidly acknowledged [1]; it does not negate the scientific value of the evidence the study can generate.

The qualitative component further enriches the knowledge contribution: approximately 60 patient interviews purposively sampled across probability groups and completion status, and 25–30 staff interviews, will illuminate mechanisms, barriers, and unintended consequences that quantitative outcomes alone cannot explain [1]. This complements the quantitative moderator analysis by providing interpretive depth even where statistical power for interaction tests is limited.

In sum, the study can be expected to yield externally validated evidence on navigation effectiveness in a high-need FQHC population; cost-effectiveness estimates stratified by predicted adherence; implementation knowledge about embedding prediction-based decision support and clinic-staff navigation into routine workflows; and qualitative understanding of barriers and experiences across language groups. This is a meaningful, practical contribution to organizing follow-up after stool-based screening in safety-net settings.

**Rating:** B — **Confidence:** B

## Innovation

The proposal advances beyond prior work in three specific and well-motivated ways, each representing a genuine step rather than a routine application. The integration of a prediction model with a randomized trial that tests effect modification in a safety-net setting is novel in the colonoscopy follow-up literature, and the use of clinic-embedded staff rather than research-funded navigators represents a meaningful innovation in the delivery model.

**Shift from screening uptake to post-FIT follow-up.** Most patient navigation programs in colorectal cancer screening have been designed to increase participation in initial screening, whether by mailed FIT outreach or by scheduled colonoscopy. PRECISE instead addresses the recognized bottleneck after a positive stool test, where the mortality benefit of screening is lost if patients do not proceed to diagnostic colonoscopy. The STOP CRC data showing only 54% one-year completion despite 87% referral [1], together with the documented barriers spanning bowel preparation, transport, cost, fear, and difficulty contacting patients [1], establish that this is a distinct problem from initial screening uptake and therefore warrants its own study rather than extrapolation from screening-focused trials.

**Risk-stratified trial design with formal effect-modification testing.** This is the proposal's most distinctive innovation. Prior navigation trials examined average treatment effects without classifying patients by their predicted probability of completing follow-up on their own. Green et al. (2014) randomized 147 patients to nurse navigation versus usual care and found a non-significant difference (91.0% vs. 80.8%, P=0.10), with the authors concluding that high baseline follow-up in a system with an EHR registry and physician reminders limited both the opportunity for incremental benefit and the statistical power to detect it [5]. The SCORE trial's centralized navigation produced a large difference (69.0% vs. 38.7% in the FIT-positive subgroup) but was not designed to examine whether the effect varied by patient characteristics [7].

PRECISE makes the stratification itself the object of study: the external validation of the prediction model determines which patients enter the randomized comparison, and the primary analytic plan includes formal tests of the interaction between predicted adherence stratum and navigation [1]. This design follows from Hayward et al.'s (2006) demonstration that risk-stratified analysis can substantially improve power to detect treatment-effect heterogeneity compared with conventional one-at-a-time subgroup analysis [4], though as discussed under Rigor, the model's discrimination constrains the precision of these tests. The originality lies not in the statistical technique alone but in its application to a concrete resource-allocation question in a pragmatic health-system trial.

**Clinic-staff delivery model testing sustainability.** The New Hampshire Colorectal Cancer Screening Program demonstrated high completion (96.2%) with navigation, but its navigation was embedded in a program that provided dedicated funding for colonoscopies [6]. Green et al. deployed research-registered nurses with additional paid time [5]. PRECISE proposes to train existing Sea Mar clinical staff—nurse practitioners or staff with similar roles—to deliver navigation integrated into EHR workflows and using the local clinical and community resources already available, rather than creating a parallel research infrastructure [1]. This directly tests a model that, if effective, could be sustained and scaled within FQHC operations without ongoing research funding.

The overall contribution is incremental rather than foundational—navigation and prediction models are established tools individually—but the combination to address resource targeting through a pragmatic trial is a well-conceived and original step.

**Rating:** B — **Confidence:** B

## Rigor

The PRECISE study employs a patient-randomized design whose core inferential structure is sound for the primary effectiveness question, but whose capacity to test the interaction that motivates stratification is materially constrained by the prediction model's modest discrimination.

**Randomization and comparator.** Patients in the moderate- and low-adherence strata are randomized individually to centralized telephone navigation or usual care within predicted-adherence level, age group, and sex, yielding 462 patients per arm across 28 clinics [1]. This individual-level randomization within clinics—coupled with a planned multilevel logistic regression using clinic as a random effect—is an appropriate analytic framework because navigation is delivered centrally rather than by clinic-specific personnel, limiting contamination through shared staff behavior [1]. The comparator, usual care comprising the existing notification, referral, and follow-up process, is appropriate for a pragmatic effectiveness trial. The statistician controls randomization while other staff are blinded to predicted probability [1]. Assignment to navigation is necessarily unblinded, but the primary outcome—chart-confirmed colonoscopy within one year—is assessed by a blinded auditor, reducing differential misclassification [1].

**Outcome ascertainment.** The two-step approach uses EHR codes to identify colonoscopy procedures, followed by chart audits for patients without coded completion, since prior work found codes retrieved only approximately 80% of reports [1]. The protocol further captures reasons for non-completion, missed appointments, preparation quality, and completeness [1]. This approach mitigates the under-ascertainment inherent in EHR-code-only methods and generates secondary outcomes that enrich interpretation.

**Primary analysis and power.** The power calculation is transparent and well-specified. Assuming 44% one-year completion under usual care in the enriched lower-adherence population, 924 randomized patients across 28 clinics (average cluster size 33), two-sided alpha 0.05, and ICC 0.03, the detectable improvement is 12.9 percentage points (to 56.9%, OR 1.68) at 80% power; without a design effect, 9.2 points (OR 1.45) [1]. The 44% assumption is plausible given STOP CRC's 54% completion in the full FIT-positive population [1], and the detectable differences are clinically meaningful. The SCORE trial found 69.0% vs. 38.7% in its FIT-positive subgroup [7], and Green et al. found 91.0% vs. 80.8% [5]; PRECISE's assumed baseline and detectable effect fall within a reasonable range for a pragmatic trial in a setting without a pre-existing registry and physician-reminder system. Power for the primary question is adequate.

**Prediction model and its consequences for interaction analyses.** Here lies the central limitation. The Cox model developed from STOP CRC has a bootstrap-corrected c-statistic of 0.65 [1]. External validation using a distinct historical Sea Mar cohort with planned recalibration if discrimination declines by 0.05 or more is methodologically appropriate [1], but even if the model validates unchanged, c=0.65 represents modest discrimination. When patients are classified into adherence strata, the highest quintile in the development sample achieved approximately 93% six-month colonoscopy probability while the lowest quintile reached approximately 25% [1]. This provides useful population-level separation, but substantial misclassification across adjacent quintiles is inevitable: some patients in the moderate-adherence group would complete colonoscopy without help, and some in the high-adherence surveillance group would fail to do so.

This classification error directly constrains the interaction analyses. The stated detectable interaction odds ratios at 80% power are 2.98 with the design effect and 2.17 without it [1]—representing very large effects. The study can detect only whether navigation roughly triples the odds of completion in one stratum while having negligible effect in another. More moderate but still policy-relevant gradients would go undetected. The proposal acknowledges limited precision for moderators [1] and appropriately plans to emphasize effect magnitude over significance testing for exploratory interactions [1]. This is candid but does not resolve the limitation: the design is primarily powered for the main effect, and the interaction tests that would directly inform resource targeting are substantially underpowered.

**Secondary analyses and qualitative work.** Shared-frailty Cox models for time-to-event outcomes, multilevel logistic models for binary process outcomes, and the qualitative component—approximately 60 patient interviews and 25–30 staff interviews purposively sampled across probability groups and completion status [1]—are appropriately specified. The qualitative work does not substitute for underpowered quantitative interaction tests but provides complementary understanding of barriers and experiences that the quantitative analyses alone cannot supply.

**Phase I–Phase II linkage.** The external validation uses a historical cohort distinct from the prospective trial cohort [1], and the timeline allows model validation (months 4–7) to be completed before randomization begins (months 12–14) [1]. This sequencing permits recalibration or revision of cut-points before enrollment, addressing a potential threat to the validity of the enrichment design.

In sum, the core design can credibly answer whether centralized telephone navigation improves colonoscopy completion in the enriched lower-adherence population. The rigor of the primary analysis, outcome ascertainment, and power calculation supports this inference. The interaction analyses are underpowered for all but very large effects, a limitation that is intrinsic to the prediction model's available discrimination and that constrains what the study can conclude about differential treatment benefit. The design's strength for the main effect does not fully compensate for this limitation in addressing the stated targeting goal, but it does not undermine the trial's capacity to deliver valid, practically useful evidence on navigation effectiveness.

**Rating:** B — **Confidence:** B

## Feasibility

The implementation pathway is well-supported by an established research partnership, team experience with pragmatic CRC screening trials in similar settings, and a recruitment plan grounded in the partner health system's own operational data. The proposal describes a feasible project whose principal risks—navigator staffing continuity, timely external documentation, and sustained clinic leadership—are acknowledged and manageable given the depth of the team's relationship with Sea Mar Community Health Centers.

**Demonstrated team capability and infrastructure.** Coronado directed the STOP CRC pilot, which built an EHR-embedded program using real-time data to identify eligible patients and track colorectal-related outcomes across 26 clinics, achieving 92.5% successful mailing of outreach kits and 53% completed motivational-interview calls with two telephone attempts [8]. More recently, the team's cluster-randomized trial in 28 rural Oregon clinics using mailed FIT outreach and patient navigation achieved 100% implementation for mailed outreach, though navigation reach was 57.9% [9], providing a realistic benchmark for engagement rates that should be monitored early in the intervention period. Sea Mar's integrated EHR and existing FIT outreach infrastructure [1] directly support the Phase I risk classification and the Phase II navigation workflow proposed here.

**Navigator staffing model.** The protocol specifies a six-call sequence delivered by nurse practitioners or staff with similar clinical roles, with two didactic training days, eight weeks of coaching, competency checklists, and ongoing fidelity monitoring through call recording [1]. The planning estimate of 126 minutes per navigated patient and approximately 970 hours for 462 patients over roughly 20 months of intervention delivery represents approximately 12 hours per week—well within the capacity of a dedicated or shared clinical staff member. The Green et al. (2014) trial provides a comparable precedent, using registered nurses already practicing within the health system with additional paid time for navigation [5]. Weekly-then-biweekly supervision meetings and quarterly specialist consultation provide reasonable quality assurance. The proposal does not, however, specify a backup navigator for leave, turnover, or workload surges when multiple patients require concurrent attention during the preparation-contact window; this is a manageable gap given the team's experience but warrants attention during implementation planning.

**Recruitment capacity.** Sea Mar's 28 participating non-specialty clinics serve approximately 29,281 screening-eligible patients, with planning figures of 1,186 annual positive FIT results, a 9% positivity rate, and approximately 949 patients falling in the moderate- or low-adherence strata before final eligibility exclusions [1]. The 18-month enrollment period provides a buffer if annual volumes fluctuate, and the five-year timeline allocates 7 months for model validation and materials adaptation before randomization begins [1]—sufficient to implement any recalibration that external validation may require.

**Critical dependencies and governance.** The proposal cites changing real-world clinic processes and dependence on supportive local leadership [1]. Sea Mar commits staff, training, tracking, and a clinical champion [1], and the quarterly advisory board including clinic representatives, gastroenterology providers, researchers, payers, and policymakers [1] provides a governance mechanism for addressing operational challenges. The team's long-standing partnership with Sea Mar, formalized through the medical director's co-investigator role, mitigates—though does not eliminate—the risk of leadership transitions or shifting organizational priorities. These are common risks in pragmatic health-system research and do not render the project infeasible given the documented institutional commitment.

**Rating:** B — **Confidence:** B

## Ethics and safety

The ethical framework is proportionate and well-specified for a pragmatic trial of an intervention that supports access to a clinically indicated procedure rather than introducing experimental treatment. Navigation helps patients overcome practical and informational barriers to completing a colonoscopy that has already been recommended following a positive FIT result; it does not alter the clinical indication, replace medical decision-making, or expose participants to procedures beyond standard care [1]. The risk profile is therefore consistent with usual care plus telephone support, and the safeguards described are appropriate for this level of risk.

**Regulatory oversight and data protection.** The proposal schedules IRB approval within the first three months [1], establishing the necessary institutional oversight before any research activity involving patient data or contact begins. The data management architecture uses study IDs and de-identified predictor data for risk classification, with secure quarterly EHR transfers and controlled access to clinical records [1]. This separation of research identifiers from clinical identifiers, combined with encrypted transfer protocols, provides a reasonable level of data protection for a study drawing on routine electronic health record information.

**Consent and fidelity monitoring.** Patients provide verbal consent specifically for the recording of navigation telephone calls, a focused consent for a discrete activity that carries negligible risk beyond privacy [1]. The recording itself supports fidelity monitoring—25% of calls during the first three months and during a new navigator's first three months, dropping to 5% thereafter [1]—and serves quality assurance rather than experimental purposes. The broader trial uses individual randomization with usual care as the comparator [1]. The offer of navigation to usual-care and surveillance patients who have not completed colonoscopy after the trial period is ethically appropriate, ensuring that control-group participants are not indefinitely denied a service that the study may demonstrate to be effective [1].

**Qualitative data handling.** Interviews, conducted in English or Spanish with approximately 60 patients purposively sampled across probability groups and completion status, and 25–30 staff interviews [1], generate potentially sensitive information about barriers, experiences, and program perceptions. The advisory board reviews de-identified interview findings [1], and the use of content analysis with consensus-based disagreement resolution in Atlas.ti provides a structured approach to interpretation that reduces the risk of selective reporting of participant views.

**Scope of practice and intervention safety.** No experimental drugs, devices, or procedures beyond standard care are involved. The intervention addresses well-documented barriers—bowel preparation, transport, time away from work, insurance, cost, fear, and scheduling difficulties [1]—that the team has characterized through prior qualitative work in the same population. The navigators are existing clinical staff (nurse practitioners or similar roles) [1] operating within their scope of practice and institutional credentials, rather than research staff performing clinical functions outside established governance.

**Equity considerations.** The enrichment design excludes the high-adherence quintile from the randomized comparison [1], which means approximately 20% of FIT-positive patients [1] will not be offered navigation during the trial period unless they subsequently fail to complete colonoscopy. This exclusion is justified by the study's purpose of testing whether scarce navigation resources can be targeted effectively—a question that cannot be answered without restricting navigation to those at elevated risk—and is mitigated by the offer of post-trial navigation to surveillance patients who have not completed the procedure. The study thus balances the knowledge gain from testing a resource-allocation strategy against the temporary restriction of a supportive service to a subgroup likely to complete follow-up without additional help. This balance is scientifically necessary and ethically defensible in a setting where navigation resources are genuinely limited.

**Rating:** A — **Confidence:** A

## Management

The management structure is well-configured for the proposed work, drawing on a multi-disciplinary leadership team with demonstrated CRC screening research experience, an established partnership with the host health system, and a five-year schedule that provides conservative time buffers for each phase.

**Leadership and expertise.** The leadership arrangement places Coronado, who directed the STOP CRC pragmatic trial and has led multiple federally funded studies of mailed FIT outreach and patient navigation in community health center settings, in the principal investigator role with overall responsibility for scientific direction and study execution [1] [8]. The co-investigator team includes Sea Mar's medical director Ricardo Jimenez, who provides the essential clinical-operational bridge between the research and the health system's service delivery, together with dedicated expertise in gastroenterology, biostatistics, health economics, qualitative methods, and health-information technology [1]. This composition covers the study's core methodological demands—prediction model validation, trial design and analysis, economic evaluation, and qualitative data collection and analysis—without evident gaps.

**Institutional commitment.** Sea Mar's commitment is documented through the provision of staff time for navigation delivery, training support, tracking infrastructure, and a designated clinical champion [1]. The formal embedding of the medical director as a co-investigator, rather than as an external collaborator or letter-of-support signatory, integrates the operational decision-making authority of the health system into the research leadership. This arrangement mitigates—though does not eliminate—the acknowledged risk that shifting organizational priorities could affect implementation [1].

**Timeline and sequencing.** The five-year schedule [1] is realistic and conservatively paced. Model validation and materials adaptation occupy months 4–9, providing a completed basis for the enrichment design before randomization begins at months 12–14—an appropriately sequenced dependency. The 20-month intervention delivery window (months 15–34) accommodates both the 18-month enrollment period and the one-year follow-up per patient. Qualitative data collection (months 34–40) is scheduled after the intervention period, allowing interviews to capture the full experience of navigation and follow-up. Overlapping chart abstraction (months 39–53) and analysis (months 47–60) provide 13 months of analytic work alongside 8 months of dissemination, adequate for a study of this scope.

**Operational coordination and quality assurance.** Navigators receive two didactic training days, eight weeks of coaching, and ongoing supervision through weekly meetings for the first three months transitioning to biweekly meetings thereafter, with quarterly specialist consultation [1]. Fidelity monitoring through call recording (25% of contacts initially, reducing to 5%) with feedback and booster training provides a structured quality assurance pathway [1]. The quarterly advisory board comprising clinic and patient representatives, gastroenterology providers, researchers, payers, and policymakers [1] furnishes multi-stakeholder governance and a forum for addressing implementation challenges.

**Remaining gaps.** The principal management gap is the absence of specified contingency authority or escalation pathways for the advisory board. While the board provides a venue for discussing operational problems, the proposal does not clarify whether it has decision-making authority, what happens if clinic leadership changes or the clinical champion departs, or how navigator leave or turnover would be managed given the single-navigator planning estimate. These are not unusual risks in pragmatic health-system research, and the depth of the PI's existing relationship with Sea Mar substantially mitigates them, but they represent points where the management plan relies on implicit coordination rather than explicit provision. The weekly supervision meetings and the medical director's co-investigator role provide practical channels for addressing such issues as they arise, even if not formalized as contingency plans.

**Rating:** B — **Confidence:** B

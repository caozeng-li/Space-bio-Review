# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project investigates how emerging commercial and clinical models of whole genome sequencing interact with the laws, regulatory practices, intellectual-property arrangements, and reimbursement systems that shape access to genetic testing. At the time of the proposal, WGS was moving rapidly from research into clinical care, yet policy frameworks had been built around single-gene or single-condition tests and did not accommodate the heterogeneous findings, multiple service providers, and complex business models that WGS entails. The planned work combines a systematic landscape analysis of the WGS industry, interviews with industry leaders, a modified Policy Delphi process involving approximately 40 stakeholders across five surveys and a two-day meeting, and coordinated legal analyses.

The most valuable expected knowledge is threefold: a detailed empirical description of the WGS industry, a stakeholder-validated and publicly available priority agenda for genomic policy, and reasoned alternative policy approaches with documented areas of agreement and disagreement for three high-stakes domains—analytical validity and testing quality, patents and licensing, and reimbursement and payment. The project is supported by a well-motivated research question, a multi-method design that is logically coherent and appropriate for the policy context, and a team with complementary and demonstrated expertise in landscape analysis, regulatory affairs, intellectual property, reimbursement, and qualitative stakeholder research. The choice of a Policy Delphi—designed to map disagreement and explore alternatives rather than to force consensus—is methodologically sound and well-suited to the diversity of stakeholder interests.

The primary limitations are measurement-related: the operational criteria for interview saturation are unspecified, the composite scoring of ordinal Likert items is not justified, and the plan lacks a formal strategy to manage and report differential attrition over the five survey rounds and 20-month engagement. The investigator-led selection of three policy domains to develop in detail, while transparently documented, means the most concrete knowledge products are contingent on that selection. These limitations constrain the strength of inference from individual components but do not undermine the project's capacity to deliver its core descriptive and priority-setting contributions.

On balance, the scientific value and innovation of the proposed work are high, and the research design is fit for the stated descriptive and priority-setting purposes. The identified limitations—such as the unacknowledged measurement assumptions in the Delphi scoring, the unspecified attrition management strategy, and the lack of quality-assurance specification for the parallel legal analyses—are genuine concerns, but they do not threaten the project's capacity to deliver its core outputs: a systematic industry landscape, a transparently derived set of policy priorities, and well-documented alternative approaches. The feasibility is strong, the safety arrangements are appropriate and proportionate, and the management plan is adequate. The limitations affect the strength of inference attainable from individual components rather than the overall value of the integrated findings, and the proposal's candour about the boundaries of consensus-seeking and the investigator-mediated selection of issues strengthens rather than weakens its credibility.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

The contribution of this project lies in its capacity to produce knowledge that is simultaneously descriptive, analytical, and practically usable at a moment when the clinical adoption of whole genome sequencing was outpacing the policy frameworks intended to govern it. The proposal identifies a well-defined and consequential gap: existing regulatory, intellectual-property, and reimbursement arrangements were built around single-gene or single-condition tests, yet WGS generates findings of heterogeneous validity, clinical significance, and utility, and the activities of sequencing, interpretation, and delivery may be performed by different organisations [1]. A policy architecture designed for individual tests does not neatly accommodate these arrangements, and the proposal correctly observes that understanding the emerging industry—what firms actually provide, the health claims they make, and their relationships with payers—is logically prior to identifying gaps or proposing policy changes [1].

Three kinds of knowledge are expected. First, a systematic landscape analysis of the WGS industry will identify organisations developing sequencing technologies, providing sequencing services, or interpreting sequence data, and will characterise their technologies, business models, patents, health claims, laboratory certification, and third-party coverage arrangements [1]. This fills a documented descriptive need: the scope of regulatory oversight depends on the activities and business models actually in play, and no comparably systematic description existed at the time.

Second, a modified Policy Delphi process involving approximately 40 stakeholders will generate a publicly available, ranked set of policy priorities across the full range of issues raised by clinical WGS integration, and will produce reasoned alternative policy approaches with documented levels of agreement and disagreement for three questions selected for importance and tractability [1]. The proposal is explicit that complete stakeholder consensus is not the intended endpoint—the purpose is to clarify options and document the reasons for agreement and disagreement [1]—which is intellectually honest and appropriate for a Policy Delphi.

Third, the dissemination plan—including Capitol Hill briefings in years two and three, meetings with federal officials and congressional staff, and multiple accessible publication formats—is designed to place these knowledge products directly in the hands of policymakers and the clinical and research communities [1].

The scientific basis for valuing these contributions rests on several supports. The landscape component is methodologically grounded in the GPPC's prior work systematically documenting companies and services in genetic testing [1], extended here to the fundamentally different context of clinical WGS. The interview component—semi-structured interviews with up to 20 industry leaders, continuing until thematic saturation [1]—adds qualitative depth about business models and pathways to clinical integration that a purely documentary landscape cannot capture.

The Delphi component is well-suited to the policy context: empirical evidence about the performance of specific regulatory approaches for WGS is limited, and the relevant actors hold different knowledge, responsibilities, and interests [1]. Anonymous rounds give participants opportunities to introduce unconventional options and to challenge arguments without an open meeting immediately favouring the most influential voices, while the final meeting provides room to explore the complexity that rankings alone cannot represent [1].

The proposal also acknowledges the boundaries of its knowledge contribution. Other consequential issues—including privacy, data ownership and storage, workforce needs, and equitable access—are recognised as important but fall outside the three domains selected for detailed policy development. The commitment to preserving these issues in the published priority list [1] ensures they remain visible rather than being treated as answered by this study. This candour about scope strengthens the credibility of the knowledge the project can actually deliver.

## Innovation

The project's originality lies in the deliberate conjunction of several elements that, individually present in prior work, had not been brought together to address clinical WGS policy as an integrated system. Three distinct contributions can be identified: a novel empirical object, a novel method application, and a novel organising structure for policy analysis.

**A novel empirical object: the clinical WGS industry.** The landscape analysis takes as its object the *clinical* WGS industry rather than the direct-to-consumer genetic testing market that had been the primary focus of prior descriptive work. The most directly relevant antecedent—Hogarth, Javitt and Melzer's 2008 review of the DTC genetic testing landscape—surveyed companies and regulatory issues but concentrated on tests marketed directly to consumers, and was a literature and documentary review without systematic company-level data collection or industry interviews. The GPPC's own prior work on oversight of genetic testing laboratories and on DTC business models provided a template for systematic landscape documentation [1], but the proposal extends this to a context where the actors, regulatory pathways, and business models differ fundamentally. In clinical WGS, the separation of sequencing, interpretation, and delivery among different organisations raises questions about which activities fall under which oversight regimes—a configuration not present in the DTC market, where a single company typically provides the test and interpretation directly to the consumer. The proposal's data extraction template, covering technologies, platform capabilities, services, customer groups, patents, health claims, CLIA certification, and third-party coverage [1], is more comprehensive than the variables tracked in earlier landscape work and is specifically designed to capture the multi-entity structure of the clinical WGS sector.

**A novel method application: Policy Delphi in genomic regulation.** The application of a structured Policy Delphi—as formulated by Turoff (1970)—to genomic testing regulation was, to the best of my assessment, without precedent at the time of the proposal. While Delphi methods had been used in healthcare quality indicator selection [2] and in health technology assessment more broadly, the Policy Delphi variant differs in its core purpose: it is designed to map disagreement and explore alternative courses of action rather than to force convergence toward a single consensus position. This is not a minor variation but a deliberate methodological choice justified by the heterogeneity of stakeholder interests in WGS policy—where industry, payers, clinicians, regulators, and patient groups hold fundamentally different responsibilities and incentives [1]. The proposal's design of anonymous rounds to allow introduction of unconventional options and challenge of dominant arguments, followed by a face-to-face meeting to explore complexity, represents a faithful and well-reasoned adaptation of Turoff's framework to a domain where the policy stakes are high and the empirical evidence base is thin.

**A novel organising structure: expertise-grounded policy domains.** The three-domain structure—analytical validity and testing quality, patents and licensing, and reimbursement and payment—organises the policy work around areas where the collaborating teams bring specific, non-redundant expertise [1]. Cook-Deegan and colleagues at Duke had examined DNA sequencing technologies, patents, and intellectual-property issues in multi-allele testing; Tunis and Deverka at CMTP had studied coverage and reimbursement for molecular diagnostics and pharmacogenomics; McGuire and the GPPC had investigated laboratory oversight and the regulation of genetic testing. Rather than attempting to cover all conceivable WGS policy issues—a scope that would risk superficiality—the proposal concentrates analytical depth in three domains where the investigators can bring substantive legal and policy knowledge to bear, while preserving visibility for other consequential issues (privacy, data ownership, workforce, equitable access) in the published priority list [1]. This approach does not claim to be comprehensive; it claims to be tractable and well-grounded, which is a more defensible contribution.

## Rigor

The multi-method architecture is logically coherent and fit for the project's descriptive and priority-setting purposes, but several measurement and design decisions constrain the strength of inference attainable from individual components. The overall sequence—documentary landscape and interviews feeding into a structured Policy Delphi with coordinated legal analyses, culminating in a stakeholder meeting and policy briefings—is well-suited to a domain where empirical evidence about regulatory performance is limited and the relevant actors hold heterogeneous knowledge and interests [1]. Each component serves a defined role, and the proposal is appropriately explicit that the intended output is not a consensus recommendation but a transparent mapping of options, agreement, and disagreement [1]. This candour strengthens rather than weakens the design, because it aligns the method's inferential ambition with its actual capacity.

**Landscape analysis: adequately specified.** The search strategy—covering Internet, PubMed, NEXIS, USPTO, and LexisNexis—provides reasonable coverage for identifying organisations developing technologies, providing sequencing services, or interpreting data [1]. The data extraction template, capturing technologies, platform capabilities, services, customer groups, patents, health claims, CLIA certification, and third-party coverage [1], is systematic and appropriate to the regulatory questions the project addresses. The initial list of 34 organisations [1] is plausible for a rapidly evolving sector. The proposal's commitment to monitoring new entrants and updating the landscape throughout the study [1] is a necessary adaptation to the dynamic industry context.

**Interviews: useful input but limited independent validation.** Up to 20 semi-structured telephone interviews with purposively selected senior executives, continuing until thematic saturation or the cap is reached [1], can yield useful qualitative insight into business models and clinical integration pathways. However, the proposal does not specify how saturation will be determined operationally—whether through a stopping rule (for example, three consecutive interviews yielding no new themes), independent coding during data collection, or post-hoc judgment. The single-analyst approach implied by the NVivo thematic analysis description [1] also limits reliability, as the proposal does not describe independent dual coding, inter-coder reliability assessment, or member checking. These are not fatal defects for an exploratory component feeding into a Delphi process, but they mean the interview findings should be understood as one input among several rather than as independently validated evidence about industry trajectories.

**Delphi design: well-conceived with four measurement concerns.** The Delphi is the analytical core of the project and is generally well-conceived, but four issues deserve attention.

First, the composite scoring for the prioritisation phase sums four five-point Likert items (importance, validity, technical feasibility, political feasibility) to produce Priority, Tractability, and Overall scores [1]. Summing ordinal items treats the response categories as interval-level data without explicit justification. While this practice is common in applied Delphi work, the proposal should acknowledge the measurement assumption and its potential effects on ranking stability; sensitivity analyses varying weighting schemes are not described.

Second, the selection of three policy questions for detailed development is delegated to the investigators in consultation with the advisory board, using the Delphi rankings as input rather than as a deterministic rule [1]. This is a defensible design choice given resource constraints, but the proposal would benefit from describing how disagreements between panel rankings and investigator judgment will be documented in the published outputs, so that the transition from stakeholder input to investigator selection remains transparent.

Third, the policy development phase (surveys 3–5) shifts from ranking to open-ended suggestion and iterative refinement of resolutions and arguments [1], but the proposal does not specify the rating scales for the new constructs (desirability and feasibility of resolutions, importance and validity of arguments) or how these differ from the constructs used in the prioritisation phase. This inconsistency limits the comparability of results across the two phases.

Fourth, the five-round Delphi with a two-day meeting over approximately 20 months [1] is more extensive than the two to three rounds typically recommended in the methodological literature. Boulkedid et al. note that "a large number of rounds may cause participant fatigue with steep dropout rates" [2]. The proposal does not specify a minimum response threshold per round or a plan for assessing and reporting differential attrition by stakeholder type—a gap that could affect the representativeness of later rounds, particularly the policy-alternative evaluations in surveys 4 and 5.

**Legal analyses: coordinated but quality assurance unspecified.** Three teams will examine the relevant laws, regulations, guidelines, and standards for the three selected questions using a common analytical framework [1], producing comparable white papers to inform the panel before survey 3. This is a sensible approach, but the proposal does not describe how differences in legal interpretation across the three teams will be identified and resolved, or how the white papers will be quality-assured before distribution to panel members. Given that these papers will frame the policy alternatives that panel members subsequently evaluate, any errors or biases in the legal analyses could cascade through the Delphi rounds that follow.

**Process evaluation: under-specified.** Anonymous real-time voting to display agreement with conclusions [1] and thematic analysis of recorded, de-identified transcripts [1] can provide useful qualitative evidence about the perceived utility of the Delphi approach. However, the proposal does not specify what would constitute evidence that the method was useful, nor does it describe a structured evaluation instrument—such as a post-process survey with standardised items about procedural fairness, representativeness, and influence on participants' views—that would allow the process evaluation to function as systematic evidence rather than impressionistic account.

**Inference strength in summary.** Taken together, these limitations mean the project's contribution should be understood as primarily descriptive and priority-setting rather than as producing validated policy recommendations or generalisable evidence about Delphi methods for genomic policy. The design is fit for delivering a systematic landscape description of the WGS industry, a transparently derived set of policy priorities, and well-documented alternative approaches to three selected questions—with the important caveat that the transition from stakeholder rankings to investigator-selected policy questions requires transparent documentation to maintain the credibility of the outputs. The limitations affect the strength of inference attainable from individual components rather than the coherence of the overall design, and the proposal's candour about the boundaries of consensus-seeking and the non-representative nature of the panel [1] is a methodological strength in a policy research context where overclaiming is a persistent risk.

## Feasibility

The project can be carried out as planned. The implementation pathway follows a clear logical sequence that connects the stated methods to the intended outputs without requiring contingencies that are beyond the team's control.

**Sequential architecture.** The landscape analysis and industry interviews in Year 1 feed directly into the panel materials and initial question list that launch the Delphi process in Years 2–3; the ranked priorities from surveys 1 and 2 determine which three issues receive detailed legal analysis and policy development in surveys 3–5; the stakeholder meeting validates and enriches the policy alternatives before the final dissemination [1]. This sequential dependence is a strength rather than a vulnerability because each downstream component draws on outputs that the upstream work is designed to produce—the project is not betting on uncertain discoveries to unlock later stages.

**Schedule.** The four-phase structure spans three years. Year 1 concentrates on landscape research, interviews, integration, and initial policy engagement. Year 2 forms the panel, runs the question-prioritisation surveys (surveys 1–2), selects the three issues, and begins legal analysis and the year-two Capitol Hill briefing. Years 2–3 complete the policy-development surveys (surveys 3–5), the two-day Washington meeting, and the year-three briefing [1]. The 20-month Delphi engagement—five surveys plus a face-to-face meeting—is more extended than the two to three rounds typical in healthcare Delphi studies [2], but the deliberate pacing reflects the Policy Delphi's purpose of iterative exploration and refinement rather than rapid convergence toward a single position. The GPPC's prior experience convening multi-stakeholder initiatives, including a national summit with more than 60 participants from regulatory agencies, professional organisations, industry, and laboratories [1], provides a credible operational foundation for managing this timeline.

**Access and recruitment.** The documentary landscape depends on publicly available information—company websites, patent databases, NEXIS, LexisNexis, PubMed—supplemented by direct company contact when necessary [1]. These sources are within the reach of a well-resourced policy research centre and do not require negotiated data-sharing agreements or privileged regulatory access. The interview sample of up to 20 senior executives from at least 40 eligible organisations [1] is adequate for thematic saturation in qualitative research. The Delphi panel of approximately 40 members, purposively recruited from three constituencies—industry, healthcare, and other stakeholders including regulators, policy staff, patient groups, and IP experts [1]—is above the median panel size of 17 reported in Boulkedid et al.'s systematic review [2] and deliberately heterogeneous, making the panel qualitatively informative even though it is not a statistically representative sample. The recruitment strategy—identifying candidates through academic and trade literature, industry leader discussions, and advisory board recommendations, then ranking and approaching them by expertise and policy interest [1]—is systematic and appropriate for a purposive panel.

**Operational risks.** Panel attrition over a 20-month, five-survey engagement is a genuine concern, and the proposal does not specify a minimum response threshold or a formal retention strategy beyond the initial disclosure of the commitment [1]. However, the panel size of approximately 40 provides margin above the minimum needed for meaningful qualitative diversity, and the two-day meeting creates an opportunity to recover perspectives that may be underrepresented in later survey rounds. The investigator-and-advisory-board selection of three policy issues from the stakeholder-ranked priorities [1] introduces a deliberate gatekeeping step, but the proposal is transparent about this and commits to publishing the full priority list so that issues outside the three selected domains remain visible [1]. Neither of these risks threatens the delivery of the core outputs—a systematic industry landscape, a ranked priority agenda, and reasoned policy alternatives for three domains—because the design does not depend on complete panel retention or on a purely algorithmic transition from rankings to policy development.

**Adaptive measures.** The project commits to monitoring developments in WGS technologies, business models, regulations, and litigation throughout the study, prioritising broader questions that cut across particular technologies, and maintaining relationships with stakeholders to identify changes [1]. The dissemination plan allocates effort specifically to translating academic materials into formats accessible to policy audiences [1], recognising that the uptake pathway for policy research differs from that of conventional academic publication.

## Ethics and safety

The human-participant activities are low-risk, and the proposed ethical and data-protection arrangements are proportionate and appropriately specified. The project involves professional adults contributing in their expert capacity through semi-structured interviews and Delphi surveys and a meeting; it does not include clinical interventions, biological samples, vulnerable populations, or experimental manipulations that would require more elaborate safeguards. The ethical framework is well-matched to the principal challenge of this research: enabling informed, candid policy discussion among stakeholders who may hold competing commercial or institutional interests, while protecting the confidentiality of individual contributions.

**Participant activities.** Aim 1 includes semi-structured telephone interviews with up to 20 senior executives or scientific officers from WGS organisations, addressing non-proprietary views of business models and clinical integration pathways [1]. Aim 2 involves approximately 40 panel members in five online surveys and a two-day Washington meeting, with the surveys eliciting ratings, rankings, open-ended suggestions, and iterative feedback on policy questions and alternatives [1]. Recruitment will not exclude people on the basis of sex, race, or ethnicity, although the proposal appropriately notes that the demographic composition of the professional populations from which participants are drawn is not sufficiently defined to specify demographic enrolment targets [1]. Withdrawal is permitted at any point, and participants may decline individual questions [1].

**Consent and residual risks.** Interviews and the summit will use verbal consent after advance provision and discussion of study information [1]. The investigators explicitly acknowledge the residual risk that commercially or legally sensitive information could be disclosed, including information potentially subject to subpoena, and commit to requesting a Certificate of Confidentiality and informing participants about its purpose and limitations [1]. This is an appropriate additional safeguard given that industry participants may discuss business strategies, and Delphi panel members may express views that could be sensitive in their institutional contexts.

**Data protection.** Identifying information will be held securely and separately from numbered interview recordings and transcripts [1]. Electronic survey access will use individual links or passwords, and responses will be encrypted and password-protected. The summit will be closed to outside participants and the press, transcripts will be de-identified before analysis, comments will be reported in aggregate, and the meeting recording will be destroyed after transcription [1]. The two-letter survey code system [1] allows the research team to track responses across rounds for analytical purposes while preserving anonymity among panel members. These measures address the specific risks of a multi-stakeholder policy process—namely, that attribution of particular positions to identifiable individuals or organisations could chill candour or create professional repercussions.

**Dissemination and confidentiality.** Study findings will be shared through publications, websites, public and policy briefings, and multiple accessible formats, while the public products—the landscape, priorities, policy alternatives, and methodological findings—will be presented in aggregate terms that do not reveal individual contributions [1]. There is no expected direct benefit to participants beyond ensuring that their perspectives inform policy development, which is an honest characterisation of the participation bargain in policy research of this kind. No material safety concerns arise from the research operations themselves: the work is desk-based and meeting-based, without laboratory, clinical, or field components that would generate occupational, environmental, or biosafety risks.

## Management

The management plan is adequately specified and draws on complementary institutional expertise with a clear division of responsibilities, though the absence of explicit quality-assurance procedures for the coordinated legal analyses introduces a modest coordination risk.

**Leadership and coordination.** The project is jointly led by Kaufman and McGuire, with a division of labour that maps sensibly onto the work packages: Kaufman oversees the Delphi process and dissemination, McGuire oversees development of policy approaches, and both share responsibility for the landscape analysis [1]. Weekly communication between the PIs and regular project meetings for key personnel across the four institutions are specified [1], establishing a rhythm of coordination appropriate to a multi-site project with interdependent work streams. The landscape produced in Year 1 must inform the panel materials and initial question list for the Delphi; the ranked priorities from surveys 1 and 2 determine which three issues receive detailed legal analysis; and the legal white papers must be available before survey 3 [1]. These sequential dependencies mean that delays or misalignments in one component could compress downstream work, making the specified coordination mechanisms more than a formality.

**Institutional expertise.** The four collaborating institutions bring complementary and non-redundant expertise. The Genetics and Public Policy Center at Johns Hopkins contributes prior landscape analyses of genetic testing companies, demonstrated experience with stakeholder engagement methods including interviews, surveys, focus groups, and town halls, and oversight of the Delphi implementation [1]. Baylor (McGuire) supplies expertise in the regulation of direct-to-consumer testing and ethical and policy dimensions of whole-genome research [1]. Duke (Cook-Deegan and colleagues) provides deep knowledge of DNA sequencing technologies, patents, business models, and intellectual-property issues in multi-allele testing. The Center for Medical Technology Policy (Tunis and Deverka) brings experience in coverage and reimbursement for molecular diagnostics and pharmacogenomics [1]. Messner's experience conducting qualitative stakeholder research, organising technical panels, and implementing Delphi processes [1] rounds out the operational capability. This distribution of expertise aligns with the three policy domains—analytical validity and testing quality, patents and licensing, and reimbursement and payment—that structure the detailed policy work [1].

**Advisory board and specialist support.** An eight-member advisory board, distinct from the 40-person stakeholder panel, provides external scrutiny at five video-conference meetings scheduled across the three-year project [1]. Its expertise encompasses clinical genetics, sequencing technology, regulation, economics, intellectual property, and consumer protection—a breadth that covers the main substantive domains of the project. The board's specified roles—clarifying study goals, discussing the landscape, advising on the selection of three issues, reviewing developing policy approaches, and guiding dissemination and future research [1]—are well-defined and appropriately sequenced. Burness Communications supports stakeholder identification, communication strategy, events, and archiving [1], a pragmatic delegation of a specialised function that the research team need not supply internally.

**Legal analysis coordination gap.** The principal limitation in the management plan concerns the parallel legal analyses conducted by the three institutions (GPPC, Duke, and CMTP) for the three selected policy questions [1]. The proposal specifies a common analytical framework—examining intended purposes, breadth and precision, enforcement, and conflicts or redundancies—and states that Kaufman and McGuire will coordinate the analyses, producing comparable white papers for panel members. However, it does not describe how differences in legal interpretation across the three teams will be identified and resolved, or how the white papers will be quality-assured before distribution to the Delphi panel. Given that these papers will frame the policy alternatives that panel members subsequently evaluate and rank in surveys 3–5 [1], any errors, omissions, or biases in the legal analyses could cascade through the remaining Delphi rounds. This is not an argument that the legal work will be flawed—the investigators have relevant expertise—but that the management plan should include a review step, such as cross-institutional peer review of the white papers or advisory board scrutiny before panel distribution, to mitigate this dependency risk.

**Adaptive management and dissemination.** The schedule is appropriately sequenced and acknowledges the need for adaptive management in a dynamic field. The team commits to monitoring developments in WGS technologies, business models, regulations, and litigation, and to reassessing the research strategy as needed, prioritising broader questions that cut across particular technologies [1]. The dissemination plan specifically allocates effort to translating academic materials into formats accessible to policy audiences [1], recognising that the management of outputs for policy impact requires different skills and timelines than conventional academic publication.

Taken together, the management arrangements are proportionate to the project's scope and complexity. The division of responsibilities is clear, the expertise is well-matched to the work, the advisory board provides appropriate external oversight, and the coordination mechanisms are specified. The gap in quality-assurance procedures for the parallel legal analyses is a manageable concern that does not undermine the overall adequacy of the management plan, but it represents a point where greater specificity would strengthen confidence that the legal inputs to the Delphi process will be consistently reliable across the three domains.

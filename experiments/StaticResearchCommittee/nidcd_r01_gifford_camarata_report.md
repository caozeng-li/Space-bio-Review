# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project will conduct the first randomized, double-blind, waitlist-controlled trial of image-guided cochlear implant programming (IGCIP) in 72 children aged 6–12. By using individual cochlear anatomy and electrode location to deactivate redundant or extracochlear electrodes, the study tests a clinically tractable intervention hypothesized to improve the electrode-neural interface. Beyond assessing immediate gains in auditory resolution and speech recognition, the project's most valuable expected knowledge lies in its ambitious, mechanistic framework that follows children for 24 months. It will test whether and how IGCIP-induced changes in spectral, temporal, and spectrotemporal auditory function influence downstream growth in phonological awareness, speech production, language, and literacy — illuminating the developmental pathways that are distinct in this population.

The proposal is strongly supported by a clear rationale for moving beyond the existing, uncontrolled within-subject studies that define the current evidence base, and by substantial preliminary data from the applicants demonstrating IGCIP's feasibility and variable benefit. The scientific premise is further strengthened by the team's own published findings that pediatric CI users show a dissociation between spectral resolution and speech-language outcomes, directly motivating the need to identify alternative auditory and linguistic pathways. The main limitations are analytical: the team's recent work found no significant correlation between the very auditory resolution measures proposed as mediators and speech recognition in children, which weakens the core mechanistic hypothesis. Furthermore, the sample size of 60 is modest for the planned cross-lagged and mediation path models, relegating these analyses to generating effect-size estimates rather than definitive mechanistic proof.

Weighing its considerable strengths against its constraints, this project is a well-conceived investigation with high potential. The randomized design provides powerful causal evidence for the intervention's primary auditory effects, a critical advancement over current knowledge. The explicit acknowledgment that developmental analyses are not contingent on a significant treatment effect reflects an appropriate, hypothesis-generating approach for the pathway investigations. The limitation in statistical power for mediation is counterbalanced by the study's pragmatic goal of generating precise effect-size estimates and testable causal hypotheses within a novel framework. The proposal's current plan is sufficient for support, delivering a substantial scientific contribution.

**Recommendation: Support**

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**Scientific value is excellent (A, confidence A).** The project targets a well-defined gap with a design that would deliver the first randomized evidence on image-guided CI programming in children, while also producing a mechanistic longitudinal dataset linking auditory change to developmental outcomes that does not currently exist.

The clinical problem is persistent and well-documented: many early-implanted children continue to show language and reading difficulties despite restored auditory access. The proposal presents institutional data showing that mean language and vocabulary standard scores for pediatric implant users remain below the age-normative range even when implanted before 24 months [1]. The background literature identifies a specific explanatory gap: spectral resolution — a strong predictor of speech recognition in adults — is not reliably associated with pediatric outcomes. Children with prelingual deafness may rely more on temporal-envelope or combined spectrotemporal information, yet which auditory dimensions support their speech and language development, and whether a clinically tractable intervention can alter them, remains unknown [1].

IGCIP addresses this gap by using individual cochlear anatomy and electrode position to deactivate contacts predicted to produce overlapping or extracochlear stimulation, while preserving as many useful active channels as possible [1]. The intervention is methodologically strategic because it provides a controlled, anatomically-grounded source of change at the electrode-neural interface — an intervention that can be experimentally manipulated and whose auditory consequences (spectral, temporal, spectrotemporal) can be directly measured. No randomized trial of IGCIP, or of any anatomically-informed electrode deactivation approach, exists in any population. The systematic review by Lien et al. (2025) identified 30 studies of electrode-neural-interface-informed re-mapping, only one of which was an RCT, reporting a moderate pooled effect size of *d* = 0.48 for speech-in-noise with substantial between-study variability; none addressed pediatric or developmental outcomes [2]. The existing pediatric IGCIP evidence comes exclusively from single-group pre-post assessments, including the team's own series of 41 children where 16% improved significantly in quiet speech, 28% improved in sentence-in-noise, and 31 of 41 chose to retain the IGCIP map [1]. These data demonstrate feasibility and signal but cannot separate intervention effects from maturation, repeated testing, or regression to the mean — a limitation the randomized waitlist-control design directly addresses.

Beyond the primary efficacy question, the project's intellectual architecture generates value that is not contingent on a significant average treatment effect. The design measures spectral, temporal, and spectrotemporal resolution alongside speech recognition, speech production, phonological awareness, language, and reading at seven timepoints over 24 months [1]. The planned cross-lagged and path analyses will examine whether IGCIP-induced auditory changes predict downstream developmental gains, and through which pathways — for example, auditory → speech recognition → phonological awareness → reading, versus direct auditory → language routes [1]. The proposal honestly acknowledges that these developmental analyses are "not contingent on a significant average intervention effect" because individual variation in auditory change can still inform the proposed pathways [1]. This dual logic — an efficacy trial nested within a mechanistic cohort study — means the project will produce a longitudinal, multi-domain dataset on auditory-cognitive-developmental relationships in pediatric CI users that the field currently lacks, regardless of the magnitude of the between-group treatment contrast.

The knowledge obtainable is therefore layered. At minimum, the project yields the first unbiased estimate of IGCIP's short-term auditory and speech-recognition effects in children, informing clinical programming practice directly. If individual variation in auditory change is sufficient, it generates evidence about which auditory dimensions predict speech, language, and literacy growth, and whether phonological awareness mediates these relationships — questions that the team's own published work on 47 pediatric CI users has shown cannot be resolved by static, cross-sectional designs, where neither spectral nor temporal resolution correlated significantly with speech recognition after correction for multiple comparisons [3]. The value of moving from cross-sectional correlation to longitudinal, experimentally-induced change is substantial, even if the path models ultimately provide effect-size estimates and "tentative causal hypotheses for further research" rather than definitive mediation proof [1].

## Innovation

**Innovation is excellent (A, confidence A).** The project brings genuine originality to a field where the evidence base has been built almost exclusively on uncontrolled within-subject designs. It proposes the first randomized controlled trial of any anatomically-informed electrode deactivation strategy in any population — a methodological advance that is both overdue and foundational. The conceptual framework that structures the developmental pathway analyses is equally novel in pediatric CI research.

**A methodological step change.** The current evidence for IGCIP, despite being published in respected journals by this team and others, rests entirely on single-group pre-post assessments. The team's own pediatric series of 41 children documented that 16% improved significantly in quiet speech, 28% improved in sentence-in-noise, and 31 of 41 chose to retain the IGCIP map [1]. Noble et al. (2016) reported statistically significant improvement in 21 pediatric ears using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test after approximately four weeks [4]. These findings are scientifically useful but methodologically incapable of distinguishing an IGCIP effect from maturation, repeated-test learning, or regression to the mean — confounds that are especially salient in children aged 6–12, where developmental gains over 12–24 months are expected. The systematic review by Lien et al. (2025) identified 30 studies of electrode-neural-interface-informed re-mapping; only one was an RCT, and none addressed pediatric populations or developmental outcomes [2]. The proposed double-blind, randomized waitlist-control design therefore represents a qualitative methodological step change, not an incremental refinement.

**A novel mechanistic framework.** The project does not merely test whether IGCIP improves hearing; it constructs and tests a mechanistic model that has no precedent in the IGCIP literature and few parallels in pediatric CI research more broadly. The pathway architecture — linking experimentally-induced changes in spectral, temporal, and spectrotemporal resolution to speech recognition, phonological awareness, language, and reading through cross-lagged and path analyses over seven timepoints [1] — is motivated by a specific finding from the team's own work that challenges standard assumptions. In 36 pediatric CI recipients, spectral modulation detection did not correlate significantly with monosyllabic word recognition (*r* = .30, *p* = .07), unlike the strong correlation (*r* = .51–.52, *p* \< .0001) observed in 542 adult CI recipients [5]. Camarata et al. (2026) further showed that pediatric CI users could display phonological processing scores more than two standard deviations below the normative mean yet maintain language and reading comprehension within the typical range, with spectral resolution uncorrelated with phonological processing [6]. These findings converge on a picture where the auditory foundations of language and literacy in children with CIs differ fundamentally from those in adults — and the proposal builds a longitudinal experimental design around that precise insight.

**Innovation in measurement.** The inclusion of temporal modulation detection at three rates (4, 32, and 128 Hz) and spectrotemporal tasks alongside the more conventional spectral modulation detection [1] is a direct response to the emerging evidence that pediatric CI users may rely on temporal-envelope cues more than on fine spectral detail [1]. The addition of subclinical acoustic speech-production measures — harmonic-to-noise ratio, formant analysis, vowel space, speaking rate — alongside standardized articulation scores [1] is similarly forward-looking, motivated by preliminary cases where children showed no change in standard scores yet demonstrated measurable improvements in acoustic precision and high-frequency speech energy after IGCIP [1]. These measurement choices operationalize the project's central hypothesis about alternative auditory and linguistic pathways in a way that conventional clinical batteries alone could not.

## Rigor

**Rigor is moderate (C, confidence B).** The randomized waitlist-control design provides a strong inferential framework for the primary auditory and speech-recognition aims, but significant limitations weaken the support for the mechanistic pathway aims that constitute the project's most ambitious intellectual contribution.

**Strengths of the experimental design for Aim 1.** The between-group comparison is well-protected. Permuted-block allocation with blocks of four [1], blinded outcome assessment, baseline clinical-map optimization requiring CI-aided thresholds of 20–25 dB HL [1], and equipment checks at every visit reduce sources of bias that have confounded prior uncontrolled IGCIP studies. The waitlist design provides an explicit counterfactual for maturation and repeated testing — a critical advance for children aged 6–12 where untreated developmental gains are expected. The power analysis indicates that 30 participants per arm yields 80% power for between-group effects of *d* ≈ 0.67–0.74 [1], consistent with the magnitude of benefit observed in the preliminary series where 28% of children improved on sentence-in-noise measures [1]. The within-child comparisons (unmodified ear, bilateral condition) provide complementary information without undermining the primary between-group contrast [1]. These design features make Aim 1 a well-powered, causally interpretable test of IGCIP's short-term auditory efficacy.

**Uncertain status of the proposed mediators.** The auditory resolution measures central to the pathway analyses have weak empirical support as mediators in the target population. DeFreese et al. (2024), on a sample of 47 pediatric CI users from this team, found no statistically significant correlations between spectral or temporal resolution and speech recognition after correction for multiple comparisons, although some moderate effect sizes (*r* \> 0.3) were observed for vowel recognition and speech-in-noise measures that did not survive correction [3]. The proposal acknowledges in its background that "spectral resolution does not consistently predict speech recognition" in children and that children "may rely more heavily on temporal-envelope or combined spectrotemporal information" [1], yet the path models in Aims 2 and 3 position resolution measures as the primary channel through which IGCIP is expected to influence downstream outcomes [1]. The dissociation between spectral resolution and speech-language outcomes motivates the study but also means that the core mechanistic premise — that IGCIP-induced resolution changes will mediate developmental gains — has weak empirical support in the target population. The inclusion of temporal and spectrotemporal tasks [1] is scientifically appropriate and may reveal relationships that cross-sectional studies missed, but this remains an exploratory premise rather than a tested assumption.

**Limited precision of the mechanistic pathway analyses.** The sample size of 60 is modest for cross-lagged panel and mediation models. The proposal reports that detectable path coefficients are approximately .35 and that detectable differences between path coefficients are .4–.5 [1]. These are large effects; smaller but theoretically meaningful path differences — for example, distinguishing direct auditory→language paths from speech-recognition-mediated paths — will not be resolvable. The proposal appropriately characterizes these analyses as generating "tentative causal hypotheses for further research" and emphasizing "effect-size estimation and mechanistic understanding" [1]. This candid framing is scientifically honest but reflects the limited precision of what Aims 2 and 3 can deliver from N = 60.

**Unaddressed practice effects.** Auditory resolution tasks are administered at seven timepoints over 24 months [1]. The proposal states that "multiple lists permit repeated testing without item-list repetition" for speech recognition [1], which addresses list-specific learning but not procedural learning on the forced-choice resolution tasks. The between-group comparison controls for practice effects common to both arms, but the within-group trajectory analyses and cross-lagged models that pool data across arms may conflate genuine auditory change with test familiarization. The proposal does not discuss whether task-specific familiarization effects asymptote or accumulate across seven administrations, which matters for interpreting individual growth curves.

**Appropriate analytical provisions.** The comprehensive measurement battery — including both clinical standardized instruments (CTOPP-2, TAPS-3 for PA; WRMT-III, GORT-5 for reading; ROWPVT-4, PPVT-4, TACL-4, CELF-4, SPELT-3 for language; GFTA-3 for articulation; Leiter-3 for nonverbal cognition) and subclinical acoustic measures [1] — the intent-to-treat framework, bootstrapped confidence intervals, and the plan to examine missing-data patterns without imputation for assessments expected to be nonrandom [1] are appropriate and well-specified. The proposal's dual strategy — an efficacy trial for Aim 1 paired with individual-difference pathway analyses for Aims 2 and 3 that are not contingent on a significant average treatment effect [1] — is a defensible response to the preliminary evidence of variable benefit, but it places the most novel scientific claims outside the protection of the randomized comparison and into analyses that the current design can support only at coarse resolution.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility is good (B, confidence B).** The technical pipeline is well-defined and supported by published operational experience; the recruitment base is appropriate; and the intervention can be delivered within the planned resources. Two areas would benefit from additional specification — CT radiation protocol details and blinding integrity assessment — but neither constitutes a decisive obstacle on the available evidence.

**Mature technical pipeline.** The IGCIP intervention rests on a technically mature pipeline with published validation. The process begins with an atlas derived from ten micro-CT scans of human cadaveric cochleae, registered to the individual child's preoperative clinical CT using the bony cochlear outline. A postoperative CT identifies the electrode-array centerline, and three-dimensional registration locates each contact relative to frequency-mapped neural endings. Distance-versus-frequency curves then identify electrodes whose stimulation regions overlap those of neighboring contacts, as well as extracochlear electrodes that leave assigned frequencies without effective intracochlear delivery [1]. The team has already applied this method to 21 pediatric ears in a published series, demonstrating statistically significant improvement in word and sentence recognition [4]. Their expanded preliminary series of 41 children, with 31 of 41 choosing to retain the IGCIP map, confirms that the imaging-to-programming workflow is operational and that children tolerate the procedure [1]. The algorithm target is concrete: the minimum-error neural-network procedure seeks to maximize the number of active electrodes above eight while removing redundant or extracochlear contacts [1]. The proposal acknowledges that if an IGCIP plan is identical to the clinical map, the participant will be withdrawn because no programming intervention exists to test [1], which is a realistic operational provision.

**Recruitment access and schedule.** The recruitment plan draws on Vanderbilt's large pediatric implant program. Children becoming age-eligible during the project period will be recruited from this existing clinical base, with staggered enrollment of approximately 12–14 during summer months and 5–6 during winter breaks across the first three years, yielding roughly 18–20 children per year [1]. The eligibility criteria are restrictive — children must be 6–12 years old with prelingual deafness, bilateral moderate-to-profound sensorineural loss, at least one CI, implantation before age three, nonverbal cognition standard score of at least 85, and no autism, neurological disorder, general cognitive impairment, or major temporal-bone abnormalities [1] — but the team has already studied 41 children meeting similar criteria in their preliminary work, demonstrating that the pool is accessible. The proposal does not provide annual pediatric implant volume figures; this information would strengthen confidence but its absence does not demonstrate infeasibility given the demonstrated recruitment precedent.

**Identified gaps.** Two aspects warrant attention without undermining overall feasibility. First, the CT radiation protocol description is limited. The proposal references a "linked approved protocol" with radiation oversight and institutional review [1], and states that existing clinically obtained scans will be used when available, but does not describe dose parameters, the specific low-dose CT technique, or the projected effective dose for participating children. Postoperative CT in pediatric CI recipients is a recognized clinical practice, and low-dose protocols exist. The absence of detail leaves the actual exposure somewhat underspecified in the proposal text, though the institutional oversight provides a governance framework.

Second, the blinding procedures are described in general terms: participants and outcome assessors are "intended to remain blind," with allocation access restricted to the PIs and statistician who do not administer or score assessments [1]. The IGCIP intervention modifies the child's everyday listening map, and children or parents may notice changes in sound quality. The proposal does not discuss how blinding success will be assessed — for example, by asking participants or assessors to guess group assignment — nor does it specify procedures for maintaining blinding during the home-based smartphone testing at one and 13 months, where parents administer the tests and could potentially infer group assignment [1]. Blinding in device-intervention trials is inherently imperfect, and the primary outcomes — auditory resolution thresholds and standardized speech recognition tests administered by blinded assessors — are relatively objective. The proposal's analysis strategy, which does not depend on a significant average intervention effect [1], provides resilience against modest expectation effects. The documented pipeline, recruitment access, and technical specifications together support the feasibility judgement at a good level.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety arrangements are good (B, confidence B).** The principal risks are identified with proportionate management strategies, including an explicit safety-monitoring protocol for performance decline. The CT radiation protocol description lacks dose-specific detail, and blinding integrity procedures are not fully elaborated, but these gaps do not undermine the overall ethical adequacy given the institutional oversight framework and the objective nature of the primary outcomes.

**Management of performance decline risk.** The proposal identifies reduced speech recognition following IGCIP activation as a primary risk [1] and addresses it through several protective layers. Before any experimental programming, conventional clinical maps are optimized to provide CI-aided thresholds of 20–25 dB HL from 250–6,000 Hz, with electrically evoked stapedial reflex thresholds verifying upper stimulation levels where feasible [1]. This ensures that any post-IGCIP decrement can be attributed to the intervention rather than to correctable clinical problems. After IGCIP activation, staff establish smartphone-based word-recognition scores at baseline and 12 months, and caregivers repeat the testing at home one month later. A decline exceeding the 95% test-retest interval triggers an option to restore the prior clinical map or to continue for one additional month until the next scheduled visit [1]. This protocol provides a defined threshold for clinical action and a clear restoration pathway, which is appropriate for an intervention where the preliminary data showed that 5% of children experienced worsening in quiet speech and 3% worsened in sentence-in-noise in the experimental ear [1]. The provision that participants withdrawn for poor performance may continue two-year outcome observation where possible [1] preserves the intention-to-treat framework while prioritizing participant welfare.

**Radiation exposure.** For radiation exposure from postoperative CT imaging, the proposal states that existing clinically obtained scans will be used when available, and that children implanted elsewhere may receive imaging through a linked approved protocol with radiation oversight and institutional review [1]. Postoperative CT in pediatric CI recipients is a recognized clinical practice. The limitation is that the proposal text does not describe the specific dose parameters or the projected effective dose. This level of specificity would strengthen the ethics documentation, but the combination of institutional oversight, the preference for existing clinical scans, and the established clinical acceptability of postoperative temporal-bone imaging in children provides a governance framework that supports the safety judgement.

**Blinding, data management, and oversight.** The proposal states that parents provide consent and children provide assent under institutional procedures [1]. Data management provisions are appropriate: REDCap and a centralized repository store coded data, a separate identity master list is maintained by the PIs, paper and electronic records are secured, and records are retained for at least six years [1]. De-identified data and methodological detail will be shared to support reproducibility. Adverse events are documented and reported, and independent monitoring is organized if the IRB requires it [1].

Blinding integrity presents a practical challenge inherent to device-intervention trials. The proposal states that participants and outcome assessors are intended to remain blind, with allocation access restricted to the PIs and statistician who do not administer or score assessments [1]. IGCIP modifies the child's everyday listening map, and children or parents may notice changes in sound quality that could unblind them. The proposal does not discuss how blinding success will be assessed, nor does it elaborate procedures for maintaining blinding during the home-based smartphone testing where parents administer the tests [1]. The practical consequence of this gap is modest: the primary auditory resolution and standardized speech recognition outcomes are administered by blinded assessors using objective psychophysical procedures, which limits the influence of participant or parent expectation. The waitlist design, where the deferred group receives IGCIP at 12 months, further reduces the incentive for differential attrition based on perceived assignment. An assessment of blinding success would strengthen internal validity but its absence does not create an ethical vulnerability.

## Management

**Management is good (B, confidence B).** The team is appropriately constituted with complementary expertise and clearly defined oversight responsibilities, supported by a plausible five-year schedule and monthly coordination mechanisms. The main limitation is the absence of explicit contingency planning for recruitment shortfalls and conflict resolution, though the demonstrated recruitment precedent and the team's collaborative track record mitigate these concerns.

**Team composition and responsibilities.** The principal investigators bring complementary expertise that maps directly onto the project's aims. Gifford oversees auditory perception, audiological assessment, and participant recruitment; Camarata oversees speech, language, and literacy outcomes [1]. This division reflects their respective research programs — Gifford on IGCIP and pediatric auditory psychophysics, Camarata on language development and phonological processing in children with hearing loss — and ensures that both the intervention delivery and the developmental outcome measurement are led by investigators with domain-specific expertise. The extended team includes otology for clinical oversight of the surgical and imaging aspects, image analysis and engineering for the IGCIP pipeline, biostatistics for the analytic framework, and consultants in phonological processing and speech production [1]. This multidisciplinary composition is appropriate for a project spanning cochlear anatomy, signal processing, psychophysics, speech-language assessment, and developmental pathway modeling.

**Coordination and schedule.** Day-to-day coordination is supported by a project manager and trained research staff who will schedule visits and administer assessments. Monthly team meetings are specified throughout the study period [1]. The statistician generates the permuted-block allocation schedule and retains allocation access alongside the PIs, who do not administer or score assessments [1] — a separation that protects blinding while keeping the analytical expertise embedded in the project governance.

The five-year schedule is detailed and accounts for the 24-month follow-up requirement. Year one establishes infrastructure: hiring, assessment acquisition, trial registration, randomization generation, and beginning recruitment of approximately 18–20 children. Years two and three continue staggered enrollment while earlier cohorts progress through their scheduled visits. Years four and five complete the longitudinal assessments and conduct integrated analyses after unblinding at the final 24-month visit [1]. The schedule accounts for the practical reality that children are best recruited around school breaks and that multiple cohorts must be tracked simultaneously. A no-cost extension is mentioned for dissemination, which is prudent for a project whose primary analyses depend on completed longitudinal follow-up.

**Identified gaps.** The proposal does not provide explicit contingency planning for recruitment shortfalls or conflict resolution among investigators. The eligibility criteria are restrictive [1], which narrows the eligible pool. The team's preliminary series of 41 children meeting similar criteria demonstrates that such children can be identified [1], but the proposal does not describe the program's annual pediatric implant volume, the proportion meeting criteria, or alternative recruitment pathways if the initial target proves optimistic. Similarly, while the PI roles are clearly delineated, the proposal does not describe a mechanism for resolving substantive disagreements about analytic decisions or interpretation — relevant given the project's dual logic as both an efficacy trial and a mechanistic cohort study, which could generate differing perspectives on the priority of analyses.

These are gaps in documentation rather than demonstrated weaknesses. The team's published track record — including the 41-child preliminary IGCIP series, the 47-child study of spectral and temporal resolution, and the Camarata et al. (2026) report on phonological processing and reading — shows sustained collaborative productivity and successful recruitment of the relevant pediatric population [1] [3] [6]. The monthly meeting structure provides a regular forum for addressing operational challenges, and the staggered enrollment design creates natural opportunities to adjust recruitment strategies across years without delaying the entire project. The judgement of good reflects the overall adequacy of the documented governance arrangements while acknowledging the contingency-planning gap as a modest limitation.

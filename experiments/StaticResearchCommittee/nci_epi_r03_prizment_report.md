# Project review

## Overall assessment

This R03 project is a secondary data analysis investigating whether circulating MHC class I-like proteins — MICA, MICB, and ULBP1–4 — measured in the blood of initially cancer-free individuals, can predict future colorectal cancer (CRC) risk. The work will use existing repeated plasma proteomics (SOMAscan), genetic, lifestyle, and long-term cancer outcome data from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) cohort. The most valuable expected knowledge is twofold: first, the first-ever prospective characterization of the four ULBP proteins in relation to incident CRC, and second, a novel analysis of whether short-term, pre-diagnostic changes in all six proteins provide additional risk information beyond a single baseline measurement, potentially offering new insight into immune surveillance dynamics during early cancer development.

The proposal's main supportive pillars are its strong scientific premise, the unique longitudinal resources of the ARIC cohort, and a generally well-considered statistical design that includes appropriate sensitivity analyses. The team's direct preliminary work in the same cohort establishes feasibility. However, the judgement is materially affected by a key measurement limitation: the SOMAscan platform provides a relative, epitope-dependent signal for these shed proteins which has not been validated against a quantitative, antibody-based method; this uncertainty means the results will be platform-specific rather than definitive measures of protein concentration. This is compounded by the ambitious but non-standard Mendelian randomization analysis. The team appropriately defers gold-standard assay validation and replication to future work, making this a hypothesis-generating study.

The scientific value and targeted innovation of the project, particularly the inclusion of four uncharacterized ULBP proteins and a dynamic change-over-time analysis, provide a worthy foundation. These strengths must be weighed against methodological limitations that constrain the strength of the immediate conclusions. While the analytic plan is thoughtfully constructed, the core measurement limitation — that SOMAscan RFU signals for these shed, polymorphic proteins are not yet validated against their true biological concentrations — and the non-standard approach to Mendelian randomization mean that the study's output will be more of a well-supported prompt for independent validation than a definitive result. Given the project's modest R03 scale, this trade-off is fundamentally acceptable: the plan responsibly generates the very evidence required to justify the more resource-intensive validation work it defers. The recommendation is therefore for support, as the project provides a sufficient, clear route to producing the first prospective data on this expanded protein panel and its dynamics.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**The project addresses a scientifically consequential question that is well-motivated by immune surveillance biology, and the attainable knowledge — while bounded by the exploratory character of an R03 — is a genuine descriptive advance.** The premise is that MHC class I-like proteins (MICA, MICB, and the ULBP family) act as NKG2D ligands whose shedding into the circulation may reflect early immune evasion during colorectal cancer development [1]. Prior work has concentrated on post-diagnosis settings, where associations between soluble MICA and advanced disease or poorer prognosis are already documented [1]. The present proposal shifts the question to the critical pre-diagnostic window: whether levels measured in initially cancer-free individuals can predict subsequent CRC. This reframing from prognostic to prospective risk marker is the central scientific contribution.

The project draws on the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) cohort, which provides repeated plasma proteomics at three time points (Visits 2, 3, and 5), genome-wide genotypes including deep whole-genome sequencing for approximately 12,000 participants, long-term cancer ascertainment through state-registry linkage, and rich lifestyle and clinical covariates [1]. These resources make it possible to examine both baseline protein–cancer associations (Aim 1a) and short-term pre-diagnostic changes as predictors (Aim 1b) [1]. The inclusion of ULBP1–4 alongside MICA and MICB is a material extension: the ULBP proteins have distinct structural properties — ULBP1–3 are GPI-anchored, ULBP4 is transmembrane — and their shedding biology differs from that of MICA/MICB [2]. Their circulating levels in relation to incident colorectal cancer have not been examined in any prospective cohort, so the proposed analysis would produce genuinely new descriptive knowledge.

**What can realistically be achieved within this 24-month secondary analysis must be distinguished from the long-term objective of informing CRC prevention and early detection** [1]. The immediate deliverables are: (i) the first prospective characterization of six circulating NKG2D ligands, singly and combined as a factor score, in relation to incident CRC; (ii) an analysis of whether short-term change (Visit 3 minus Visit 2) provides risk information beyond a single baseline measurement, using marginal structural models to address time-dependent confounding [1]; and (iii) a systematic examination of demographic, lifestyle, and genetic determinants of these protein levels and their trajectories (Aim 2) [1]. The secondary Mendelian randomization aim is explicitly exploratory and depends on identifying adequate genetic instruments [1].

**The knowledge gain is incremental rather than transformative, but proportionate to the R03 mechanism.** The core concept — that SOMAscan-measured MICA and MICB levels can be associated prospectively with CRC — was already demonstrated by the same investigative team in the same ARIC cohort (Wang et al., 2023), which reported lower sMICA levels associated with lower CRC risk in males (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.49–0.96) and identified rs2596542-T as associated with both lower sMICA and decreased CRC risk [3]. The current proposal adds four uncharacterized proteins and a change-over-time analysis that represents a conceptually distinct addition: it could, if informative, support a model in which dynamic changes in immune-regulating proteins — rather than static levels — signal evolving immune dysregulation or preclinical tumor shedding. The value therefore lies in expanding the evidence base from two proteins to six and from single measurements to trajectories, providing the first prospective data needed to justify the independent validation and replication studies that the proposal appropriately defers [1].

The descriptive contribution is well-scoped. It would not establish clinical utility, causal mechanisms, or generalizability, nor does it claim to. It would produce internally consistent, platform-dependent associations that represent a necessary and proportionate step toward understanding whether these immune-regulating proteins merit further investment as early risk markers.

## Innovation

**The proposal makes genuine, incremental advances beyond the team's own recently published work on MICA and MICB in the same cohort.** The 2023 Wang et al. paper already demonstrated that SOMAscan-measured sMICA and sMICB levels at ARIC Visit 2 are prospectively associated with CRC risk, finding lower sMICA associated with lower CRC risk in males (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.49–0.96) and identifying rs2596542-T as associated with both lower sMICA and decreased CRC risk [3]. That prior publication also reported genetic determinants of sMICA levels and included a sensitivity analysis using the mean of Visit 2 and Visit 3 protein levels as a single exposure [3]. The current proposal must therefore be assessed for what it adds beyond that established foundation.

Four distinct advances support the judgement that the contribution is meaningfully original for an R03-scale project.

**First, and most important, the proposal extends the protein panel from MICA and MICB to include the four ULBP family members (ULBP1–4).** These proteins are NKG2D ligands with distinct structural properties: ULBP1–3 and ULBP6 are GPI-anchored, while ULBP4 and ULBP5 are transmembrane proteins, and their shedding biology differs from that of MICA/MICB [2]. No published study has examined circulating ULBP levels in relation to incident CRC in any prospective cohort, so the proposed analysis would produce genuinely new descriptive knowledge about these understudied ligands.

**Second, the change-over-time analysis in Aim 1b represents a conceptual and methodological innovation.** The proposal examines short-term protein change (Visit 3 minus Visit 2) as the exposure, with cancer follow-up beginning at Visit 3 to ensure the change precedes the outcome period [1]. This design can distinguish whether rising protein levels provide risk information beyond a single baseline measurement — potentially supporting a model in which dynamic immune dysregulation, rather than static protein levels, signals developing neoplasia. Neither the team's prior work nor the broader literature has examined short-term change in these ligands as a predictor of cancer.

**Third, the principal-axis factor analysis combining six correlated proteins into a single exposure is a reasonable dimension-reduction strategy not previously applied to this protein family** [1]. It addresses the multiple-testing problem inherent in a six-protein panel while potentially capturing a shared biological signal. **Fourth, Aim 2's comprehensive determinant analysis** — simultaneously examining demographic, lifestyle, and genetic factors across all six proteins — has no close precedent and would characterize the correlates of these proteins in a population-based sample.

These advances are genuine but moderate in scale. The core question — whether circulating NKG2D ligands measured by SOMAscan precede CRC — was already answered affirmatively for MICA and MICB by the same investigators. The Wang 2023 publication established proof of principle for the biomarker concept, and the current proposal would confirm those findings (using the additional Visit 2 SOMAscan v4 measurements) while extending descriptive coverage to four additional proteins. The factor-score and change-over-time analyses offer the most distinctive scientific contributions and, if informative, could reshape how the field conceptualizes the relationship between immune-regulating proteins and early cancer development. The Mendelian randomization aim, while adding a causal-exploration dimension distinct from the observational aims, is explicitly exploratory and its methodological implementation is non-standard [1], which limits the weight it carries in the overall innovation assessment. Within the scope of an R03 secondary analysis, the project advances from two proteins to six, from single measurements to change-over-time analyses, and from primarily genetic determinants to a broader set of predictors — a proportionate and sufficiently distinct contribution that justifies pursuit beyond the team's prior publication.

## Rigor

**The methodological design is largely appropriate to the study's exploratory R03 stage, but a core measurement limitation and a material analytic gap constrain the strength of inference that can be drawn.** The rigor assessment focuses on whether the evidence generated can adequately support the stated aims: establishing prospective associations, characterizing short-term protein changes as predictors, and identifying determinants of protein levels.

### Strengths in the primary analytic design

The statistical design for Aims 1a and 1b is carefully constructed. In Aim 1a, multivariable Cox regression will estimate CRC hazard ratios for baseline protein levels, with proportional-hazards assumption testing and alternative models (nonproportional hazards, generalized gamma regression) specified as fallbacks [1]. The proposed sensitivity analyses — excluding diagnoses within two or five years of sampling to address possible reverse causation from occult cancer, examining interactions by sex, race, and cancer subsite, and handling missing values through inverse-probability weighting when missingness exceeds 10% — are appropriate and thorough [1]. The principal-axis factor analysis to combine six correlated proteins (anticipated correlations 0.4–0.55) into a single exposure is a defensible dimension-reduction strategy that addresses the multiple-testing problem, and the plan to also examine individual proteins safeguards against dilution of a genuine single-protein signal [1].

Aim 1b's use of marginal structural Cox models with inverse-probability-of-treatment weights to address time-dependent confounding by BMI, smoking, and other covariates is methodologically well-suited to the problem [1]. The timing rules — excluding cancer through Visit 3 and beginning follow-up at Visit 3 — correctly ensure the protein change precedes the outcome period [1].

### An unaddressed positivity concern

**The proposal does not discuss how it will assess the positivity assumption, a critical requirement for valid IPTW-based inference.** Near-positivity violations, where certain covariate patterns almost deterministically predict protein-change category, can substantially destabilize IPTW estimators, inflating both variance and bias [4]. With 11,340 participants and 306 CRC cases, and with protein change categorized into quartiles, some covariate-by-quartile cells may be sparse. Weight truncation, while commonly applied, can itself introduce bias [4]. The absence of a plan to assess positivity or to handle extreme weights is a material gap in an otherwise well-specified design.

### The Mendelian randomization aim

The Mendelian randomization aim is the weakest methodological component. The proposal specifies comparing standard probit with instrumental-variable probit models, using first-stage F-statistics above 10 as the instrument-strength criterion [1]. This approach is not aligned with contemporary MR practice, which has converged on two-stage least squares or inverse-variance weighted meta-analysis of SNP-specific Wald ratios, with extensive sensitivity analyses for horizontal pleiotropy (weighted median, MR-Egger, MR-PRESSO) [5]. The probit model carries the additional complication that its parameters are not collapsible, making causal interpretation more fragile than for linear IV estimators. Since this aim is explicitly described as secondary and exploratory, its limitations do not threaten the primary objectives, but they constrain what the causal exploration can contribute even on its own terms.

### The core measurement limitation

**The most consequential limitation concerns measurement validity.** The SOMAscan version 4 assay produces relative fluorescence units (RFU) that reflect aptamer binding to specific three-dimensional protein epitopes, not absolute protein concentrations. The proposal documents good technical reproducibility (median CV 5% in a 42-participant pilot, manufacturer's median CV 6.2% across 220 runs) [1], establishing adequate within-platform precision for epidemiological analysis. Precision, however, does not establish that the RFU signal tracks the biologically relevant protein concentration.

Three considerations are relevant here. First, SOMAmer reagents recognize specific protein conformations rather than amino acid sequence alone, and missense variants in surface-accessible regions can disrupt aptamer binding [6]. This is directly pertinent because the proposal's own preliminary data identify functional MICA variants — including rs1051792 (annotated as Val123Met in the proposal, though the literature more commonly uses Val129Met) — that alter the extracellular protein domain [1]; if such a variant affects the epitope the SOMAmer recognizes, measured RFU differences between genotype groups would reflect binding interference in addition to, or rather than, true concentration differences. Second, the proteins under study are shed ligands, and proteolytic cleavage may alter the conformation or remove the epitope recognized by the aptamer [1] [2]; the proposal does not address whether the SOMAmer reagents have been characterized for binding to shed versus membrane-bound forms. Third, cross-platform agreement between SOMAscan and antibody-based methods is moderate: a study measuring 107 shared proteins on the Olink PEA and SOMAscan 1.3K platforms reported a median Spearman correlation of ρ = 0.62 [7], and a recent comprehensive review notes that "a finding replicated on two platforms is substantially stronger evidence than the same finding replicated twice on one" [6].

The proposal implicitly acknowledges this limitation by deferring validation: "If associations are observed, the investigators plan later validation of individual protein measurements using standard methods and replication in an independent larger asymptomatic cohort, such as PLCO" [1]. For an R03 exploratory study, this staged approach — discovery on a research platform, then validation with calibrated assays — follows a logical sequence. The consequence for rigor is that the reported hazard ratios must be interpreted as SOMAscan-measured signal associations, not as calibrated protein-level effects, and a null finding would be difficult to interpret because it could reflect either a true absence of association or inadequate measurement. This uncertainty constrains but does not negate the study's capacity to achieve its stated goal of generating the first prospective evidence that would justify such validation.

## Feasibility

**The project is a secondary data analysis of an existing cohort; its implementation depends on the timely availability of SOMAscan proteomic data and a standard set of analytical tools, making it inherently feasible within the R03 small-grant framework, though one critical external dependency warrants attention.**

The ARIC cohort has already collected all participant data, plasma specimens, genotypes, and cancer outcomes through state-registry linkage and active follow-up [1]. The project requires no new participant contact, specimen collection, or laboratory work, which eliminates the logistical, recruitment, and biospecimen-quality risks that attend primary data-collection studies.

### Data availability and the principal dependency

The principal feasibility consideration is the status of the SOMAscan version 4 proteomic measurements. Visit 3 (1993–1995) and Visit 5 (2011–2013) assays are described as completed; Visit 2 (1990–1992) assays are described as "underway" at the time of the proposal [1]. Visit 2 supplies the baseline for Aim 1a (12,589 cancer-free participants, 360 CRC cases) and the starting point for the change analyses in Aim 1b [1]. The project schedule allocates months 1–3 to IRB arrangements and data extraction, with Aim 1 analyses beginning in month 4 [1], implying that the Visit 2 SOMAscan data are expected by approximately month 4. The proposal does not specify a contingency plan if the Visit 2 assays are delayed.

This is a noted vulnerability rather than a disqualifying flaw. The completed Visit 3 and Visit 5 data already provide a partial analytical foundation, and the team could begin Aim 2 determinant analyses or the secondary Mendelian randomization aim using Visit 3 as a provisional baseline while awaiting Visit 2 data. For an R03 mechanism, which explicitly accommodates exploratory risk, this external dependency is manageable. The assay quality-control infrastructure is well documented, with hybridization-control normalization, 26 pooled blinded plasma controls in triplicate across batches, and approximately 5% split-specimen duplicates [1]. The pilot work in 42 ARIC participants yielded a median CV of 5% and Spearman correlation of 0.89, establishing adequate within-platform precision [1].

### Analytical and computational resources

The analytical methods — Cox regression, factor analysis, marginal structural models with inverse-probability weights, linear mixed models, and instrumental-variable probit — are computationally modest for a dataset of approximately 12,000 participants and well within the capacity of standard desktop computing using SAS and Stata [1]. No specialized hardware, software licenses beyond those stated, or computational infrastructure is required.

The secondary Mendelian randomization aim is contingent on identifying genetic variants that are "strongly associated with the relevant proteins and associated with CRC" [1]. The proposal acknowledges that instrument strength will be assessed using first-stage F-statistics with the conventional threshold above 10 [1] and describes the aim as secondary and exploratory. If adequate instruments cannot be identified, the aim cannot proceed as designed, but this contingency is intrinsic to an exploratory R03 aim and does not threaten the primary objectives.

Taken together, the implementation pathway is well-defined, the principal resource dependencies are either already met or reasonably anticipated, and the residual uncertainty about Visit 2 assay timing is proportionate to the small-grant mechanism.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety are well assured: this is a secondary analysis of de-identified existing data with no participant contact, new collection, or intervention, and the described data-governance arrangements follow established cohort-study practice.**

The project draws exclusively on data, genotypes, and stored plasma samples already collected by the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities study. No new participant recruitment, specimen collection, clinical intervention, or environmental manipulation is proposed [1]. The analysis therefore introduces no physical, psychological, or social risk beyond that already managed by the parent cohort's original informed consent and ongoing ethical oversight. No vulnerable populations beyond those already enrolled in ARIC are involved, and no animal subjects or environmentally sensitive activities are contemplated.

The data-access and privacy protections are proportionate and conventional for a multi-center cohort study. Investigators receive coded research IDs; direct identifiers and the identity linkage remain with the ARIC Coordinating Center and are not released to the analytical team [1]. The proposal describes privacy protections covering transmission, handling, and distribution of study data, and commits to sharing descriptive and association results under NIH policy, with access to the underlying study dataset governed by ARIC leadership and publication-committee policies [1]. These arrangements ensure that individual-level data with potential re-identification risk remain within the established cohort governance structure, while analytical results are disseminated.

Institutional Review Board arrangements are scheduled for the project's first three months [1], consistent with the expectation that secondary analyses of de-identified data may qualify for expedited review or exemption, but that formal determination must be obtained before analytical work begins. The proposal's acknowledgment of this requirement is appropriate and realistically timed.

No biological, chemical, radiological, or physical hazards are associated with purely computational epidemiological analysis using standard desktop software [1]. The data-sharing plan — descriptive and association results shared openly, with access to the study dataset following ARIC publication-committee procedures — is consistent with both NIH data-sharing expectations and the norms of large, long-running cohort studies where careful stewardship of participant data is essential.

The only residual ethical consideration is the interpretability of the findings. If the project generates false-positive or false-negative associations due to the SOMAscan platform's measurement characteristics (discussed under Rigor), the downstream consequence could be either premature clinical enthusiasm or unwarranted dismissal of a potentially informative biomarker family. However, this risk is managed by the proposal's explicit framing: validation using standard assays and replication in an independent cohort are explicitly deferred to future work [1], and the project does not propose clinical implementation or direct return of results to participants. Within the scope of an R03 discovery study, the approach is ethically appropriate: it generates the prerequisite evidence for subsequent validation without exposing participants to misleading individual-level risk information.

## Management

**The 24-month plan follows a logical sequence and the principal investigator has directly relevant expertise, but the proposal's general description of team composition without named co-investigators or specified responsibilities limits confidence in workload distribution.**

The work is organized around a clearly sequenced timeline: months 1–3 cover IRB arrangements and data extraction; Aim 1 analyses (baseline protein–cancer associations and change-over-time analyses) occupy months 4–12; Aim 2 (determinants of protein levels and trajectories) runs months 13–18; and the secondary Mendelian randomization aim occupies months 19–24, with manuscript preparation overlapping months 13–24 [1]. This ordering is logical: the primary risk analyses, which constitute the proposal's core contribution, are prioritized in the first year, while the determinant and causal-exploration work follows once the protein measurements and their basic epidemiological behavior have been characterized.

The principal investigator, Anna Prizment, has published directly relevant prior work linking MICA variants to soluble MICA levels in a pancreatic cancer case–control study and has conducted preliminary analyses of MICA SNPs and CRC in ARIC [1]. The team's 2023 publication demonstrating prospective associations of SOMAscan-measured sMICA and sMICB with CRC in the same cohort establishes their familiarity with the ARIC data infrastructure, the SOMAscan platform, and the analytical methods required [3]. The proposal states that "the team combines molecular cancer epidemiology, computational genetics, and biostatistics" [1], and the range of methods described — factor analysis, Cox regression with sensitivity analyses, marginal structural models with inverse-probability weights, mixed models, and instrumental-variable approaches — spans standard cancer epidemiology and more specialized causal-inference and genetic-epidemiology techniques. This breadth of methods implies a multi-investigator team with complementary skills, which is appropriate for the proposed work.

**However, the management plan's documentation is thin by the standards that would support high confidence.** The proposal narrative describes only general combined expertise rather than naming specific co-investigators, delineating their roles, or specifying FTE allocations. The absence of named personnel responsibilities means that the workload distribution cannot be independently assessed: it is unclear who will implement the marginal structural models with inverse-probability weighting, who will handle the genetic analyses (including microsatellite genotyping from sequencing data), and who will lead the Mendelian randomization work. The 24-month timeline allocates months 19–24 to the secondary MR aim while manuscript preparation is ongoing across months 13–24 [1]; without clarity on staffing, it is difficult to judge whether the team has sufficient analytical capacity to run these activities concurrently without compromising quality.

The Mendelian randomization aim introduces a particular coordination consideration beyond what the timeline directly addresses. Its feasibility depends on identifying genetic instruments that are strongly associated with the relevant proteins and with CRC, a process described as ongoing: "the team is identifying such variants in ARIC and replicating associations in CRC datasets available through dbGaP" [1]. This instrument-discovery work must logically precede the MR analysis itself, and the proposal does not specify how it will be integrated with the Aim 2 genetic determinant analyses (which also examine variant–protein associations) or what analysis would substitute if adequate instruments prove unavailable. For an exploratory R03 aim this dependency is intrinsic, but the management implications — that resources allocated to months 19–24 may need to be redirected — are not discussed.

These documentation gaps do not demonstrate incapacity. The PI's track record with these proteins in this cohort, combined with the R03 mechanism's acceptance of modest scope and exploratory risk, supports the judgement that the team can deliver the planned analyses. The gaps do, however, reduce confidence in the management assessment from high to moderate: the plan is sufficient to proceed, but the internal coordination that will determine its success is not fully specified.

# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project investigates the molecular signals by which developing lymphoid cells induce growth, differentiation, and survival of thymic stromal cells—the less-understood lymphoid-to-stromal direction of thymic crosstalk. It will use an inducible growth model in IL7R-deficient mice, combined with region-matched microdissection of cortex and medulla, to generate a time-course microarray dataset. A published computational method will estimate stromal gene expression, and receptor-ligand pairs will be identified using the Human Plasma Membrane Receptome and literature-based searches. A multi-field prioritization scheme will rank candidates, and a small number of high-priority interactions will undergo initial biological validation in existing genetic mouse models.

The most valuable expected knowledge includes a dynamic stromal expression resource, a systematically prioritized and searchable collection of candidate signaling interactions, and proof-of-concept validation that can open a currently narrow field for future mechanistic studies. The proposal is supported by several key strengths. The scientific question is important and understudied; the experimental approach is systematic and discovery-driven rather than limited to pre-selected candidates; the core methods—including the transplantation model, microdissection, and computational stromal estimation—have been published by this experienced laboratory; and preliminary data demonstrate that RNA can be recovered from the small, atrophic tissue that will be studied. The prioritization framework, which uses multiple evidence fields rather than a single filter, is thoughtful.

The main limitations are structural to the exploratory design. The entire expression experiment depends on a growth trajectory that is currently theoretical and must be determined empirically; if the kinetics deviate substantially from expectations, the five planned sampling stages may not capture the most informative transitions. The computational stromal estimation method, while validated in prior work, rests on assumptions about the comparability of *in situ* and sorted lymphoid expression that are not fully re-examined here. Finally, the personnel plan concentrates both wet-lab and computational work on a single postdoctoral fellow, which is a notable constraint for a two-year project with an intensive analysis phase.

Overall, the proposal is a well-conceived exploratory project that targets a genuinely important gap in thymus biology. Its systematic, discovery-based design and multi-layered prioritization scheme are clear strengths, and the intended products—a dynamic expression resource, a candidate interaction database, and initial biological validation—are both valuable and proportionate to the R21 mechanism. The limitations do not undermine the project's capacity to identify novel candidate signals with associated evidence and to provide initial biological confirmation, which is exactly what it sets out to do. **The proposal's strong scientific value and innovative approach outweigh its manageable risks, making it supportable as submitted.**

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

This project addresses a genuinely important and understudied question in thymus biology: the molecular signals by which developing lymphoid cells support the growth, differentiation, and survival of thymic stromal cells [1]. The reciprocal relationship between thymic lymphocytes and stroma is well established—loss of lymphoid cells disrupts thymic architecture and stromal function, and hematopoietic transplantation restores functional thymic tissue in immunodeficiency [1]—yet the signals mediating the lymphoid-to-stromal direction remain poorly characterized compared with the extensively studied stromal-to-lymphoid instructions such as Notch ligands, IL-7, Kit ligand, and Cxcl12 [1]. Identifying these signals would fill a recognized gap and could inform understanding of thymic involution, regeneration after damage, and engineered thymic tissue. The scientific value of the proposed work lies less in resolving a single mechanism than in systematically generating the resources and candidates that would enable multiple mechanistic investigations to follow.

The attainable knowledge products are well-defined and proportionate to the exploratory R21 scope. Aim 1 will produce a dynamic, spatially resolved stromal expression resource covering five growth stages—baseline, growth initiation, log-phase, pre-peak (\~36 hours before the peak), and peak or approximate steady state—in both cortical and medullary compartments [1]. This time-course dataset, generated from an inducible physiological growth model in IL7R-deficient mice that avoids the stromal injury introduced by myeloablative conditioning [1], would itself constitute a useful reference for the thymus field, quite apart from the candidate signals ultimately prioritized.

Aim 2 will generate a systematically identified collection of receptor-ligand pairs by combining the curated Human Plasma Membrane Receptome resource (more than 900 receptors and 600 paired ligands) with a complementary literature-based search using Gene Ontology categories and co-citation analysis [1]. Requiring both a stromal receptor and its lymphoid partner to be expressed at a relevant stage provides a biological filter that should reduce false-positive candidates [1]. Aim 3 will attach multiple evidence fields—co-citation frequency, thymus-specific citation, expression dynamics by ANOVA, signal intensity, STRING interaction evidence, and practical reagent availability—to each candidate and preserve individual scores alongside possible combined scores in a searchable database [1]. This prioritization framework, with its layered evidence and links to existing mouse mutants, could seed multiple lines of investigation beyond this project. Aim 4 will provide initial biological validation for a small number of high-priority interactions through thymic size and cellular composition readouts in existing genetic models, with transplantation rescue tests available for lymphoid ligand candidates [1]. The proposal is appropriately explicit that detailed dissection of how individual signals control proliferation, differentiation, or survival is a subsequent research direction [1].

The value of this contribution is reinforced by its systematic, discovery-driven character. Known lymphoid-to-stromal signals—such as lymphotoxin-β receptor ligands, RANKL, and CD40L in medullary epithelial development—have largely emerged from focused genetic studies of individual pathways. The proposed work instead uses genome-wide expression profiling across a physiological growth response to nominate candidates without presupposing which pathways matter [1]. This breadth, combined with the spatial resolution provided by region-matched microdissection of cortex and medulla [1], represents a genuine departure from existing approaches and increases the likelihood of identifying signals not previously suspected to function in thymic stromal biology. The question is also carefully delimited: by using an already-formed organ with intact cortical and medullary compartments, the study focuses on growth and maintenance rather than embryonic specification of the thymic primordium [1], avoiding conflation with a distinct developmental process.

**Rating: A (excellent). Confidence: B (high).**

## Innovation

The project's innovation is excellent: it addresses a systematically understudied direction of thymic crosstalk through a discovery pipeline that combines spatial resolution, temporal dynamics, and genome-wide scope in a way that has no clear precedent in the field.

The lymphoid-to-stromal direction of thymic communication remains significantly less characterized than the reciprocal stromal-to-lymphoid signals. Known lymphoid-to-stromal signals have emerged primarily from candidate-based genetic studies of individual pathways. The proposal departs from this tradition by adopting a systematic, discovery-driven approach that does not presuppose which pathways are important, using genome-wide expression profiling across a physiological growth response to nominate candidates without prior selection bias [1]. This breadth is a genuine departure from the existing literature.

Several features of the experimental design constitute specific innovations beyond the choice of question. The inducible growth model in IL7R-deficient mice provides a physiological context for studying lymphoid-to-stromal signaling that avoids the stromal injury introduced by myeloablative conditioning, allowing the investigation of growth and maintenance in an already-formed organ with intact cortical and medullary compartments [1]. The region-matched microdissection strategy adds spatial resolution that bulk thymic analysis cannot provide. The computational method for estimating stromal expression from tissue-versus-lymphoid comparisons offers a practical solution to the difficulty of physically purifying stromal cells from a hypoplastic organ [1].

The receptor-ligand identification strategy introduces additional methodological originality. The use of the Human Plasma Membrane Receptome—supplied with mouse microarray identifiers for batch matching [1]—provides a structured foundation complemented by a broader literature-based search using Gene Ontology categories and automated co-citation analysis [1]. Merging both outputs while preserving their separate provenance is a pragmatic innovation that balances quality and breadth [1]. The multi-field prioritization scheme—combining receptor-ligand co-citation frequency, thymus-specific citation measures, expression dynamics by time-course ANOVA, signal-intensity decile, STRING evidence, and practical reagent availability [1]—is a thoughtful departure from single-filter ranking approaches. Preserving individual scores alongside possible combined scores in a searchable database allows users to apply their own weighting [1], increasing the resource's utility beyond this project.

These innovations collectively establish a discovery framework that could identify communication axes difficult to nominate from single-pathway studies. The systematic, spatially and temporally resolved approach has no evident precedent in the thymus field, and the combination of an expression resource, a prioritized candidate database, and initial biological validation represents an original contribution to the resources available for studying lymphoid-stromal communication. The innovation lies not in any single technical advance but in the coherent integration of established methods into a discovery pipeline directed at an understudied scientific question.

**Rating: A (excellent). Confidence: B (high).**

## Rigor

**The experimental design is logically coherent and well-matched to the project's exploratory goals, with the region-matched tissue–lymphoid sampling strategy as its central methodological strength. The principal limitations are the dependence on an unmeasured growth trajectory, assumptions underlying the computational stromal estimation, and the inherently provisional nature of the Aim 4 validation.**

### Design logic and sampling strategy

The design follows a clear inferential chain: a two-series kinetic study first determines the growth trajectory of the IL7R-deficient thymus after transplantation of wild-type progenitors, establishing five biologically distinct sampling stages [1]; a time-course microarray experiment then captures gene expression in microdissected cortex and medulla, compared with matched flow-sorted lymphoid populations from the contralateral lobe [1]; a published computational procedure estimates the stromal contribution by attributing the tissue signal not explained by the lymphoid component to stroma [1]; receptor–ligand pairs are identified [1]; a multi-field prioritization scheme ranks candidates [1]; and a small number of high-priority interactions receive initial biological validation in existing genetic models [1]. Each step depends on the preceding one—the sequential logic of a discovery pipeline that is appropriate for an exploratory R21 project [1].

The five-stage sampling design—baseline, growth initiation, log-phase, a pre-peak point approximately 36 hours before the peak, and the peak or approximate steady state [1]—is sensible for capturing the dynamic stromal response. The use of three independently pooled biological replicates per sample type (3 mice per pool) [1] provides adequate replication for the planned comparisons across 60 microarrays (5 stages × 4 sample types × 3 replicates). The Affymetrix MOE430\_2 platform is a mature technology with well-characterized performance. The proposal's treatment of expression thresholds is appropriately cautious: the median calculated signal serves as the default cut-off, with the 40th or 60th percentile available to adjust stringency, and binary present/absent calls are used supplementally to flag probesets unlikely to be expressed or potentially stromal-specific [1].

### Computational stromal estimation

The computational stromal estimation method, published by this laboratory in Griffith et al. (2009, *Immunity*), previously recovered known stromal signals including Notch ligands, IL-7, Kit ligand, Cxcl12, and MHC-associated genes [1], providing face validity. Two assumptions deserve attention. First, the method assumes that the lymphoid expression signature measured in sorted cells is comparable to that of lymphocytes *in situ* within the tissue sample. Dissociation and sorting may alter gene expression, and the tissue sample contains lymphocytes at microanatomical positions that differ from those recovered from the contralateral lobe. Second, the endogenous lymphoid population in IL7R-deficient mice changes in composition as wild-type donor cells expand; the proposal correctly notes that each lymphoid profile is used only to account for its own matched tissue sample [1], a sound analytical choice, but it means the stromal estimate at each time point rests on a changing lymphoid baseline. The method also estimates an aggregate stromal signal without distinguishing epithelial cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and other stromal constituents—the proposal appropriately indicates that histological methods would localize expression to specific cell types after candidate identification [1].

### Prioritization and validation

The prioritization framework in Aim 3 uses appropriate evidence fields—receptor–ligand co-citation frequency, thymus-specific co-citation with a proposed ratio measure, time-course ANOVA q-values, signal-intensity decile, STRING evidence, and reagent availability [1]—each addressing a relevant dimension for selecting validation candidates. The proposal states that individual measures can be used separately or in combination without specifying a formal combination rule [1]. This flexibility is appropriate for an exploratory R21 where final selection depends on the data obtained and on manual review of the strongest quantitative candidates against known biological roles; the commitment to preserve individual scores and make the database searchable [1] mitigates concerns about post-hoc flexibility. The Aim 4 validation is explicitly limited to establishing initial biological relevance through thymic size, cellular composition, and, where possible, transplantation rescue [1], deferring mechanistic dissection to future work [1]—a proportionate scope given its dependence on upstream discovery.

### Principal limitations

The most consequential limitation is that the entire expression study depends on a growth trajectory that remains theoretical: Figure 2 is explicitly described as "a theoretical growth curve used to define sampling stages, rather than observed results" [1]. The planned two-series kinetic study (a coarse survey at 4-day intervals followed by a finer one-day-interval series around growth onset and the peak) [1] is an appropriate mitigation, but if the kinetics prove slower, more variable, or less synchronous than anticipated, the five planned stages may not capture the biologically most informative transitions. A second material dependency is the performance of the stromal estimation on samples at the earliest growth stages, when the thymus is smallest and endogenous lymphocytes are most prominent. The demonstration that approximately 10 ng of RNA can be recovered from a single 20 µm section of an atrophic thymus comparable in size to the IL7R-deficient organ supports technical feasibility [1], but it does not address whether the computational separation remains reliable when the stromal-to-lymphoid ratio is most unfavorable.

These limitations do not make the design incapable of answering its scientific question; they define the boundaries of what can be concluded and qualify the strength of the resulting candidate set. The design can identify novel candidate lymphoid-to-stromal signals with associated evidence and provide initial biological confirmation for a small number of them, which is exactly what the proposal promises.

**Rating: B (good). Confidence: B (high).**

## Feasibility

**The project is largely feasible within the two-year R21 framework, grounded in published methods and an experienced laboratory. The principal risks are the unmeasured growth kinetics that define the critical path, the concentration of demanding wet-lab and computational work on a single postdoctoral fellow, and the downstream dependence of Aim 4 on candidates not yet identified.** These are characteristic of an exploratory discovery pipeline and are appropriately mitigated, but they warrant attention.

### Established methodological foundation

The core methods are established and accessible. The non-myeloablative transplantation model that induces growth in the IL7R-deficient thymus was published by this laboratory (Porritt et al., ref 9), as was the computational stromal-expression mapping method that compares microdissected tissue regions with matched lymphoid populations (Griffith et al. 2009, *Immunity*, ref 1) and successfully recovered known stromal signals [1]. The Human Plasma Membrane Receptome resource (Ben-Shlomo et al. 2003, ref 4) has been supplied with mouse microarray identifiers for batch matching [1], and the laboratory maintains the hCD2-Cre and Foxn1-Cre lines needed for conditional targeting [1]. Shared facilities for flow cytometry, laser microdissection, microarray processing, and computational infrastructure are confirmed as accessible [1]. The preliminary demonstration of RNA recovery from an atrophic thymus—approximately 10 ng from a single 20 µm section of tissue comparable in size to the IL7R-deficient organ [1]—provides direct evidence that the microdissection and RNA preparation are technically achievable at the required scale.

### Dependence on unmeasured growth kinetics

The critical path depends on growth kinetics that remain theoretical. The proposal is candid that "Figure 2 is consequently a theoretical growth curve used to define sampling stages, rather than observed results" [1]. The two-series kinetic design—a coarse survey at 4-day intervals (days 0–32, n = 3 per time point) followed by a finer one-day-interval series around growth onset and the peak [1]—is an appropriate strategy. However, the main expression experiment cannot begin until these kinetics establish the five sampling stages [1]. The 6–8 months allocated to this phase [1] is realistic for the transplantation and measurement, but leaves limited margin if the growth response proves slower, more variable, or less synchronous than the theoretical curve anticipates. The proposal does not describe contingency plans for this scenario, which represents a manageable but genuine risk.

### Personnel constraints

Personnel load is concentrated on a single postdoctoral fellow, who is assigned "most experimental and computational work" [1]. The workload across two years spans two kinetic transplantation series, microdissection of cortical and medullary regions from small thymic lobes, RNA preparation and processing of 60 microarrays, implementation of the published computational pipeline, receptor–ligand matching using the Receptome and GO-based searches with PubMed co-citation mining, multi-field prioritization scoring with clustering, construction of a searchable database, and execution of mutant mouse validation. The proposal acknowledges that "intensive data-processing work" is anticipated and that the informatic scientist Fallahi (10 % effort) "does not have capacity to absorb that workload alone" [1]. A single postdoc must therefore possess or rapidly acquire both microsurgery and transplantation skills and the bioinformatic competence to execute the computational pipeline. The PI's supervisory role and Fallahi's mentoring provide support, but the concentration of diverse technical demands on one person in a two-year project is a notable feasibility constraint.

### Provisional validation

Aim 4 validation resources are necessarily provisional. The proposal acknowledges that candidate identities and therefore the detailed validation experiments depend entirely on Aims 1–3 [1]. The estimate of fewer than 200 additional animals [1] is a reasonable planning figure, and the commitment to file an amendment after candidate selection is appropriate. However, the number and availability of suitable existing germline or conditional mutants cannot be confirmed until prioritized candidates emerge. This dependence is inherent to the exploratory design and does not by itself undermine feasibility, but it means Aim 4 carries higher uncertainty than Aims 1–3.

Taken together, the technical pathway is coherent, the core methods are published and accessible, and the resources—facilities, mouse colonies, informatic support, and shared equipment—are adequate. The feasibility concerns are characteristic of an exploratory R21 and do not render the plan unworkable, but the dependence on an unmeasured growth curve and the concentration of personnel effort are material constraints that qualify the confidence in full delivery of all four aims within the stated timeline.

**Rating: B (good). Confidence: B (high).**

## Ethics and safety

**The ethics and safety arrangements are proportionate and well-documented; the project involves standard rodent procedures with no hazardous materials or exceptional risks, and the animal justification, welfare provisions, and data-sharing plan meet the expected standard for this type of work.**

The project uses only established mouse models and standard laboratory procedures—non-myeloablative bone-marrow transplantation, timed harvesting of thymic tissue, and genetic analysis of existing mutant lines—that carry no unusual biological, chemical, or radiological hazards. No human subjects, infectious agents classified above BSL-2, or environmental release are involved.

The animal justification requests a total of 420 mice for the primary work, kinetic studies, and breeding, with a transparent breakdown: 90 IL7R-deficient mice to yield 45 male recipients for the main expression experiment, 90 wild-type donors, 108 mice per kinetic series (two series planned), and 24 animals assigned to breeding [1]. The main expression experiment uses 45 male recipients organized as five stages × three independently pooled samples × three mice per pool, which is the minimum structure needed to provide biological replicates at each stage. The practice of using marrow from a single donor for multiple recipients is noted as a reduction measure [1]. Aim 4 is expected to require fewer than 200 additional animals, with the commitment that an amendment will specify the number after candidate selection [1]. This phased approach—deferring the precise Aim 4 animal count until the upstream discovery work identifies the candidates to be validated—is appropriate for an exploratory R21 where the validation targets cannot be specified in advance and avoids committing animals to experiments whose design remains conditional.

The proposal describes veterinary oversight, anesthesia for procedures causing more than momentary distress, monitoring through recovery and at least daily for the subsequent week, and humane intervention or euthanasia for animals experiencing severe discomfort [1]. These provisions are consistent with standard institutional animal care and use committee requirements and are adequate for the described procedures. The justification for using an animal model—that thymic function and organization must be studied as an intact organ, with its three-dimensional architecture and dynamically interacting cell populations [1]—is scientifically sound given that no *in vitro* system recapitulates the full complexity of thymic stromal-lymphoid crosstalk.

The data-sharing plan states that data will be communicated through publications and presentations and made available upon acceptance of the main findings, subject to institutional conditions including a possible 60-day delay for intellectual-property review [1]. Unique mouse resources, if produced, will be shared under applicable NIH requirements. While the absence of a pre-specified repository or format introduces some uncertainty about the accessibility of the expression resource and interaction database—the most broadly useful products of Aims 1–3—this is a management consideration rather than an ethical concern.

No material risks to the environment, laboratory personnel, or the broader community are identified. The project falls squarely within the category of well-justified, appropriately monitored animal research with no ethical deficiencies that would affect the support decision.

**Rating: A (excellent). Confidence: B (high).**

## Management

**The management arrangements are appropriate in structure and supervision but carry a notable personnel risk: the successful delivery of all four aims depends heavily on a single postdoctoral fellow who must span demanding wet-lab transplantation and microdissection work as well as the full computational pipeline, within a two-year schedule that already allocates 20–28 months of activity with limited buffer.**

### Supervision and expertise

The PI, Howard Petrie, has direct published experience with both of the central methods on which the project depends: the non-myeloablative transplantation model in IL7R-deficient mice and the computational stromal-expression estimation from region-matched tissue–lymphoid microarray comparisons [1]. He will supervise the biological and informatic work, train the postdoctoral researcher, oversee protocols and resources, and review raw data before publication [1]. This level of PI involvement is appropriate for an R21 where laboratory methods are established and the primary execution risk lies at the postdoctoral level. Mohammad Fallahi, an informatic scientist with prior collaborative experience with the laboratory, will provide training and specialist assistance at 10 % effort [1]—a mentoring arrangement that is well-targeted at the computational phases but limited in capacity, as the proposal itself acknowledges [1].

### Schedule and dependencies

The three-phase schedule allocates 6–8 months to establishing growth kinetics and obtaining matched samples, the next 6–8 months primarily to analysis and prioritization, and the remaining approximately 8–12 months to refining results for publication and biological testing [1]. This sequencing correctly respects the dependencies: the expression study cannot begin until the growth trajectory is experimentally determined [1], and the Aim 4 validation depends entirely on candidates emerging from Aims 1–3 [1]. The overlap between sample collection and the start of analysis (months 7–8) and between analysis and validation (months 12+) is sensible. However, the summed activity spans 20–28 months on a 24-month grant, leaving limited buffer against any delay. The schedule does not identify which phases could be compressed if the kinetic determination takes longer than anticipated—an omission that matters because the kinetic series sits on the critical path for all subsequent work.

### Personnel concentration

The personnel plan concentrates both experimental and computational work on a single full-time postdoctoral fellow [1]. The workload encompasses two kinetic transplantation series with timed harvesting; laser microdissection of cortex and medulla from small thymic lobes; RNA preparation and processing of 60 microarrays (5 stages × 4 sample types × 3 independent pools); implementation of the published computational procedure for stromal signal estimation; receptor–ligand matching using the Receptome and GO-based searches with automated PubMed co-citation mining; multi-field prioritization scoring with clustering; construction of a searchable database; and execution of mutant mouse validation experiments. The proposal acknowledges that the data-processing phase around months 7–12 will be intensive and that Fallahi cannot absorb that workload alone [1]. A single postdoc must therefore possess or rapidly acquire competence in both microsurgery and transplantation techniques and the bioinformatic skills to execute the computational pipeline. While the PI's training role provides a mechanism for skill development, the diversity of technical demands concentrated on one person in a two-year project is a genuine management constraint.

The linked outputs—the expression time course, the annotated interaction database, initial mouse-model findings, and publications [1]—are appropriately scoped. The data-sharing commitment to make data available upon acceptance, with a possible 60-day institutional intellectual-property review delay [1], is adequate for this stage, though the absence of a pre-specified repository or sharing format represents a minor planning gap for the expression resource and interaction database, which are the most broadly reusable products of Aims 1–3. The institution's resource-sharing obligations under NIH policy are acknowledged for any unique mouse resources that may be produced [1].

**Rating: B (good). Confidence: B (high).**

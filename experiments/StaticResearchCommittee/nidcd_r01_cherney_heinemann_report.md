# Project review

## Overall assessment

This prospective, three-site observational study will define recovery trajectories of linguistic impairment, communicative participation, and health-related quality of life over 18 months following stroke-related aphasia. The project will integrate standardized behavioral assessments with detailed therapy-exposure logging, candidate genetic polymorphisms and epistasis, and advanced neuroimaging in a cohort of 300 retained participants. The most valuable expected knowledge is the empirically derived characterization of multi-domain recovery patterns and their associations with treatment content, brain connectivity, and genetic variation—information that no single existing study provides and which is needed to generate hypotheses for personalized rehabilitation.

The project is strongly supported by a well-motivated research question, a comprehensive measurement battery spanning ICF domains, and an innovative, multi-method approach to capturing therapy content that goes substantially beyond billing-code proxies. The investigative team possesses directly relevant expertise, and the internal validation procedures are conservative and appropriate. The principal limitations are that treatment is observed rather than assigned, so associations with therapy cannot be interpreted causally, and that the planned gene-gene interaction analysis is very likely underpowered to detect epistasis of realistic magnitudes, risking uninformative null findings. The demanding assessment protocol and ambitious recruitment target also present operational risks that, while prospectively acknowledged, lack detailed contingency triggers in the current plan.

Weighing the study's ambitions against its constraints, the core descriptive and associational aims are well-supported by the cohort design, measurement plan, and analytic strategy. The unresolved confounding of treatment exposure and the underpowered genetic interaction analysis are recognized limitations that circumscribe, but do not negate, the value of the knowledge to be produced. On balance, the project constitutes a worthwhile scientific investment that will generate a uniquely detailed observational dataset capable of advancing the field's understanding of aphasia recovery, provided the results are interpreted with appropriate caution regarding causality and statistical power.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

**Judgement: A (excellent). Confidence: A (very high).** The question is important and unresolved, the knowledge to be gained would meaningfully advance the field beyond current evidence, and the practical resources produced—particularly the therapy-exposure methodology and the trajectory models—would be valuable even if some of the more ambitious predictor analyses yield null or inconclusive results.

**A question the field has not yet answered.** This study addresses how linguistic impairment, communicative participation, and health-related quality of life evolve together after stroke-related aphasia, and what patient, treatment, and biological factors shape those trajectories over the critical first 18 months. Most previous aphasia studies have emphasized linguistic outcomes alone, used small or retrospective samples, or measured only a few endpoints, producing inconsistent findings about recovery predictors [1]. The existing large prospective stroke cohorts—notably the FIND study in Sweden, which recruits a minimum of 500 participants and collects multi-domain outcomes with advanced imaging and biomarker profiling—are general stroke cohorts that include only brief language screening rather than the comprehensive, aphasia-specific instruments proposed here [2]. The proposed study would therefore provide recovery trajectory estimates of substantially greater granularity and condition-specific relevance than currently available.

**Empirically grounded trajectory estimates.** By applying individual growth-curve models to five repeated assessments (admission, discharge, 6, 12, and 18 months) in a retained cohort of 300 participants, the study will select among candidate trajectory forms—linear, quadratic, cubic, and negative-exponential—for each primary outcome [1]. The central hypothesis, that linguistic improvement slows around six months while communicative participation and QoL continue improving toward 18 months, addresses a clinically consequential dissociation under the ICF framework that has been discussed but not tested in a single adequately powered longitudinal design [1].

**A methodological advance in therapy-exposure measurement.** The study moves decisively beyond billing-code proxies. The proposal's own preliminary work showed that billing records can be extracted from 71 stroke admissions but "do not adequately describe what language modality or task was treated" [1]. Instead, clinicians will log each task's start and stop time and target modality (auditory comprehension, oral expression, reading comprehension, written expression) on tablets, with 10% of sessions video-recorded for reliability verification [1]. Post-discharge, patient and caregiver checklists supplemented by weekly research assistant contact will capture outpatient and community services [1]. This multi-method approach will produce treatment-exposure descriptors—amount, intensity, modality distribution, continuity across settings—of substantially greater richness than any prior observational aphasia study, enabling the field to move beyond simple dose-response questions toward understanding what kinds of therapy, delivered at which points in recovery, are associated with which outcome domains.

**Simultaneous integration of biological predictors.** The genetic component addresses a gap explicitly identified in the literature: Harnish and colleagues' 2022 narrative review noted that genetic influences on aphasia recovery have been "largely unexplored" and called for BDNF and APOE to be incorporated into future research [3]. The neuroimaging component extends the team's pilot finding that default-mode-to-auditory connectivity related to language change while salience-to-visual connectivity related to attention change, suggesting domain-specific neural substrates of recovery [1]. Integrating these with clinical and demographic predictors within the same growth-curve framework will enable assessment of whether biological and treatment factors carry independent and interactive information about recovery trajectories [1].

**Recognized scope of inference.** The study is observational, and the treatment-exposure data—however well-measured—cannot support causal inferences about therapy effects because patients who receive more or different therapy differ systematically in ways that cannot be fully adjusted [1]. The genetic interaction analysis is very likely underpowered at the proposed sample size, as discussed under Rigor. These limitations circumscribe but do not negate the scientific value: the core descriptive contribution—empirically derived, multi-domain recovery trajectories with rich characterization of associated factors—would constitute a significant advance and would generate hypotheses for future intervention studies. The project also includes an interactive trajectory tool, which, if validated, could provide clinicians with empirically grounded expected recovery patterns [1].

## Innovation

**Judgement: A (excellent). Confidence: A (very high).** The project's originality rests not on any single novel component but on the deliberate, simultaneous integration of five predictor domains within a single prospective cohort large enough to examine their joint and interactive associations with multi-outcome recovery trajectories. This breadth of integrated measurement in aphasia research is unprecedented.

**Integration as the primary innovation.** Prior aphasia research has examined demographic and clinical predictors, or lesion characteristics, or genetic variants, or neuroimaging markers, or broad treatment amount—but typically one or two domains at a time, in smaller samples, with inconsistent instruments and assessment schedules [1]. The FIND study similarly collects multi-domain outcomes with advanced imaging and biomarkers, but it is a general stroke cohort that includes only brief language screening rather than aphasia-specific instruments, and it does not capture the granular, modality-level therapy content proposed here [2]. The proposed study's simultaneous measurement of impairment (WAB-R), participation (CPIB), and QoL (SAQoL-39) trajectories—each with empirically selected curve forms—and the testing of whether these domains recover on different timescales, operationalizes the ICF framework in a longitudinal design that the field has discussed but not executed [1].

**Methodological innovation in therapy-exposure capture.** The multi-method therapy-exposure system—clinician tablet logs capturing modality and timing, verified against video recordings, supplemented by post-discharge patient/caregiver checklists with weekly research assistant contact—will produce treatment-descriptor data of a kind not previously available in aphasia outcome research [1]. This moves from the billing-code proxies the team's own preliminary work extracted from 71 stroke admissions [1] to a description of actual service content, intensity, and continuity across inpatient and community settings [1].

**Conceptual step in genetic analysis.** The proposal examines ten SNPs across six genes (BDNF, APOE, IGF1, COMT, FGF2, VEGFA) [1] and uses multifactor dimensionality reduction to test epistasis before integrating results with clinical, demographic, treatment, and imaging variables [1]. Existing empirical genetic work in aphasia has examined BDNF alone in small samples: Dresang et al. (2022) combined BDNF genotype with neurophysiological measures in 18 chronic patients [4], Fridriksson et al. (2018) reported a BDNF × tDCS interaction in 67 treated patients [5], and Randighieri et al. (2025) found that BDNF effects on naming recovery in 77 patients were "at best, modest" [6]. The proposed study extends these efforts by testing multiple genes simultaneously and modeling their interactions, consistent with the stated framework that these variants "may interact rather than act independently" [1]. This is a conceptual step beyond the current literature, even though statistical power for detecting epistasis will be limited (see Rigor).

**Appropriate calibration.** The individual components—growth-curve modeling, resting-state connectivity, candidate genotyping, therapy logging—are each applied methods. The innovation lies in their convergence within a single adequately sized, prospectively followed aphasia cohort, which will enable questions about joint and domain-specific associations that no existing dataset can answer.

## Rigor

**Judgement: B (good). Confidence: B (high).** The prospective observational design is fundamentally appropriate for the descriptive and associational aims, and the comprehensive measurement battery spanning ICF domains is a genuine strength. However, three material limitations constrain the strength of attainable inferences: unmeasured confounding of treatment-outcome associations, very low statistical power for detecting gene-gene interactions, and an under-specified strategy for the high-dimensional imaging predictors in the growth-curve models.

**Design structure and measurement strengths.** The five-assessment schedule (admission, discharge, 6, 12, and 18 months) provides sufficient time points to estimate the candidate trajectory forms specified in Aim 1 [1]. The three primary outcomes—WAB-R Aphasia Quotient, CPIB, and SAQoL-39—span the ICF domains of impairment, participation, and lived impact [1]. Secondary measures extend coverage to attention (CPT-3), communication confidence, caregiver-rated effectiveness, apraxia of speech, and multiple Neuro-QoL domains [1]. The instruments are well-validated for the aphasia population. The internal validation plan in Aim 3—500 bootstrap resamples to assess predictor-selection stability and Monte Carlo cross-validation with performance benchmarked against the minimally clinically important difference—is conservative and clinically grounded [1].

**Critical limitation: confounding by indication.** Aim 2 tests whether earlier, more intensive, and longer treatment accompanies better outcomes [1]. The proposal acknowledges that treatment is not randomized, and analyses will be adjusted for measured patient characteristics [1]. However, patients who receive more therapy differ systematically in ways that extend beyond measured covariates—motivation, family support, insurance coverage, and clinician expectations all influence both treatment receipt and recovery trajectory. Covariate adjustment within the IGC framework reduces but cannot eliminate this confounding. The study can describe how therapy is actually delivered and how outcomes unfold, and it can identify associations between therapy characteristics and recovery, but those associations cannot be attributed to causal effects of treatment. This is inherent to the observational design and not a flaw per se, but it means the interactive trajectory tool [1] will reflect predicted outcomes from observed patterns rather than expected effects of modifying treatment. The proposal would be strengthened by sensitivity analyses that partially address confounding, such as propensity-score methods or instrumental-variable approaches.

**Gene-gene interaction power is very low.** The genetic component examines epistasis among ten SNPs in six genes using multifactor dimensionality reduction [1]. Statistical power for detecting gene-gene interactions at sample sizes in the hundreds is known to be very poor. Ma et al. (2024) demonstrated that with 1,000 participants and a relative risk of 2.0, power is approximately 0.09 when the interaction coefficient K = 1.0 and only 0.22 when K = 2.0 [7]. They concluded that "looking for epistasis from genetic association studies is an extremely tough task" and that "lack of epistasis tends to be an inevitable result caused by the statistical principles of genetic association research methods" [7]. Listopad et al. (2026) further showed that MDR and related methods produce F1 scores below 0.05 for most interaction types, and that detection performance varies substantially by interaction architecture [8]. With the proposed N of 300–400, power would be lower than the already-weak levels in those simulations. The epistasis analysis is therefore very likely to yield uninformative null results; failure to detect interactions should not be interpreted as evidence of their absence. The proposal should acknowledge this limitation and explicitly frame the genetic interaction analysis as exploratory.

**Imaging predictor dimensionality is unaddressed.** The imaging subsample of 100 participants will generate structural, functional, and diffusion measures across multiple parcellation schemes (90–500 regions) and network densities (2–50%), including global and local graph metrics [1]. The number of candidate imaging predictors substantially exceeds what can be stably modeled in a subsample of 100. The proposal does not specify how dimensionality will be reduced before imaging variables enter the IGC models alongside clinical and demographic predictors. Without a pre-specified reduction strategy, there is a substantial risk of overfitting when testing whether imaging measures add information beyond conventional variables.

**Missing data and sample size.** The proposal acknowledges that dropout "is especially important because it may challenge" the missing-at-random assumption [1] and notes the absence of a formal power calculation because suitable long-term aphasia parameters are unavailable [1]. This is an honest recognition of the evidence gap the study itself aims to fill, and 300 retained participants is a defensible size for descriptive trajectory estimation. However, if attrition systematically affects more severely impaired participants—a plausible pattern—the missing-at-random assumption underlying the IGC models will be violated, and the proposal does not describe planned sensitivity analyses for non-ignorable missingness such as pattern-mixture models.

## Feasibility

**Judgement: B (good). Confidence: B (high).** The implementation plan rests on a strong multi-site team, relevant preliminary feasibility evidence, and a coherent five-year schedule. However, the recruitment target is ambitious relative to the restrictive eligibility criteria, and the proposal lacks site-specific patient-volume data and explicit decision rules for activating its acknowledged contingency measures. The sustained clinician therapy-logging effort and the demanding assessment protocol create operational risks that the current plan addresses with only general mitigation language.

**Credible preliminary evidence.** The team has de-risked several specific procedures. Extraction of billing records from 71 stroke admissions confirmed that therapy time data can be assembled and related to length of stay (median 12 days, SD 9.2), establishing data-access feasibility [1]. A pilot of eight people with left-hemisphere stroke and aphasia demonstrated that structural and resting-state imaging plus behavioral testing could be obtained alongside a four-week intensive therapy block, with principal-component analysis revealing separable language and attention factors and connectivity associations with domain-specific improvement [1]. A survey of 56 speech-language pathologists at the participating sites supported the feasibility of identifying targeted treatment modalities [1]. Prior individual growth-curve applications by this team in spinal cord injury provide methodological precedent [1]. These elements appropriately de-risk components of the proposed work, though each pilot is small and was conducted under selected conditions that may not fully represent the broader cohort.

**Recruitment: the central feasibility concern.** The proposal targets 400 enrolled participants across three sites over approximately 30 months to retain 300 at 18 months, assuming 15% attrition by 6 months and an additional 10% by 18 months [1]. This translates to roughly 4.4 participants per site per month, which appears moderate on its face. However, the eligibility criteria are restrictive: first-ever left-hemisphere infarct confirmed by imaging, first inpatient rehabilitation admission, sufficient capacity to consent and participate in testing, and exclusion of right-hemisphere lesions and other neurological conditions [1]. The 71 stroke admissions examined in the preliminary record extraction represent total stroke volume before applying these aphasia-specific restrictions [1]. The proposal does not report the annual aphasia admission volume at each site, nor the expected proportion meeting all criteria, making independent verification of the recruitment calibration impossible.

The contingency provisions—longer accrual, added recruitment resources, or a fourth site [1]—are mentioned but lack numeric triggers, decision timelines, or pre-identified backup sites. Without these specifics, the contingency language represents prudent acknowledgment of risk rather than a credible operational risk-management plan.

**Attrition risk in an aphasia population.** The 25% overall attrition estimate merits scrutiny. Post-stroke aphasia cohorts face elevated dropout risk from recurrent medical events, mortality, fatigue, mobility limitations, and the communication barriers that make sustained research participation burdensome. The proposal's strategy of weekly research assistant calls during the first two months after discharge and monthly thereafter, combined with home visits where appropriate, represents reasonable retention effort [1]. However, if attrition systematically affects more severely impaired participants, the missing-at-random assumption will be violated, and the proposal does not describe planned sensitivity analyses for non-ignorable missingness.

**Clinician therapy logging at scale is unproven.** The therapy-exposure measurement system—requiring clinicians to log each task's start and stop time on tablets and identify the target modality during clinical sessions—is scientifically valuable but operationally demanding [1]. The reliability framework (training to 90% criterion, video-recording 10% of sessions, provision for research assistant logging when clinicians need additional practice) is well-specified [1]. However, sustaining this real-time documentation across multiple sites, numerous therapists, and a multi-year enrollment period—amidst clinical productivity pressures and inevitable staff turnover—has not been demonstrated. The SLP survey addressed feasibility of identifying treatment modalities but not the more burdensome component of real-time start/stop logging during actual clinical workflow. Pilot testing under clinical conditions would substantially strengthen confidence in this component.

**Assessment burden is substantial.** The full follow-up battery requires an estimated three to four hours per visit [1]. The mitigation strategies—frequent breaks, split visits across consecutive days, home assessments where appropriate, and supported communication techniques—are appropriate and reflect the team's clinical experience [1]. However, the cumulative burden across five assessment points is substantial for participants with aphasia, for whom fatigue and frustration are recognized risks. A formal pilot of the complete assessment protocol in a small sample would strengthen the plan.

**Schedule concentration.** The five-year schedule is logically structured: setup and training in Year 1, enrollment from Year 1 Q3 through Year 3 Q3, with follow-up assessments extending through Year 5 Q1 [1]. However, the development of growth-curve models, hypothesis testing, bootstrap stability assessment, and cross-validation are all concentrated into the final five quarters (Year 4 Q4 through Year 5 Q4) [1]. Given the complexity of the modeling plan—separate unconditional trajectory models for each outcome with form selection [1], purposeful covariate selection across five predictor groups [1], gene-gene interaction testing [1], and 500 bootstrap and cross-validation iterations [1]—any delay in data cleaning or unanticipated modeling challenges would compress the analytic and dissemination phases. The schedule allows for extension [1], but the concentration risk is identified rather than managed through intermediate analytic milestones.

## Ethics and safety

**Judgement: A (excellent). Confidence: A (very high).** The ethics and safety arrangements are proportionate, well-conceived, and appropriately tailored to the specific vulnerabilities of participants with aphasia.

**Informed consent with communication support.** The plan addresses the key ethical challenge—obtaining genuinely informed consent from individuals with communication impairments—through supported conversation techniques that ensure aphasia does not itself prevent understanding of the study's purpose, procedures, and voluntary nature [1]. The voluntary nature of participation is appropriately reinforced: the proposal states explicitly that withdrawal will not affect clinical services [1], an important assurance in a population whose rehabilitation care is ongoing. The eligibility requirement for sufficient capacity to consent, combined with aphasia-supportive communication during the consent process, appropriately balances inclusion against the ethical imperative of informed consent [1].

**Risk mitigation integrated into protocol design.** The proposal acknowledges that participation may cause fatigue, frustration, MRI discomfort, and loss of confidentiality [1]. The response to each risk is practical and operationalized: frequent breaks during assessment sessions, the option to split follow-up visits across consecutive days, home-based assessments where appropriate, and trained communication support throughout [1]. These measures are not merely listed but are integrated into the protocol—the three-to-four-hour follow-up battery explicitly allows splitting across days, and the assessment schedule includes home-visit provision [1].

**MRI safety and participant protection.** The MRI substudy incorporates appropriate safety screening for implants, claustrophobia, and pregnancy considerations, with only eligible participants undergoing research scanning [1]. The study further protects participant welfare by ensuring that those unable to undergo MRI—whether for safety reasons, tolerance issues, or motion artifact—can still contribute the core observational data and available clinical imaging [1]. This prevents the imaging component from creating pressure toward inappropriate scanning and preserves the scientific value of the broader cohort data even when the imaging subsample is reduced. One consideration is the absence of explicit discussion about management of incidental findings on research MRI scans; this is a standard consideration in neuroimaging research protocols. While the single-IRB mechanism and the Data Monitoring and Oversight Committee provide existing routes through which such a policy could be developed during site approvals, a prospective statement would complete the safety framework.

**Oversight and quality assurance.** Northwestern serves as the single IRB, streamlining ethical review across the three sites [1]. A Data Monitoring and Oversight Committee with clinical, imaging, genetic, epidemiologic, and patient/family representation provides multi-perspective oversight through annual review of readiness, safety, burden, progress, and data integrity [1]. The inclusion of patient and family perspectives is particularly appropriate given the study's focus on participation and quality-of-life outcomes. Quality assurance procedures—videotaping and rescoring 10% of assessment sessions, rechecking 10% of case-report forms, and verifying a separate 10% data-entry sample—are standard and adequate [1]. Adverse event communication pathways are specified, with events reported through site leadership to the co-PIs and institutional oversight according to a stated reporting schedule [1].

**Data management and confidentiality.** Research records are maintained separately from medical records, identified by codes rather than names, with password-protected electronic files and secured physical records under restricted access [1]. The genetic data handling is integrated within the broader biomarker analysis framework rather than treated as standalone determinants, which is both scientifically and ethically sound given the study's explicit position that genetic variants may interact with rather than independently determine recovery trajectories [1]. The data-sharing plan—de-identified REDCap dataset available on request under a data-use agreement prohibiting reidentification and unauthorized redistribution, defining reporting responsibilities, and requiring destruction after analyses—is appropriate and specifies the inclusion of behavioral, patient-reported, treatment-exposure, imaging, and candidate-marker information [1].

## Management

**Judgement: B (good). Confidence: B (high).** The investigative team combines directly relevant and complementary expertise appropriate to the study's multi-domain aims, and the coordination infrastructure across the three sites is well-conceived. However, the proposal provides insufficient detail on staffing levels to independently assess whether personnel resources match the substantial data-collection workload, and the concentration of complex analytic activities into the final project quarters creates schedule risk that is acknowledged but not operationally resolved.

**Team expertise is directly relevant.** The co-PIs, Allen W. Heinemann and Leora R. Cherney, bring complementary strengths that map onto the study's core components [1]. Heinemann's established record in rehabilitation outcomes research and individual growth-curve modeling—demonstrated in prior IGC applications in spinal cord injury that described how demographic, injury, and rehabilitation characteristics relate to individual recovery plateaus and rates—provides the methodological foundation for the trajectory analyses [1]. Cherney's expertise in aphasia treatment and assessment, reflected in her work on instruments such as the Communication Confidence Rating Scale for Aphasia [1], anchors the clinical measurement and therapy-exposure components. The proposal further documents team expertise in genomics and neuroimaging, though the specific personnel responsible for these components are not individually named in the supplied passages [1].

**Appropriate coordination infrastructure.** The three inpatient rehabilitation facilities—Shirley Ryan AbilityLab, Alexian Brothers Rehabilitation Hospital, and Mary Free Bed Rehabilitation Hospital—are established clinical sites with the patient populations needed for this work [1]. Shared infrastructure is appropriate: REDCap provides centralized case-report forms and data checks, and the single-IRB arrangement through Northwestern simplifies regulatory coordination across sites [1]. Standardized training, NIHSS certification, and continuous speech-language pathology supervision are specified to maintain assessment consistency across the three locations [1].

**Staffing levels are unspecified.** The proposal describes dedicated research assistants who will screen admissions daily, coordinate assessments, and maintain post-discharge contact [1], but it does not specify the number of research assistants per site. The workload is substantial: screening admissions against restrictive eligibility criteria [1]; conducting three-to-four-hour assessment batteries at five time points [1]; performing weekly phone calls during the first two months after discharge and monthly thereafter [1]; extracting therapy records and coding progress notes; and maintaining data quality across 400 enrolled participants. The proposal also does not detail MRI technologist time for the imaging substudy of 100 participants requiring a specialized protocol [1], nor does it specify data management personnel beyond the REDCap platform itself. These are typical omissions in a research grant narrative and do not themselves demonstrate incapacity, but they limit the evidence available for evaluating resource adequacy.

**Schedule concentration poses a delivery risk.** Data cleaning is interleaved throughout the five-year timeline, but the development of Aim 1 and Aim 2 growth-curve models, hypothesis testing, bootstrap stability assessment, and cross-validation are all concentrated into the final five quarters (Year 4 Q4 through Year 5 Q4) [1]. This is aggressive given the complexity of the modeling plan: separate unconditional trajectory models for each primary and secondary outcome with selection among linear, quadratic, cubic, and negative-exponential forms [1]; purposeful covariate selection across demographic, clinical, therapy, imaging, and genetic predictor groups with model comparison [1]; gene-gene interaction testing using multifactor dimensionality reduction [1]; and 500 bootstrap and cross-validation iterations [1]. Any delay in data cleaning from the earlier phases—or unanticipated modeling challenges such as convergence failures—would compress the analytic and dissemination periods. The schedule allows for extension [1], but the absence of a detailed analytic timeline with intermediate milestones means the concentration risk is identified rather than managed.

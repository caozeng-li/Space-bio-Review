# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project investigates how humans combine visual and inertial information to perceive heading direction, determine whether the two sensory signals share a common cause, and adapt when the signals remain systematically inconsistent. Healthy participants and people with unilateral vestibular loss will perform psychophysical tasks on a motion platform where visual and inertial headings are presented with controlled discrepancies. The work will measure explicit unity judgments alongside separate visual and inertial heading estimates to test whether cross-modal influence persists at larger discrepancies than explicit common-cause perception. It will also characterize two perceptual adaptation methods—sustained visual-inertial offsets and rotating virtual environments—and longitudinally track heading errors after acute vestibular damage to test whether these adaptation methods produce durable correction in chronic vestibular hypofunction.

The proposal is strongly supported by a published study from the same laboratory. Rodriguez and Crane (2020) demonstrated the key dissociation between explicit common-cause judgments and implicit cross-modal influence in eight participants: common-cause judgments dropped below 15% at offsets ≥90° while inertial heading remained biased 11–20° toward the visual stimulus [1]. Convergent evidence from the computational study of Acerbi et al. (2018) showed that only joint analysis of explicit and implicit tasks could rule out forced fusion in heading perception [2]. The proposal extends these findings by systematically manipulating timing, motion profile, stimulus duration, and visual field size to modulate the evidence for common causation, and by applying the same measurements to participants with unilateral vestibular loss, where reduced inertial reliability provides a convergent test of causal inference principles. Preliminary data in four participants confirm feasibility of the factorial causal-inference design, and both adaptation methods have produced measurable aftereffects in individual pilots [3]. The laboratory possesses capable equipment including a six-degree-of-freedom hexapod, VR displays, and established psychophysical analysis routines [3], and the safety framework is proportionate and well-specified.

The main material limitations are that the insights will remain primarily descriptive without a specified computational modeling framework, the generalization testing in Aim II is underspecified, and delivery of all three aims at the stated scope depends on a single technician managing approximately 150 participants across parallel work streams with no documented contingency for staff absence, with the acute-lesion longitudinal study depending on clinic-specific referral rates and patient willingness that are assumed rather than documented.

On balance, the proposal pursues a valuable set of questions with methodologically capable experiments and an established technical platform, and its central innovation—separating explicit causal inference from implicit cross-modal influence—is well-supported by both published and preliminary data. The principal limitations are a descriptive rather than model-driven framework that constrains theoretical insight, and a lean staffing model with ambitious parallel patient recruitment that introduces material feasibility risk, particularly for the longitudinal acute-lesion aim. These limitations do not undermine the core mechanistic contributions, which can be delivered with the stated methods and resources, but they reduce the certainty with which secondary subgroup analyses and the full scope of all aims can be achieved as proposed.

## Scientific value

The project addresses an important and understudied set of questions at the intersection of multisensory perception, causal inference, and vestibular rehabilitation. It will produce the first systematic characterization of visual-inertial causal inference for heading across the full horizontal plane, establish two candidate perceptual adaptation methods with translational potential, and provide the first longitudinal description of heading bias evolution after acute unilateral vestibular loss. These contributions would advance both basic multisensory neuroscience and clinical vestibular science.

The scientific value of the work rests on three interrelated gaps that the proposal identifies and is positioned to fill. First, vestibular evaluation has historically emphasized reflexes—the vestibulo-ocular reflex and vestibular-evoked myopotentials—while the perceptual symptoms that patients actually report (dizziness, vertigo, motion sickness) remain poorly understood at a mechanistic level [3]. The proposal's focus on perception rather than reflexes addresses a clinically meaningful mismatch between laboratory measures and patient experience. Second, prior multisensory heading studies have examined small discrepancies near straight ahead, asked for a single combined estimate, or measured one modality without independently measuring the other [3]. Such designs cannot distinguish a participant's explicit judgment that two stimuli share a common cause from the continued influence of one sensory modality on the other. The proposal's measurement strategy—obtaining separate visual and inertial heading estimates alongside explicit unity judgments—directly addresses this inferential gap. Third, although perceptual asymmetries may persist after reflex recovery following vestibular damage, the natural history of heading errors and their amenability to perceptual intervention have not been systematically characterized [3]. By longitudinally tracking heading bias from the acute phase through one year and testing two adaptation methods in chronic hypofunction, the project would generate knowledge with direct relevance to the design of future perceptual rehabilitation.

The attainable knowledge is substantial. Aim I would deliver the first parametric description of how timing, motion profile, stimulus duration, and visual field size modulate the relationship between explicit common-cause perception and implicit cross-modal heading influence across the full 360° horizontal plane [3]. The published work from this laboratory has already demonstrated the core dissociation, and the proposal extends these findings by systematically manipulating the stimulus factors that should alter the evidence for common causation, and by applying the same measurements to participants with chronic unilateral vestibular loss, where reduced inertial reliability provides a convergent test of causal inference principles that no prior study has attempted [3]. Aim II would establish the scope and generalization of two perceptual adaptation methods—sustained visual-inertial offsets and rotating virtual environments—and determine whether adaptation alters causal inference itself [3]. Aim III would provide the first longitudinal characterization of heading bias after acute vestibular loss, distinguishing otolith-mediated from canal-mediated contributions, and test whether repeated perceptual adaptation can produce durable correction in chronic unilateral hypofunction [3].

The contribution is primarily empirical and descriptive rather than driven by a specified computational modeling framework. The proposal mentions integrating results into a model in Year 5 [3], but no concrete modeling approach, model comparison strategy, or computational framework is described. The project is more likely to generate data that constrain future models than to produce a new computational account of causal inference in heading perception. This does not diminish the value of the empirical contribution: characterizing understudied phenomena with rigorous psychophysical methods is a legitimate and valuable scientific aim, and the dataset this project would produce would be uniquely suited to inform subsequent computational work. The explicit recognition that a subsequent randomized clinical trial would be needed to test functional benefit of the adaptation intervention [3] appropriately positions the mechanistic contribution of Aim IIIB without overclaiming its immediate therapeutic significance.

**Rating: B (good). Confidence: B (high).** The question is well-motivated, the attainable empirical knowledge would advance both basic and clinical vestibular science, and the proposal is appropriately positioned as mechanistic groundwork. The absence of a specified computational modeling framework constrains theoretical insight but does not fundamentally weaken the value of the empirical characterization.

## Innovation

The proposal makes a genuine advance by systematically combining explicit common-cause judgments with separate visual and inertial heading estimates across the full 360° horizontal plane—a measurement strategy that no prior study has implemented at this scope. The central conceptual distinction—that explicit unity judgments and implicit cross-modal influence on heading perception may dissociate—is not itself new: the Crane laboratory demonstrated it in Rodriguez and Crane (2020), and the independent computational work of Acerbi et al. (2018) established that only joint analysis of explicit and implicit tasks ruled out forced fusion [2]. The proposal's innovation lies in what it builds on this established finding.

**Parametric manipulation of common-cause evidence.** Experiment IB represents a systematic extension beyond any existing heading-perception study. Prior work examined headings restricted to ±25° near straight ahead and did not independently manipulate timing, motion duration, or visual field size as factors altering the evidence for a common cause [2] [4]. The de Winkel et al. (2017) study demonstrated that longer-duration motions increase reliance on visual cues and decrease tolerance for discrepancies, but did so without explicit common-cause reports and with headings limited to the forward hemifield [4]. The present proposal tests temporal offsets from 50 to 200 ms, varying durations, and three visual field sizes (98°, 45°, 20°) while collecting both explicit unity judgments and separate heading estimates across 360° [3]. This combination of parametric stimulus control with the dual-measurement strategy would produce a far richer dataset for testing causal inference principles than currently exists.

**Lesion-based perturbation of cue reliability.** Experiment IC applies these same measurements to participants with chronic unilateral vestibular loss. Using lesion-based perturbation of cue reliability to test causal inference predictions is a convergent approach that has no precedent in the heading-perception literature. The predicted increase in visual influence and broadening of common-cause judgments with reduced vestibular reliability [3] provides an independent test of the reliability-based integration framework and bridges basic psychophysics with clinically relevant populations.

**Adaptation studies with novel extensions.** The offset-adaptation paradigm builds on the classic work of Zaidel et al. (2011), who demonstrated fixed-ratio visual-vestibular adaptation with small (±10°) offsets near straight ahead, measured only left/right discrimination, and did not assess causal inference [5]. The present proposal uses a larger illustrative offset (20°), measures visual and inertial heading separately, tests generalization across the horizontal plane, and reassesses common-cause judgments after adaptation [3]. The rotating virtual environment paradigm is timely: Behery et al. (2025) recently demonstrated that slowly rotating visual spaces (1–2°/s) induce persistent heading biases in humans that scale with rotation speed and are modulated by head movement [6]. The proposal's faster rotation (40°/s) with reduced landmark coherence and active head movement [3] represents a substantially different paradigm whose relationship to the Behery et al. phenomenon is itself an interesting scientific question.

The proposal's contribution remains primarily empirical rather than theoretical. No specific computational modeling framework is described, and the experimental design emphasizes parametric characterization over formal model comparison. This limits the theoretical depth of the insights obtainable relative to what the rich prior modeling literature might support, but it does not diminish the originality of the empirical agenda.

**Rating: B (good). Confidence: B (high).** The combination of dual-measurement psychophysics, parametric manipulation of common-cause evidence, and lesion-based perturbation of cue reliability constitutes a genuine methodological and empirical advance. The adaptation studies extend prior recalibration work by testing generalization and reassessing causal inference.

## Rigor

The experimental designs are conceptually well-matched to the scientific questions, and the core measurement strategy represents a genuine methodological advance. However, several design features limit the strength of inference for secondary questions, and the adaptation and lesion studies carry feasibility risks that constrain their evidential contribution.

**Core measurement strategy.** The central measurement innovation—obtaining separate visual and inertial heading estimates in different trial blocks while collecting explicit unity judgments alongside—directly addresses the key inferential challenge that neither a single combined estimate nor a unity judgment alone can reveal whether cross-modal influence occurs without explicit common-cause attribution. The proposal's preliminary data in four participants demonstrate that at 90° offsets, agreement judgments drop below 4% while inertial estimates remain biased toward the visual heading [3], a dissociation consistent with the published findings in Rodriguez and Crane (2020). The factorial design of Experiment IA—12 visual × 12 inertial headings across 360° with three repetitions per report-modality block, yielding six unity judgments and three directional estimates per combination per participant—provides adequate precision for individual psychometric fitting and full characterization of both unity and bias across the horizontal plane [3].

Two concerns qualify this strength. First, the primary outcome comparison—the discrepancy range producing unity versus the range producing attraction—relies on a descriptive comparison of two response domains (binary same/different judgments and continuous heading estimates) that have fundamentally different measurement properties. The proposal does not specify a formal computational model that jointly accounts for both responses, analogous to the Bayesian model-comparison framework used by Acerbi et al. (2018), who demonstrated that only joint fits of explicit and implicit task data could rule out forced fusion [2]. Without such a model, the central claim that cross-modal influence spans a larger discrepancy range than explicit common-cause perception [3] remains a qualitative empirical description rather than a model-tested inference. The published data already support this qualitative description [1], so the absence of a modeling framework does not threaten the core empirical contribution, but it limits the theoretical precision with which the dissociation can be characterized.

Second, visual and inertial estimates are obtained in separate trial blocks because reporting both in the same block proved confusing [3]. While pragmatically justified, this means the within-trial relationship between a participant's unity judgment and their heading estimate is never directly observed; the dissociation evidence rests on aggregated responses across blocks. This is a practical trade-off acknowledged by the proposal rather than a design flaw, but it weakens the directness of the evidence that a participant can simultaneously judge stimuli as independent while their heading estimate remains attracted to the conflicting modality.

**Adaptation design.** The offset paradigm (IIA) is appropriately grounded in the multisensory recalibration literature. The present proposal extends the work of Zaidel et al. (2011) by using a larger illustrative offset (20°), measuring both modalities separately, and reassessing common-cause judgments after adaptation [3]. The critical limitation concerns the generalization testing. The proposal states it will test "whether adaptation generalizes beyond the heading range used in training" [3] and notes that "the design explicitly tests the alternative that learning remains confined to trained headings" [3]. However, the primary forced-choice test is near straight ahead, and continuous estimates "in selected experiments" provide the generalization measurement. The proposal does not specify which heading directions will be tested for generalization, how many directions will be sampled, or what criterion constitutes evidence for versus against generalization. A design that tests only one or two untrained heading directions cannot adequately distinguish narrow generalization from broad recalibration.

For Experiment IIB, the rotating virtual environment paradigm introduces an additional inferential concern: the adaptation stimulus (a rotating star field at 40°/s with reduced landmark coherence [3]) differs in motion type from the test stimuli (translational headings), so the measured aftereffect reflects cross-modal and cross-motion-type transfer. The proposal's concurrent measurement of rotational thresholds partially addresses this but does not fully isolate whether observed heading shifts reflect genuine inertial recalibration or a change in how visual rotation is interpreted during subsequent heading tests.

**Longitudinal lesion study.** Experiment IIIA has a mechanistically sound design: weekly testing in the first month, monthly for six months, and at nine months and one year, with translational heading, yaw rotation, and sway measurements combined with clinical tests (calorics, VEMPs, vHIT, DHI) to distinguish otolith-mediated sway asymmetry from canal-mediated rotational contributions to heading bias [3]. The stated sample of 16 with approximately 50% anticipated attrition implies enrolling approximately 32 acute patients to retain roughly 16 completers, and the etiologies are appropriately analyzed separately [3]. At this retention level, the longitudinal trajectory can be adequately described, but the planned comparisons among three etiology categories (surgery, neuritis, gentamicin) would have very low power with only 5–6 participants per subgroup.

**Psychophysical methods and analysis.** The forced-choice psychophysical methods, cumulative Gaussian fitting with Monte Carlo maximum-likelihood procedures, and resampling-based confidence intervals are established and appropriate for estimating bias and precision [3] [7]. The sample-size justifications use 80% power and a 5% type-I error rate with expected effect sizes drawn from preliminary data, which is appropriate for planning. However, the pilot estimates—from four participants for Aim I and individual participants for Aim II—carry substantial uncertainty; the actual standard deviation in the full sample, particularly in patient groups, may exceed the pilot estimates [3]. The decision to use p ≤ .01 as a conservative criterion reflects awareness of multiple-comparison concerns, though the proposal does not specify formal correction methods for the many planned comparisons across heading directions, conditions, and experiments [3].

**Rating: B (good). Confidence: B (high).** The factorial causal-inference design with separate modality estimates and unity judgments appropriately addresses the central dissociation question, and the psychophysical methods are well-established. The principal limitations are the absence of a joint computational model for unity and heading estimates, the underspecified generalization testing in Aim II, and the small, heterogeneous lesion samples that constrain Aim III's mechanistic comparisons. These reduce the certainty with which some secondary questions can be answered but do not undermine the core measurements, which can address the primary aims as stated.

## Feasibility

The laboratory equipment and core psychophysical procedures are well-established and demonstrated in pilot work, supporting the technical feasibility of the experimental paradigms. The principal feasibility risks concern personnel capacity and patient recruitment, particularly for the longitudinal acute-lesion study, where the proposal's assumptions about referral rates and participant retention are not substantiated by clinic-specific data.

**Technical pathway.** The technical pathway is grounded in demonstrated capability. The laboratory possesses a six-degree-of-freedom hexapod motion platform synchronized with a 98° visual display, VR headsets with head tracking and binocular disparity, and a custom multi-axis sled/rotator that extends translation and yaw range beyond the platform limits [3]. Motion tracking, video eye tracking, accelerometers, and rate gyros characterize stimulus delivery. The full 12×12 visual-inertial heading paradigm with unity judgments and separate heading estimates has been completed in four preliminary participants [3], and both adaptation methods have produced measurable aftereffects in individual pilots [3]. These are substantial technical assets, and the platform has already yielded publishable results. The multiplicity of available hardware (second platform, VR headset, sled/rotator) provides alternative stimulus-delivery routes and mitigates the risk that a single equipment failure would halt the project.

**Personnel capacity.** The feasibility concern centers on the staffing model: one full-time technician supporting recruitment and testing across three parallel work streams [3]. Across five years, approximately 120 healthy controls and 30 participants with vestibulopathy are anticipated [3], though the per-experiment targets suggest higher totals when acute lesion recruitment (IIIA, requiring approximately 32 approached to retain roughly 16 after 50% attrition) and chronic adaptation (IIIB, 20 or more) are tallied separately. The experimental sessions are demanding: Experiment IA alone requires 144 stimulus combinations per block, with each block repeated three times per report modality [3], and the adaptation experiments add pre- and post-adaptation testing sessions. The Year 1 schedule initiates four streams simultaneously—causal-inference methods in normals (IA), heading-offset adaptation (IIA), acute unilateral-loss follow-up (IIIA), and chronic-lesion adaptation (IIIB) [3]—yet the proposal does not specify the division of labor between the PI and the technician for session conduct, nor does it provide any contingency for the technician's absence due to illness, leave, or turnover. A single point of staffing dependency for approximately 150 participant studies across five years is a material vulnerability.

**Patient recruitment.** Patient recruitment for the acute-lesion longitudinal study (IIIA) presents an additional and consequential risk. The design requires identifying patients shortly after acute unilateral vestibular loss from surgery, neuritis, or gentamicin treatment [3], with the first weekly visit occurring within the first post-lesion month. The proposal states that existing resources include patients from otolaryngology clinics [3] but does not provide clinic-specific referral volume data, estimates of the proportion meeting eligibility criteria, or the typical consent rate during an acute illness episode. Vestibular neuritis and acute Meniere's treatment are episodic clinical events; identifying patients within the narrow recruitment window requires close clinical coordination that is described aspirationally but not operationalized. The anticipated 50% attrition [3] is realistic and well-acknowledged, but the combination of uncertain referral flow, demanding visit schedules for patients who may travel long distances, and the subdivision of an already small sample into three etiology categories means the longitudinal aim is the component most vulnerable to under-recruitment.

**Schedule resilience.** The five-year schedule distributes activities in a logical sequence, with Year 1 establishing methods, Years 2–4 extending stimulus parameters and continuing patient studies, and Year 5 integrating results and completing dissemination [3]. However, the schedule does not contain explicit decision gates—if acute lesion recruitment falls substantially behind in Year 2, there is no stated plan for whether to extend recruitment at the expense of other aims or reduce the longitudinal scope. The core psychophysical experiments with healthy participants (IA, IB, IIA, IIB) are clearly within the laboratory's demonstrated capability; the chronic lesion experiments (IC, IIIB) draw on an established patient database and are achievable at a major academic otolaryngology center over five years; the acute-lesion longitudinal follow-up is the component whose feasibility depends most heavily on assumptions rather than documented capacity.

**Rating: C (moderate). Confidence: C (moderate).** The technical methods are demonstrated, and the core healthy-participant experiments are clearly feasible. The combination of a lean single-technician staffing model with no documented contingency, and acute-lesion recruitment assumptions that are not substantiated by clinic-specific data, introduces material risk to the delivery of all three aims at the stated scope, with the longitudinal acute-lesion study most vulnerable.

## Ethics and safety

The safety arrangements are proportionate, well-specified, and aligned with the noninvasive nature of the research. The proposal identifies the principal foreseeable risks, implements appropriate monitoring and stopping rules for the higher-risk adaptation experiments, and maintains an ethically appropriate distinction between the current mechanistic investigation and future clinical trials.

**Risk profile.** The research is entirely noninvasive. Participants are seated on a motion platform and view visual displays while reporting perceived heading direction; no pharmacological interventions, invasive procedures, or ionizing radiation are involved. The proposal correctly identifies the principal foreseeable risks as motion sickness, temporary worsening of dizziness, and loss of privacy [3]. These are intrinsic to vestibular research and are managed through standard institutional mechanisms: informed consent is obtained before participation, participants may discontinue at any time, and adolescents, if enrolled, require parental consent with an age-appropriate assent process [3].

**Safeguards for higher-risk experiments.** The adaptation experiments, particularly those involving rotating virtual environments (IIB) and repeated recalibration in patients with vestibular hypofunction (IIIB), carry a higher risk of provoking motion sickness or exacerbating dizziness than the purely psychophysical experiments in Aim I. The proposal addresses this with several specific safeguards. The Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI) is administered during adaptation visits to monitor for worsening symptoms [3]. For Experiment IIIB, the proposal explicitly states that "DHI monitoring guards against worsening symptoms, and reducing exposure duration or rotational speed is a proposed response to discomfort" [3]. The adaptation protocol always targets the direction that reduces the participant's baseline heading bias, and further adaptation stops when bias disappears or reverses [3]. These are appropriate design choices: the adaptation procedure is tuned to the individual's perceptual deficit rather than applying a fixed stimulus that might inadvertently worsen asymmetry, and the stopping rule prevents over-adaptation. The preliminary data from five chronic participants who underwent two or three rotational-adaptation sessions showed a mean DHI change of −0.8 (range −4 to 2) [3], providing direct evidence that the procedures did not systematically worsen symptoms in this small pilot sample, although the clinical significance of these DHI changes is negligible, as the proposal appropriately treats them as safety monitoring data rather than efficacy evidence.

The rotating virtual environment paradigm in Experiment IIB uses a stimulus (40°/s rotation with reduced landmark coherence) that the proposal notes became "subjectively unobtrusive after one or two minutes" in preliminary testing [3]. The plan to measure motion sickness, movement amount, and attention, with minimum movement requirements or adjusted parameters to standardize exposure, reflects awareness that participant state affects both safety and data quality.

**Governance and ethical framing.** Adverse events will be documented with severity and relationship to participation and reviewed through institutional oversight [3]. Identifying information is maintained separately from research measurements, with access limited to designated study personnel [3]. Trial registration is planned before data collection. These arrangements meet expected standards for noninvasive human psychophysical research with patient populations.

A notable ethical strength is the proposal's explicit distinction between its current scope and a future clinical trial. The statement that "a subsequent randomized clinical trial would be needed to test functional benefit" [3] appropriately frames the adaptation studies in Aim IIIB as mechanistic investigations of durable correction and tolerability rather than efficacy trials. This prevents overinterpretation of the results and correctly positions the work as generating the evidence base that would justify—or argue against—a future trial. The research will not prevent participants from receiving other clinical care [3], and the recruitment from otolaryngology clinics positions the study within existing clinical pathways rather than competing with them.

**Rating: A (excellent). Confidence: B (high).** The safety framework is proportionate and comprehensive for the noninvasive nature of the research. The proposal identifies risks, implements monitoring and stopping rules tailored to the higher-risk adaptation experiments, and maintains a clear ethical boundary between mechanistic investigation and therapeutic claims. Confidence is high rather than very high only because the proposal does not provide institutional review board approval documentation or a data safety monitoring plan, though the described procedures are consistent with standard institutional oversight for this category of research.

## Management

The management arrangements are appropriate in expertise and logical in sequencing, but the staffing model is lean for the stated scope of parallel activity, and the schedule lacks explicit contingency provisions for the most vulnerable recruitment components.

**Expertise and scientific oversight.** The principal investigator has an established track record in vestibular psychophysics and has developed the laboratory's experimental platform, which has produced published work directly relevant to the proposed experiments [3] [1] [7]. The scientific oversight is appropriately placed with a researcher who has demonstrated the core paradigms in preliminary and published studies.

**Schedule logic and sequencing.** The five-year schedule distributes activities in a logical sequence: Year 1 establishes causal-inference methods in healthy participants while simultaneously beginning all patient streams; Years 2–3 extend stimulus parameters and continue lesion studies; Year 4 emphasizes duration and rotational parameters and completes key lesion experiments; Year 5 integrates results and completes dissemination [3]. The sequential refinement of stimulus manipulations (timing in Year 2, duration in Year 4) follows coherently from the core measurements established in Year 1, and the concurrent initiation of mechanistic and patient studies makes efficient use of the five-year window given the slow pace of clinical recruitment.

**Personnel capacity.** The critical management risk concerns personnel. The staffing model consists of the PI and one full-time technician, who will support recruitment and testing [3]. Across five years, approximately 120 healthy controls and 30 participants with vestibulopathy are anticipated [3], though the per-experiment targets for acute lesion recruitment (requiring approximately 32 approached to retain roughly 16 after 50% attrition) and chronic adaptation (20 or more) suggest somewhat higher patient totals when each aim is tallied separately. The experimental sessions are demanding: Experiment IA alone requires 144 stimulus combinations per block, repeated three times per report modality per participant [3], and the adaptation experiments add pre- and post-adaptation testing sessions. The Year 1 schedule initiates four parallel streams simultaneously [3], yet the proposal does not specify the division of labor between the PI and the technician for session conduct, nor does it describe any contingency for the technician's absence due to illness, leave, or turnover. A single point of staffing dependency for approximately 150 participant studies across five years creates a bottleneck with no documented redundancy.

**Decision gates and contingency planning.** The schedule itself lacks explicit decision gates. If acute lesion recruitment (IIIA) falls substantially behind projections in Year 2, there is no stated plan for whether to extend recruitment into later years at the expense of other aims or to reduce the scope of the longitudinal analysis to focus on the trajectory of a smaller sample. Similarly, if the technician departs, there is no cross-training or backup arrangement described. These are not fatal omissions—research programs routinely adapt to circumstances—but they represent vulnerabilities that are not addressed in the proposal as presented.

**Data management and dissemination.** The data management and dissemination arrangements are appropriate. Identifying information is maintained separately from research measurements with access limited to designated personnel [3]. Dissemination through scientific publications and presentations is planned, and trial registration is scheduled before data collection. These meet expected standards for human psychophysical research.

**Rating: C (moderate). Confidence: C (moderate).** The PI's expertise is appropriate, the schedule is logically sequenced, and the data management plan is sound. However, the lean staffing model with a single technician and no contingency for absence, combined with a schedule that initiates all four experimental streams simultaneously without decision gates, introduces material risk to delivery of the full scope. The confidence rating reflects the limited information available in the proposal about clinic-specific referral volumes and the unspecified division of PI-technician responsibilities, which are critical to assessing whether the management structure can support the stated aims rather than evidence of a specific defect.

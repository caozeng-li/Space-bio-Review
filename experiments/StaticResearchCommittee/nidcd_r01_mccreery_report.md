# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project investigates why school-age children who use hearing aids continue to experience difficulty understanding speech in noise, even when amplification improves access to the speech signal. The central hypothesis holds that selective attention, working memory, and language contribute to listening performance, with hearing loss adding both immediate and cumulative effects of reduced audibility. Through cross-sectional experiments and repeated assessments over 24 months, the study will examine spectral resolution, individual perceptual weighting of speech frequencies, and susceptibility to competing talkers in children with mild-to-severe hearing loss, children with normal hearing, and a mature normal-hearing adult comparison group. Its most valuable expected knowledge is a developmental account of the sensory, cognitive, and linguistic bases of individual differences in speech-in-noise performance. Such an account would refine clinical models of speech intelligibility for children and could inform more individualized approaches to amplification and rehabilitation.

The proposal is supported by a well-articulated theoretical framework, extensive preliminary data from the investigative team, and methodologically refined tasks that improve on prior work—most notably the narrowband spectral-ripple procedure with level roving and interstimulus interval manipulation, the efficient band-importance estimation method, and the hearing-aid simulator for controlled study of perceptual weighting in children with hearing loss. The longitudinal design across three aims within a single sample is a genuine advance over the existing cross-sectional evidence base.

The principal limitations are the persistence of cognitive confounding in the spectral-ripple measure, documented in the team's own published work, and power constraints. The sample sizes are adequate for detecting moderate bivariate effects but may be insufficient for the planned mediation and path models, particularly after accounting for collinearity among cognitive predictors. The attention measure shows ceiling effects in children with hearing loss, limiting its ability to detect the predicted relationships in Aim 3. Recruitment and longitudinal retention also present practical risks that are only partially addressed. These limitations do not prevent the project from producing a rich descriptive dataset on how spectral discrimination, perceptual weighting, and masked speech recognition relate to cognitive and linguistic skills cross-sectionally and over time, but they constrain the mechanistic specificity of the claims the design can support.

The judgement turns on a balance between methodological care and the limits of the core measures. The proposal's sophisticated design—interstimulus interval variation, ear-asymmetric controls, masker-type comparisons, and a longitudinal framework—represents a genuine advance in addressing the cognitive confound problem, and the willingness to specify alternative outcomes reflects scientific maturity. However, the central sensory assay in Aim 1 cannot be assumed to provide a pure index of peripheral spectral resolution, as the team's own published work makes clear. The mediation analyses risk estimating relationships among measures that share cognitive variance, and the sample sizes, while adequate for bivariate effects, may be insufficient for the planned indirect-effect tests. The project's main contribution lies in a rich descriptive characterization of how spectral discrimination, perceptual weighting, and masked speech recognition interrelate with cognitive and linguistic skills in children with hearing loss, both cross-sectionally and longitudinally. The design is well-matched to this worthwhile increment, even though some causal claims the framework implies will remain provisional. On balance, a supportable scope is identifiable, and the project would advance understanding of individual differences in pediatric hearing outcomes in a manner that can inform future intervention work.

The recommended support scope centres on characterizing individual differences in the relationships among spectral discrimination, perceptual weighting, masked speech recognition, and cognitive-linguistic skills in children with hearing loss, both cross-sectionally and longitudinally. The precondition is that the longitudinal mediation analyses be reframed as exploratory descriptions of developmental covariation rather than formal tests of indirect effects, and that the spectral-ripple measure be interpreted as a composite sensory-cognitive index rather than a pure proxy for peripheral spectral resolution.

**Overall conclusion: Conditional support**

## Scientific value

**This project addresses a question of genuine developmental and clinical importance, and the attainable knowledge would represent a valuable, if incremental, advance.** The persistent difficulty that school-age children with hearing aids experience in understanding speech in noise, despite improved audibility, is well documented in the team's pilot dataset, where 130 children with hearing loss show substantially poorer sentence recognition than 80 children with normal hearing in speech-shaped noise at +6 dB signal-to-noise ratio [1]. Because everyday learning and social interaction occur in noisy environments, explaining the sources of this deficit beyond audibility carries direct practical implications for individualized amplification and rehabilitation strategies.

The proposal's theoretical framework is coherent and productive. Three processing routes—attention to spectral features, inhibition of less useful frequency information, and segregation of target from masker speech—are posited, with working memory affecting all tasks requiring storage and processing and language mattering particularly when target or masker content is speech [1]. The framework further distinguishes immediate effects of audibility on signal access from cumulative effects operating through language development [1]. This structure generates testable, well-motivated hypotheses for each aim and organizes a large set of measurements into a tractable explanatory scheme. The goal of a developmental account that can support future clinical models of speech intelligibility for children is clearly stated and proportionate to a five-year R01 programme.

**The scientific contribution must be assessed relative to what is already established.** The investigative team has published extensively on these relationships. McCreery et al. (2020) demonstrated that working memory and selective attention predicted sentence recognition specifically in two-talker speech masking, not in speech-shaped noise [2]. Lalonde et al. (2023) subsequently showed that vocabulary and aided audibility, rather than attention, predicted two-talker performance in children with hearing loss, with the attention measure showing ceiling effects in that group [3]. Bosen et al. (2025) documented developmental shifts in frequency-band importance in children with normal hearing and explicitly called for parallel work in children with hearing loss [4]. Kirby et al. (2019) established that nonverbal intelligence and visual working memory were associated with spectro-temporal ripple discrimination in children with hearing loss, while noting that these relationships may reflect the cognitive demands of the psychophysical task [5]. The central hypothesis has thus already received considerable cross-sectional support. Finding further evidence for these relationships would consolidate rather than challenge existing knowledge.

What the proposal adds over this prior work is methodologically refined tasks that better isolate sensory from cognitive contributions, comparison of three processing routes within a single sample, and a 24-month longitudinal design. The spectral-ripple task with narrowband carriers and level roving improves on the earlier spectrotemporal measure [1]; the interstimulus interval manipulation in Experiment 1b offers a novel method for dissociating sensory resolution from memory demands [1]; and individual band-importance measurement in children with hearing loss using a hearing-aid simulator addresses Bosen et al.'s explicit call [1] [4]. The longitudinal tracking across all three aims within one sample is the proposal's most distinctive contribution, as the existing evidence is almost entirely cross-sectional.

**The practical value lies in the project's potential to inform clinical practice.** The Speech Intelligibility Index currently uses average adult importance functions that do not account for individual differences or developmental change [1]. Extending developmental band-importance measurement to children with hearing loss could support more individualized hearing-aid fitting strategies that account for a child's cognitive and linguistic profile, not only their audiogram. The project's contribution to public health thus operates through refining the evidence base for pediatric amplification, even if direct translation to clinical protocols lies beyond the current proposal's scope.

**The value is moderated by two considerations.** First, the largely confirmatory character of the central hypothesis means that positive results would primarily extend established findings rather than open a substantially new direction. Second, the integration across aims—how spectral resolution, band importance, and two-talker susceptibility jointly explain speech-in-noise performance—is the conceptual core of the project, yet the analytic plan for this cross-aim integration is thinner than the within-aim analyses. The attainable knowledge is therefore a well-characterized set of interrelated predictors and their developmental trajectories, rather than a definitive mechanistic model. This is a useful and achievable contribution for a single R01, and the design is well-matched to delivering it.

## Innovation

**The project advances a mature research programme through refined methods and a longitudinal framework, rather than through a substantially new question.** The central hypothesis—that selective attention, working memory and language contribute to speech-in-noise performance in children with hearing loss, with audibility acting both directly and through language development—largely consolidates findings that the investigative team has already published extensively [1]. What is novel is the methodological approach and the integration across processing routes, not the underlying conceptual claim.

**Three methodological contributions stand out.** First, the interstimulus interval manipulation in Experiment 1b dissociates sensory resolution from the memory demands of the spectral-ripple comparison task [1]. By systematically varying the retention interval from 0.4 to 3 seconds and examining how attention and working memory associations change with interval duration, this experiment can characterize the cognitive contribution to what is conventionally treated as a purely sensory measure. Kirby et al. (2019) demonstrated that nonverbal intelligence and visual working memory were associated with spectro-temporal ripple thresholds in children with hearing loss, and concluded that these relationships likely reflect the cognitive demands of the task [5]. The ISI manipulation is a genuinely original method for addressing this confound, and the proposal's willingness to specify alternative outcomes—including the possibility that children judge each interval independently without comparing retained representations—makes the experiment scientifically productive regardless of direction [1].

Second, the individual band-importance measurement in children with hearing loss using a hearing-aid simulator under headphones provides experimental control not achievable with personal hearing aids [1]. The ear-asymmetric control, where symmetric audiograms are expected to produce similar ear-specific weights and remaining asymmetry helps distinguish shared cognitive influences from ear-specific audibility effects, is an elegant use of the listener as their own control. Bosen et al. (2025) documented developmental shifts in frequency-band importance in children with normal hearing and concluded that additional work is needed on how hearing loss affects this trajectory [4]; the proposed experiments address that call directly.

Third, the 24-month longitudinal tracking across all three aims within a single sample represents a genuine advance over the existing evidence base, which is almost entirely cross-sectional. Characterizing how spectral resolution, perceptual weighting, and competing-speech susceptibility develop in relation to cognitive and linguistic growth in the same children would provide evidence that the field currently lacks [1]. The developmental questions—whether baseline spectral resolution predicts later speech recognition through cognitive development, whether selective attention growth shifts frequency-weighting profiles, and whether audibility gains accompany improvements in selective attention and sentence recognition—are well-motivated and cannot be answered from cross-sectional data alone.

**The innovation is moderated by the confirmatory character of the central hypothesis.** McCreery et al. (2020) already demonstrated that working memory and selective attention predicted sentence recognition in two-talker speech masking [2]. Lalonde et al. (2023) showed that vocabulary and aided audibility predicted two-talker performance in children with hearing loss [3]. The proposal's expected outcomes largely align with these published patterns. Finding further evidence for these relationships with refined methods and a longitudinal design would consolidate knowledge rather than challenge it, which is productive but limits the potential for scientific surprise.

## Rigor

**The experimental design is methodologically sophisticated and self-aware, with targeted manipulations that address known measurement confounds.** The proposal's central design strength is its explicit treatment of the cognitive confound problem: ostensibly sensory measures may reflect task demands rather than peripheral encoding. Three design features directly address this.

In Aim 1, the interstimulus interval manipulation varies the retention interval for spectral-ripple discrimination across 0.4, 1.2, and 3 seconds, predicting that longer intervals will strengthen associations with attention and working memory [1]. Kirby et al. (2019) showed that nonverbal intelligence and visual working memory were associated with spectro-temporal ripple thresholds in children with hearing loss, concluding that these relationships likely reflect the cognitive demands of the task [5]. The ISI manipulation can distinguish conditions where cognitive involvement is greater, and the proposal specifies that weak interval effects would themselves be informative by identifying task conditions less dependent on cognition [1].

In Aim 2, the ear-asymmetric control exploits bilateral testing: symmetric audiograms are expected to produce similar ear-specific frequency weights, so remaining asymmetry can help distinguish shared cognitive influences from ear-specific audibility or resolution effects [1].

In Aim 3, the masker-type comparison between speech-shaped noise and two-talker speech is grounded in McCreery et al. (2020), where working memory and selective attention predicted recognition only in two-talker speech, not in speech-shaped noise or amplitude-modulated noise [2]. This validates the assumption that competing speech differentially recruits cognitive resources.

**The measurement battery is well-specified.** Spectral-ripple discrimination uses narrowband octave-wide carriers at 500 and 2000 Hz with trial-level level roving, improving on the earlier broadband spectrotemporal task [1]. Band-importance estimation employs logistic regression of word recognition on the presence or absence of six octave bands across 300 trials, with individual weights centered to preserve relative importance [1]. The cognitive and language measures—PPVT-IV, WASI Vocabulary, PEBL Flanker and Switcher, AWMA subtests—are standardized tools with published psychometric properties. Audiometric monitoring, electroacoustic verification of aided SII, and data logging are specified at each visit [1]. The analysis plan deploys linear mixed models with participant intercepts, path models distinguishing predictors from mediators, and scientific reasoning to address collinearity, with preregistration and data sharing through the Open Science Framework [1].

**Two interrelated concerns limit the rigor.** First, despite the ISI manipulation, the spectral-ripple measure cannot be assumed to provide a pure index of peripheral spectral resolution. Davidson et al. (2021) also found that nonverbal intelligence predicted spectral modulation detection in pediatric cochlear implant users, confirming that cognitive demands are a general feature of such tasks rather than an artifact of one specific procedure [6]. If the ripple task already reflects cognitive ability, then finding that cognitive measures predict ripple performance—and that ripple performance mediates the cognition–speech-recognition relationship—estimates associations among measures that share cognitive variance. The proposal acknowledges this by specifying that failure to find mediation would still characterize the sensory-cognitive relation [1], but the positive mechanistic interpretation of any mediation found would remain provisional.

Second, the planned mediation analyses face power constraints. The proposal's power calculations address detectable bivariate effects, with Experiment 1a targeting group differences above d = 0.2 and associations above r = 0.3 in a three-predictor model [1]. Mediation analysis, however, tests indirect effects as the product of two path coefficients, with larger standard errors. With N = 80 per group and moderately correlated cognitive predictors, the power to detect indirect effects of plausible magnitude is uncertain. This concern is heightened for Experiment 3b, where Lalonde et al. (2023) showed the attention measure exhibited ceiling effects in children with hearing loss: 41.5% of that group scored at least 63 out of 66, and the attention–speech-recognition relationship was absent for children with hearing loss [3]. The proposal acknowledges that the attention task may have limited sensitivity for this group [1] but does not fully resolve how ceiling effects will affect the planned mediation models.

**These are limitations of degree rather than kind.** The design will produce a rich descriptive dataset on how spectral discrimination, perceptual weighting, and masked speech recognition interrelate with cognitive and linguistic skills, both cross-sectionally and longitudinally. The specification of alternative outcomes for most experiments—from weak ISI effects to stable band-importance weights—reflects genuine scientific uncertainty and ensures the data will be informative under multiple scenarios.

## Feasibility

**The project can be carried out as described: the core experimental methods are grounded in pilot data and published work, and the two-site recruitment model provides meaningful capacity.** The technical pathway is the strongest feasibility feature. The spectral-ripple task has been refined from an earlier spectrotemporal version to use narrowband carriers and level roving, addressing a known confound [1]. The band-importance procedure has been shortened from approximately four hours to under 30 minutes per child [1]. The two-talker masking paradigm shows expected correlations with attention measures in pilot data from 40 children with normal hearing (r = 0.52 and 0.51) [1]. These refinements substantially reduce technical risk. The hearing-aid simulator used in Experiment 2b replaces personal hearing aids for that specific experiment, trading ecological validity for the experimental control necessary to test band-level effects—a defensible scientific choice rather than an implementation barrier [1].

**Recruitment is ambitious but grounded in institutional resources.** The overall target of approximately 150 children with normal hearing and 200 with hearing loss across five years, plus 25 adults, is distributed between Boys Town National Research Hospital and the University of Iowa, both institutions with established pediatric audiology programs and existing participant databases [1]. The per-experiment targets of 80 per group include a buffer exceeding the assumed 10% attrition rate [1]. The testing burden of approximately 2,100 total hours across 955 visits corresponds to roughly 210 hours per site per year—approximately one full-time research assistant equivalent at each site [1].

**The eligibility criteria compound recruitment difficulty for the hearing-loss group.** Children must have bilateral hearing loss with better-ear PTA between 20 and 80 dB HL, must use hearing aids rather than cochlear implants, must demonstrate nonverbal cognition within 1.5 SD of age norms, must have at least 25% word recognition in quiet, must have no secondary disabilities, must have at least one hearing parent using spoken English at home, and must be primary English speakers [1]. In the population of children with hearing loss, secondary disabilities and non-English home language are not rare. The proposal does not provide estimates of how many children in the existing databases meet all criteria simultaneously, nor does it specify alternative recruitment pathways if the initial databases prove insufficient. The two-site design provides some resilience, but the absence of explicit contingency planning for under-recruitment is a planning gap.

**Longitudinal retention over 24 months faces additional pressure.** Families of children with hearing loss face additional clinical appointments and may relocate for educational or audiological services. A 10% attrition rate over two years in a pediatric clinical population is optimistic, and differential attrition between groups could bias longitudinal comparisons. The proposal acknowledges that additional recruitment will occur as children age out [1] but does not discuss how differential attrition would be handled analytically.

**The five-year schedule staggers aims to reduce concurrent demands.** Aim 1 occupies Years 1–3, Aim 2 Years 2–4, and Aim 3 Years 3–5 [1]. This sequencing prevents all nine experiments from competing for participants and staff simultaneously. However, Experiment 3c—the longitudinal follow-up of children with hearing loss in competing speech—concludes data collection in Year 5 without allocated manuscript preparation time within the grant period [1]. While post-award completion of final analyses is not unusual, it represents a dependency on sustained productivity beyond the funded period. The two-site coordination is supported by a single-IRB arrangement, shared experimental software and analysis code, and preregistration [1]. Overall, the project can be carried out as described, with recruitment and retention assumptions requiring active management during execution.

## Ethics and safety

**The study is described as minimal risk, and the proposed ethical safeguards and safety arrangements are proportionate, thorough, and appropriate for the activities described.**

Auditory safety is managed through hardware and software safeguards that limit signal levels below potentially damaging thresholds [1]. This is particularly important given that the study deliberately tests children with hearing loss, for whom residual hearing must be preserved. The use of headphones at 20 dB sensation level in Aim 1, and the presentation of speech materials at 65 dB SPL in the sound field, are within standard clinical and research safety limits. The specification that participants unable to tolerate 20 dB SL will be tested at a lower level, with absolute presentation level included in the interpretation, reflects an appropriate integration of safety and scientific considerations [1].

Probe-microphone measurements conducted at each visit to verify aided audibility involve inserting a flexible tube near the eardrum. The proposal acknowledges the associated discomfort risk and specifies mitigation through removal and repositioning of the tube if discomfort occurs [1]. This is a standard procedure in pediatric audiology research, and the management approach is consistent with established practice.

Informed consent and assent procedures are specified, with questions addressed at the study visit and oversight through a single-IRB arrangement across both participating institutions [1]. The inclusion criterion requiring at least one hearing parent using spoken English at home, while restrictive for recruitment, serves an ethical function in supporting families' capacity to provide fully informed consent and to understand study communications in their primary language [1].

A noteworthy ethical provision is the commitment to share clinically relevant audiological, language, or cognitive findings with the child's care or education team, contingent on caregiver permission [1]. This practice recognizes that research participation may incidentally generate information of direct clinical or educational value to the child, and respects parental autonomy in deciding whether that information should be disclosed. The arrangement is consistent with the team's embedded position within clinical audiology settings.

Data protection arrangements are comprehensive. Research records and recordings will be protected by access controls and password-protected storage; published reports will not identify children. De-identified data and documentation will be shared through the Open Science Framework under agreements that restrict reidentification and require secure use and return or destruction after analysis. Experimental and R analysis code will be released through the Boys Town GitHub repository [1]. These arrangements appropriately balance the benefits of open science—including preregistration and reproducible analysis code—against the obligation to protect participant confidentiality, particularly for a pediatric clinical population where reidentification risk must be carefully managed.

The study does not involve invasive procedures, investigational devices, or experimental clinical interventions. The behavioral tasks and non-invasive audiological measurements fall within the minimal-risk category as described. The overall ethics and safety arrangements are thorough, well-documented, and proportionate to the activities proposed.

## Management

**The two-site management structure draws on established institutional resources and prior collaborative experience, providing a credible framework for delivering the nine experiments.** The project is led by Ryan W. McCreery, with data collection split approximately equally between Boys Town National Research Hospital and the University of Iowa [1]. Both institutions operate established pediatric audiology programs with existing participant databases. The investigative team has published together extensively on the relationships among audibility, cognition, language, and speech recognition in children with hearing loss, including the McCreery et al. (2020) study demonstrating cognitive and linguistic predictors of masked speech recognition [2], and the Kirby et al. (2019) study documenting cognitive contributions to spectro-temporal discrimination [5]. This publication record demonstrates a functioning collaboration with shared protocols, co-authored analyses, and coordinated data collection across sites.

**The five-year schedule staggers the three aims appropriately.** Aim 1 cross-sectional data collection occupies Years 1–2 with longitudinal follow-up extending into Year 3; Aim 2 begins in Year 2 and runs through Year 4; Aim 3 launches in Year 3 and continues through Year 5 [1]. Manuscript preparation for most experiments is scheduled within the grant period: Aim 1 cross-sectional reports in Year 3, Aim 1 longitudinal and Aim 2 cross-sectional in Year 4, and Aim 2 longitudinal and Aim 3 cross-sectional in Year 5. The exception is Experiment 3c, whose data collection concludes in Year 5 with no manuscript preparation time allocated within the grant period. While post-award completion is common, this represents a dependency on productivity beyond the funded period.

**Operational resources are proportionate to the planned work.** The total testing burden of approximately 2,100 hours across 955 visits corresponds to roughly 210 hours per site per year—approximately one full-time research assistant equivalent at each site [1]. Shared measures and participant reuse across aims reduce duplication: children enrolled for Aim 1 can contribute to Aims 2 and 3 where group membership overlaps, and the cognitive, language, and audiological battery is administered once and leveraged across experiments [1]. The single-IRB arrangement streamlines ethics oversight, and shared experimental software and analysis code deposited on the Boys Town GitHub repository support protocol fidelity across sites [1].

**Two management risks deserve attention.** First, the restrictive eligibility criteria compound recruitment difficulty, as noted under Feasibility, and the proposal does not estimate what proportion of the existing participant databases meets all criteria simultaneously, nor specify alternative recruitment pathways [1]. Second, the assumed 10% longitudinal attrition over 24 months may be optimistic for a pediatric clinical population. The proposal budgets for attrition by recruiting beyond per-experiment targets [1] but does not specify how differential attrition between groups would be handled analytically. These are planning gaps that the experienced investigative team is well-positioned to manage during execution, given their demonstrated track record of pediatric audiology research at both sites, but they represent areas where proactive monitoring will be needed.

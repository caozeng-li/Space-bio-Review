# Project review

## Overall assessment

This project evaluates whether screening with digital breast tomosynthesis using synthesized two-dimensional mammography (s2D/DBT)—which eliminates the separate conventional digital mammogram and reduces radiation dose by approximately 45%—maintains clinical performance compared with the established combined DM/DBT workflow. Using a before/after natural experiment at seven radiology facilities that uniformly switched from DM/DBT to s2D/DBT, the study will analyze approximately 185,000 screening examinations with one-year cancer outcome ascertainment through pathology and registry linkage.

**The proposal’s most valuable expected contributions are genuine and clinically important.** They include the first multicenter estimates of sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rates for s2D/DBT screening with prospective outcome ascertainment; a structured lesion-specific analysis distinguishing invasive cancer, DCIS, and benign lesion subtypes to assess whether altered image appearance differentially affects detection; and an evaluation of whether s2D shifts radiologists’ BI-RADS density classifications, a question with direct implications for density notification policy and supplemental screening recommendations.

The project is strongly supported by the established PROSPR consortium data infrastructure, which has collected standardized screening and outcomes data since 2011 and already supported multiple peer-reviewed publications by this team, including Conant et al. (2016) [1], Sprague et al. (2016) [2], and Zuckerman et al. (2016) [3]. The common-facility, common-radiologist before/after design with uniform Hologic equipment and C-View reconstruction software removes important sources of confounding. The measurement framework—BI-RADS fifth-edition definitions, 12-month cancer registry linkage, and standardized pathology data aligned with College of American Pathologists and cancer registry standards—is rigorous and well-validated. The team’s published track record with the Miglioretti–Heagerty GEE analytical approach [4] confirms methodological competence.

**Two material limitations constrain the strength of conclusions on the most ambitious endpoints.** The before/after observational design cannot fully exclude temporal confounding from secular trends in screening practice and radiologist experience, and the minimum detectable difference in sensitivity of 5.6 percentage points at 80% power is too large to distinguish comparable sensitivity from a clinically meaningful degradation of 2–3 percentage points. Similar power constraints affect the lesion-specific analyses in Aim 2, where detectable differences represent large relative changes from baseline.

These limitations mean that null findings on sensitivity and lesion subgroups will not constitute strong evidence of equivalence. They do not, however, negate the study’s ability to produce useful evidence—particularly on recall, specificity, cancer detection rates, and density classification, where precision is adequate and clinical relevance is high. The project represents good value as a focused R03 secondary analysis that can inform practice while acknowledging the inferential limits of its observational framework. The current plan is sufficient for support with these caveats clearly understood.

**Overall conclusion: Support**

## Scientific value

**Scientific value: good.** The question—whether replacing conventional DM with s2D in the DBT screening workflow maintains screening performance while reducing radiation dose—is clinically important and directly relevant to screening policy, facility implementation decisions, and breast density notification practices. The multicenter design with one-year outcome ascertainment enables estimation of sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rates, going beyond the recall and cancer detection rates reported in prior single-institution series. The structured assessment of shifts in lesion-specific detection and BI-RADS density classification addresses clinically meaningful collateral effects of the technology change.

**The evidence gap at the time of the proposal’s design was genuine.** DBT combined with conventional DM had been shown to reduce recalls and improve invasive cancer detection (Friedewald et al., 2014 [5]; Conant et al., 2016 [1]), but acquiring both DM and DBT approximately doubles radiation exposure. Synthesized 2D images reconstructed from the DBT acquisition reduce dose by roughly 40–45% [6]. The critical uncertainty was whether s2D/DBT screening performance is comparable to DM/DBT for the full set of performance measures requiring outcome follow-up—not merely recall and cancer detection rates, but sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rates. The proposal correctly identifies that most prior studies lacked the one-year cancer ascertainment needed to estimate these measures [6].

The existing evidence at the time fell into several categories, each with identifiable gaps. Single-institution clinical series—Zuckerman et al. (2016) at the University of Pennsylvania, Ambinder et al., Aujero et al., and Freer et al.—had reported recall and cancer detection rates for s2D/DBT [3] [7]. The PI’s own Pennsylvania work compared 5,366 s2D/DBT with 15,571 DM/DBT examinations and showed recall of 7.1% versus 8.8% (P \< 0.001), biopsy rate of 1.3% versus 2.0%, and cancer detection of 5.0 versus 5.5 per 1,000 (P = 0.72) [3]. None of these studies reported sensitivity, specificity, or false-negative rates because they lacked the outcome follow-up necessary to ascertain interval cancers. Reader studies, of which the TOMMY trial was the most rigorous, reported reader-study sensitivity of 88% for s2D+DBT and 89% for DM/DBT on a retrospectively assembled case set with high cancer prevalence, but these estimates reflect enriched reading conditions rather than clinical screening performance [8] [9]. Other studies compared s2D/DBT with DM alone rather than with DM/DBT, which addresses a different clinical question [6].

**The attainable knowledge has several layers of practical value.** First, the s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT comparison is the clinically relevant decision faced by facilities already using DBT: whether to replace the conventional DM acquisition with s2D. This differs from the dominant comparison in the literature (DBT+DM versus DM alone) [6]. Second, the inclusion of seven facilities with 22 radiologists across academic and community settings in two demographically distinct regions—rural Vermont, predominantly white, and urban Philadelphia, with nearly half of screened patients African-American—provides evidence about performance across practice settings and patient populations that single-institution studies cannot offer [6]. Third, the structured lesion-specific analysis in Aim 2 addresses a biologically grounded concern: the altered image appearance of s2D may make architectural distortion more conspicuous while potentially suppressing the visibility of calcific lesions, with differential consequences for invasive cancer detection, DCIS detection, and benign biopsy rates [6]. Fourth, the density classification analysis in Aim 3 has direct relevance to breast density notification legislation and supplemental screening recommendations. The same team previously documented wide inter-radiologist variation in density assessment, from 6.3% to 84.5% of examinations called dense across 83 radiologists [2] [6].

**Scientific value is constrained but not negated by two material limitations.** The before/after observational design cannot exclude temporal confounding from secular trends in screening practice, radiologist experience with DBT, and population characteristics—though the common-facility and common-radiologist design removes fixed between-site confounding and represents the strongest feasible non-randomized comparison [6]. More importantly, the study is underpowered for its central sensitivity hypothesis: the minimum detectable difference of 5.6 percentage points at 80% power would correspond to a decrease from approximately 90.9% to 85.3% [6], a clinically alarming degradation far larger than the 2–3 percentage point differences that would represent plausible equivalence margins. The study therefore cannot provide strong evidence that s2D/DBT sensitivity is comparable to DM/DBT at clinically meaningful thresholds.

This limitation does not negate the study’s value, because it remains adequately powered for the clinically important endpoints of recall (detectable difference 0.4 percentage points), specificity (0.4 percentage points), cancer detection rate (0.8 per 1,000), and density classification (0.6 percentage points) [6]. These outcomes have direct implications for screening program implementation and patient experience. The practical value of the evidence is proportionate to the rapid clinical adoption of DBT and the need for data on the s2D/DBT workflow specifically.

## Innovation

**Innovation: good.** At the time the proposal was designed, the evidence base for s2D/DBT screening was developing rapidly but remained incomplete in several specific respects that this project directly addresses. The proposal’s contribution lies in being the first multicenter study to combine prospective outcome ascertainment, structured lesion-specific analysis, and explicit density classification evaluation for s2D/DBT screening. The overall approach is a logical extension of the team’s established PROSPR research program rather than a departure into new methods or conceptual frameworks, reflected in a ‘good’ rather than ‘excellent’ rating.

The proposal makes a distinct contribution through the combination of four elements not simultaneously present in any prior study. First, it mounts a multicenter design with 22 radiologists across seven facilities in two geographically and demographically distinct regions, moving beyond single-institution experience to provide evidence about performance across academic and community practice settings [6]. Second, it uses one-year cancer outcome ascertainment through pathology and state cancer registry linkage, enabling the first clinical—as distinct from reader-study—estimates of sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rates for s2D/DBT screening [6]. The PI’s own single-site work from Zuckerman et al. (2016), which compared 5,366 s2D/DBT and 15,571 DM/DBT examinations, reported recall rates, biopsy rates, and cancer detection rates but explicitly could not provide these outcome-based measures [3]. The TOMMY trial (Gilbert et al., 2015) reported reader-study sensitivity of 88% for s2D+DBT and 89% for DM/DBT on a retrospectively assembled case set with approximately 16% cancer prevalence, conditions that do not translate directly to clinical screening performance estimates [8] [9].

Third, the proposal structures the lesion-specific analysis to disaggregate detection of invasive cancer, DCIS, and benign lesion subtypes including non-proliferative changes, fibroepithelial proliferations, proliferative lesions without atypia, and atypical lesions [6]. This directly tests the mechanistic concern that s2D’s altered image appearance might differentially affect calcific lesion visibility and thus DCIS detection [6]. Fourth, the proposal explicitly evaluates whether s2D/DBT shifts radiologists’ BI-RADS density classifications, a question with direct policy relevance for the approximately half of U.S. states with density notification legislation and for supplemental screening recommendations [6]. The prior Sprague et al. (2016) study documented wide between-radiologist variation in density assessment [2], and the current proposal extends this line of inquiry to ask whether the imaging modality itself systematically shifts assignments.

The research question—s2D/DBT versus DM/DBT—is a natural next step from the team’s prior DM/DBT versus DM work (Conant et al., 2016 [1]; Friedewald et al., 2014 [5]), and the statistical methods using GEE with working independence and empirical standard errors are established approaches the team has validated in multiple BCSC publications [10] [11] [4]. The innovation therefore lies not in conceptual novelty or methodological invention but in assembling the right design elements to address a specific, clinically important evidence gap at a time when the available literature was insufficient to guide practice. Its contribution is proportionate to its focused R03 scope.

## Rigor

**Rigor: moderate.** The measurement framework and analytical methods are well-specified, validated, and appropriate. The before/after observational design exploits a natural experiment and removes important sources of confounding. However, two material limitations affect the study’s ability to answer its central hypotheses: the observational design cannot exclude temporal confounding, and the study is materially underpowered for its sensitivity and lesion-specific endpoints.

**The measurement infrastructure is a clear strength.** Screening performance is defined using the ACR BI-RADS fifth-edition standard: a positive examination is assessment category 0, 3, 4, or 5; assessments 1 and 2 are negative [6]. Cancer outcomes are ascertained through linkage to pathology reports and state cancer registries with a 12-month follow-up window, the accepted standard for identifying false negatives and calculating sensitivity, specificity, and false-negative rates [6]. The data elements align with College of American Pathologists and central cancer registry standards, and the same team has used this outcome-ascertainment pipeline in multiple published PROSPR studies including Conant et al. (2016) [1] [6].

The statistical approach—generalized estimating equations with a working independence assumption and three-step empirical standard errors to account for non-nested clustering within women, radiologists, and facilities—follows the Miglioretti–Heagerty method [4] that this team has applied and validated in major BCSC publications. Lowry et al. (2020) used this approach with robust covariance estimates for non-nested clustering to estimate relative risks of screening outcomes by modality across breast density, age, and screening round subgroups [11]. Sprague et al. (2023) applied the same framework with marginal standardization to estimate absolute screening outcome rates adjusted for differences in examination characteristics across modality groups [10]. This methodological continuity provides strong assurance that the analytical plan is executable and defensible. Exam-level covariate adjustment for age, race/ethnicity, breast density, family history, and screening interval is planned, and the inclusion of interaction tests between patient characteristics and modality is appropriate for identifying subgroups that may benefit differentially [6].

**The before/after design is the strongest feasible non-randomized comparison for this question.** Because the same seven facilities and 22 radiologists contribute examinations to both periods, the design removes confounding by fixed facility and radiologist characteristics—a genuine strength [6]. Uniform adoption at the facility level means patients did not select s2D versus DM/DBT individually, and the use of a single vendor (Hologic Selenia Dimensions with C-View reconstruction software) eliminates technology heterogeneity [6].

However, the before/after design cannot separate the effect of s2D from secular trends during 2011–2017. Radiologists’ increasing experience with tomosynthesis, evolving biopsy thresholds, and changes in the screened population could influence outcomes independently of the imaging modality. The proposal does not describe sensitivity analyses—such as comparing pre-switch trends in facilities that switched at different times—that would test the plausibility of the key assumption that temporal trends are not driving observed differences. This vulnerability is inherent to the design and is not fully mitigated by the planned covariate adjustments.

**More critically, the study is underpowered for sensitivity, one of its three primary endpoints.** The proposal projects approximately 1,114 cancers and states 80% power to detect an absolute difference of 5.6 percentage points in sensitivity [6]. The DM/DBT sensitivity baseline from the team’s prior PROSPR work is 90.9% [6], so the detectable decrease would be to approximately 85.3%—a clinically alarming degradation. The proposal’s own hypothesis is that sensitivity will be “comparable” between modalities [6]. A null result from this study cannot distinguish true comparability from a sensitivity loss of 2–3 percentage points, which would be clinically meaningful. This limitation arises because sensitivity is estimated from the small subset of examinations associated with cancer (approximately 1,114 cancers split across two modality groups), contrasting with the adequate precision for specificity, where 174,546 non-cancer outcomes provide power to detect a 0.4 percentage point difference.

The detectable differences for Aim 2—0.8 per 1,000 for invasive cancer, 0.5 per 1,000 for DCIS, and 1.5 per 1,000 for benign lesions at 80% power [6]—represent large relative changes from baseline. The DCIS baseline is 1.7 per 1,000 [6]; a 0.5 per 1,000 decrease represents a nearly 30% relative reduction. The single-site preliminary data showed DCIS detection of 0.9 versus 1.5 per 1,000 (P = 0.30), consistent with the hypothesized direction but not statistically significant [6]. The multicenter study will have greater precision, but the power calculations indicate that moderate differences in lesion-specific detection will remain unresolved. The subdivision of benign lesions into four histologic categories is a useful contribution, as existing s2D/DBT studies rarely provide this granularity, but these subgroup analyses will have even lower power.

The power calculations for recall (detectable difference 0.4 percentage points), specificity (0.4 percentage points), cancer detection rate (0.8 per 1,000), and density classification (0.6 percentage points) are adequate, and these endpoints have direct clinical relevance [6]. The density classification analysis benefits from the common-radiologist design: between-radiologist variation in density assessment, which the same team documented as ranging from 6.3% to 84.5% for calling examinations dense across 83 radiologists [2], is controlled by comparing each radiologist to themselves across periods. The detectable difference of 0.6 percentage points is precise relative to the dense classification baseline of 34.0% [6].

The proposal acknowledges that GEE correlation adjustment “could change variances” but expects “limited inflation because both modalities are represented within the same facilities, radiologists, and most women” [6]. This expectation is qualitatively reasonable—the before/after design with shared clusters does reduce between-cluster variance relative to a cross-sectional comparison of unrelated facilities—but intraclass correlation estimates from the preliminary data are not provided to quantify the actual design effect. Women with examinations in both periods contribute correlated observations that may inflate the effective variance more than anticipated, particularly for sensitivity.

**In summary,** the measurement infrastructure and analytical methods are strong, and the common-facility before/after design removes important sources of confounding. However, the study cannot resolve its hypotheses about sensitivity or lesion-specific detection at clinically meaningful effect sizes, and the observational design leaves residual uncertainty from temporal confounding that the planned analyses do not fully address. These limitations materially affect the inferences that can be drawn for the proposal’s central aims, warranting a moderate rating for rigor.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility: good.** The study is a retrospective analysis of existing, prospectively collected registry data requiring no new patient recruitment or investigational imaging. The data infrastructure, linkage pathways, and analytical methods are well-established and have already supported peer-reviewed publications by the same team. The projected sample sizes are arithmetically plausible given the participating facilities’ screening volumes, though the proposal provides transition timing and examination counts at a qualitative rather than fully verified level.

The project is fundamentally a secondary data analysis of modest scope, appropriately submitted under the R03 small-grant mechanism. The work consists of four defined steps: assembling exam-level records from seven facilities that uniformly switched from DM/DBT to s2D/DBT, pooling common data elements, establishing twelve-month cancer outcomes through pathology and registry linkage, and performing three coordinated statistical analyses using generalized estimating equations [6]. Each step uses existing data systems and established methods; no methodological development or new data collection is required.

**The data infrastructure is well-established and fit for purpose.** The Vermont and Pennsylvania PROSPR centers have collected standardized breast screening and outcomes information since 2011, with common data elements and existing linkage infrastructure that support pooled analysis [6]. Vermont’s center collects patient, radiology, pathology, and outcome data across the state’s breast imaging facilities; six facilities—three academic-center and three community-hospital sites—together screen approximately 38,000 women annually with 16 interpreting radiologists [6]. The Pennsylvania medical center screens approximately 11,000 women annually with six radiologists and adopted s2D/DBT uniformly in 2015 [6]. All facilities use Hologic Selenia Dimensions equipment with C-View reconstruction software, eliminating technology heterogeneity as a source of between-facility variation [6]. The data elements align with BI-RADS, College of American Pathologists, and central cancer registry standards [6].

The feasibility of extracting and analyzing data from this infrastructure is demonstrated by completed publications. Zuckerman et al. (Radiology, 2016) reported the Pennsylvania site’s s2D/DBT implementation using the same data source and outcome definitions proposed here [3]. Sprague et al. (Annals of Internal Medicine, 2016) analyzed breast density assessment variation across 216,783 mammograms from 83 radiologists at 30 PROSPR facilities, confirming the consortium’s capacity to pool and analyze exam-level data from multiple sites [2]. The PI is an author on both studies, and the team has a sustained publication record—Conant et al. (2016), Lowry et al. (2020), and Sprague et al. (2023)—demonstrating that the core extraction, linkage, and analysis functions operate reliably [1] [11] [10].

**The statistical methods are established and validated.** The Miglioretti–Heagerty GEE approach for non-nested clustering [4] has been applied across multiple BCSC publications with robust covariance estimates and marginal standardization, as detailed above [10] [11]. The analytical plan is executable without methodological development.

**The projected sample sizes are internally consistent but not fully verified.** The proposal projects approximately 185,700 examinations (98,850 DM/DBT, 86,850 s2D/DBT) yielding an anticipated 1,114 cancers at a detection rate of approximately 6 per 1,000 [6]. Given the reported annual screening volumes—approximately 49,000 across all seven facilities—the projected totals over the 2011–2017 window are arithmetically plausible. However, the proposal provides these projections at an aggregate level without facility-by-facility transition dates or year-by-year examination counts that would permit independent verification. The bulk of the s2D/DBT examinations must come from the six Vermont facilities, since the single-site Pennsylvania preliminary data contained only 5,366 s2D/DBT examinations [6], and the feasibility of assembling the full s2D/DBT cohort depends on when each Vermont facility transitioned. The proposal’s expectation that GEE correlation adjustment will produce “limited inflation” is qualitatively reasonable but is not supported by intraclass correlation estimates from the preliminary data that would quantify the design effect [6]. These are documentation limitations rather than evidence of infeasibility, and they are proportionate to the R03 mechanism’s expectations for a focused secondary analysis.

The ethical and data-access arrangements are already in place. Vermont’s registry uses a passive consent process achieving approximately 95% participation; Pennsylvania secured a waiver of informed consent [6]. Exam-level data will be transferred securely to Vermont for pooled analysis. No additional institutional approvals are required beyond what already exists for the PROSPR infrastructure, removing administrative barriers that could otherwise delay or prevent project execution.

## Ethics and safety

**Ethics and safety: good.** The study is a minimal-risk retrospective records analysis using clinical imaging obtained during routine care, with no additional research radiation exposure, experimental intervention, or biospecimen collection. The consent and data-governance arrangements are proportionate and already operational, though specific data-security measures are not detailed in the proposal.

The project introduces no physical risks to participants. All mammography examinations were performed as part of routine clinical screening; the study retrospectively analyzes the records of those examinations together with linked pathology reports and cancer registry data [6]. No woman is asked to undergo additional radiation exposure for research purposes—a relevant consideration given that the clinical motivation for adopting s2D/DBT is precisely the reduction in radiation dose of approximately 40–45% relative to combined DM/DBT [6]. This alignment between the research question and the absence of research-related radiation is ethically coherent.

**The primary risks are to data privacy and confidentiality.** The proposal describes two consent models that are both appropriate for a minimal-risk retrospective records study. The Vermont PROSPR registry uses a passive consent process on its imaging questionnaire, with an opt-out checkbox that women may check if they do not wish their data to be included; approximately 95% of eligible patients participate, indicating broad acceptance [6]. The Pennsylvania medical center secured a waiver of informed consent from its institutional review board for inclusion of women screened in its network [6]. A waiver is ethically justifiable because the study uses existing clinical records, involves no direct participant contact, and would be impracticable to conduct if individual consent were required for approximately 100,000 examinations spanning a six-year retrospective window. The waiver is consistent with Common Rule criteria for minimal-risk research where the research could not practicably be carried out without the waiver and where the waiver will not adversely affect subjects’ rights and welfare.

The inter-site data-sharing arrangement specifies that exam-level data from Pennsylvania will be transferred securely to Vermont for pooled analysis [6]. Centralizing analysis at a single site reduces the number of copies of the dataset and concentrates data-security responsibilities within the Vermont PROSPR center, which has extensive experience managing the consortium’s pooled data—as demonstrated by prior multicenter analyses including Sprague et al. (2016), which pooled 216,783 mammograms from 30 facilities across three PROSPR centers [2]. The proposal does not, however, specify the encryption standards, access controls, or data-retention policies that will govern either the transfer or the pooled dataset. For a study handling approximately 185,000 examination records with linked identifiers sufficient for registry and pathology matching, these are relevant details. This absence is a minor documentation limitation rather than evidence of disproportionate risk, given the team’s established track record and the embedding of data-handling practices within existing IRB-approved registry protocols.

No vulnerable populations are disproportionately burdened. The study population reflects the demographics of the participating facilities: Vermont sites serve a predominantly white rural population (96.2% white), while the Pennsylvania medical center serves an urban population with a large African-American representation (48.1% of screened women) [6]. This diversity is a scientific strength, permitting subgroup analyses across populations historically underrepresented in breast imaging research. The exclusion of women with a personal history of breast cancer is justified on clinical grounds—their imaging protocols often include additional views of the prior cancer site that would confound the comparison of standard screening workflows [6]—rather than representing an arbitrary restriction of research access.

In summary, the ethical and safety arrangements are proportionate to a minimal-risk secondary data analysis. No experimental intervention, no additional radiation exposure, consent models that balance respect for participant autonomy with the practical requirements of large-scale records research, and a data-pooling arrangement consistent with the team’s established PROSPR practices all support a sound safety profile.

## Management

**Management: moderate.** The PI and the broader PROSPR team have the relevant expertise and a demonstrated track record of delivering similar multicenter analyses. However, the proposal’s documentation of responsibilities, timeline, and coordination is minimal even for a focused R03 secondary analysis, leaving the management plan underspecified in ways that matter for a project dependent on multi-site data extraction and pooling.

**The expertise to execute the work is credibly present.** The PI, Brian Sprague, has led the PROSPR multicenter collaborations on screening performance and density assessment and is an author on the key publications that demonstrate the team’s capacity to deliver the kind of analysis proposed here. The prior DM/DBT versus DM study by Conant et al. (2016), on which Sprague is a co-author, pooled 142,883 DM and 55,998 DBT examinations from three PROSPR centers with one-year outcome follow-up, using the same data infrastructure, common data elements, and GEE analytical framework [1]. The density variation study by Sprague et al. (2016) analyzed 216,783 mammograms from 83 radiologists at 30 facilities—a substantially larger and more complex pooling exercise than the current proposal [2]. More recent work by the same group has applied these methods to successive BCSC datasets [11] [10]. The proposal states that the team “combines radiology, pathology, epidemiology, and experience in multicenter PROSPR collaborations” [6]; while specific named co-investigators and their roles are not provided in the supplied text, the publication record provides assurance that the required collaborative relationships and analytical expertise exist within the group.

**The project’s limited scope makes sparse management documentation less concerning than it would be for a trial or multi-site prospective study.** The work consists of assembling eligible records, linking outcomes, transferring Pennsylvania data to Vermont, and conducting GEE analyses—none of which involves developing new methods or coordinating ongoing data collection [6]. The single-vendor environment and uniform s2D/DBT adoption protocol reduce the complexity of data harmonization [6]. The data elements are already aligned with BI-RADS, College of American Pathologists, and cancer registry standards [6], and the Pennsylvania-to-Vermont secure transfer pathway has been used in prior consortium analyses [6].

What is missing—and what prevents a higher rating—is visible project management structure. The Implementation and Resources section consists of a single paragraph [6]. No timeline indicates when data extraction will be completed, when the pooled dataset will be ready for analysis, or when the three aims will be analyzed and reported. No specification identifies who at Vermont and who at Pennsylvania will extract and prepare the exam-level files. No plan describes data-quality checks before the pooled analysis begins. The R03 mechanism does not demand an elaborate management plan, but the proposal’s near-silence on coordination leaves open questions about how the two-site data assembly will be sequenced, reviewed, and quality-assured—matters that are not automatically resolved by the team’s prior collaborations.

The management criterion therefore rates moderate, reflecting capable people and proven infrastructure coupled with documentation that does not adequately describe the operational plan for the proposed work.

# Addressing Ethical Challenges in Networked Biorepositories

**Principal investigators:** Kyle B. Brothers and Aaron Goldenberg  
**Lead institutions:** University of Louisville and Case Western Reserve University  
**Mechanism:** R01

## 1. Project overview

This four-year mixed-methods project will characterize ethical and regulatory challenges in networked biorepositories and develop practicable recommendations for their governance. It combines a retrospective inventory and stakeholder interviews, prospective participant observation in five contrasting networks, and normative analysis refined through focus groups and an expert symposium.

A networked biorepository is defined here as a research collection providing centralized access to biospecimens, genotyping data, or health-related data collected at multiple institutions. Networks may use central databases, coordinated policies, or virtual interfaces while specimens remain at local sites. The project studies the ethical implications of these organizational arrangements.

## 2. Background and rationale

Precision medicine often requires very large and diverse cohorts to study rare diseases, rare variants, and biologically defined patient subgroups. Connecting existing repositories can conserve resources and retain relationships between local institutions and their communities. However, ethically permissible local practices may differ. A network must decide how to reconcile those differences without erasing donor commitments or creating unworkable barriers to research.

The principal domains are informed consent; donor control over specimens and data; investigator access; privacy and data security; return of research results; and governance and oversight. A donor may have limited the purposes or sharing of samples at one institution, while another site offers different choices. Client investigators may request broad access, whereas donor institutions see themselves as stewards. Returning a result found at a remote client site may require cooperation among investigators, central data managers, local clinicians, and donors. Local IRBs and community advisory bodies may also weigh priorities differently from a central network.

The study will examine how different network configurations address these tensions, including developing networks and an abandoned networking effort, rather than studying only successful mature organizations. It will also observe how networks respond to the changing regulatory environment, including genomic data-sharing policy and anticipated Common Rule changes. The project is not limited to the proposed Precision Medicine Initiative cohort, although its findings may inform such large collaborations.

## 3. Objectives and specific aims

1. **Retrospective aim:** identify established and developing networks, characterize their configurations, and examine ethical or regulatory challenges and solutions through interviews and primary-document analysis.
2. **Prospective aim:** observe governance, oversight, and problem-solving in representative networks as issues arise, including how organizational context influences deliberation and choice of solutions.
3. **Translational aim:** formulate generalizable recommendations for consent, privacy, security, access, governance, and oversight; refine them with network stakeholders and external experts.

## 4. Preliminary work and research basis

Prior work by the investigators inventoried and surveyed 465 US biobanks and conducted detailed studies of six. Sixteen percent described themselves as formal or informal networks, partnerships, or consortia. The work identified questions about data management and the fate of samples if an organizing institution closes or loses support. Its search and case-study methods provide the basis for the new network inventory.

Another project surveyed IRB administrative directors at 60 CTSA institutions and examined publicly available policies. Policies and practices for secondary sample use and sharing varied. Forty-one percent of available policies addressed sharing outside the institution, and 13% addressed contributions to public databases or repositories. These findings motivate examination of how local differences are managed within networks.

The team has experience in eMERGE, the Newborn Screening Translational Research Network, pediatric biobank consent, return of genomic findings, qualitative interviewing, and embedded observation of genomic decision-making. The project uses that access and experience to combine observations of actual practice with normative and legal analysis.

## 5. Research design and methods

### 5.1 Aim 1a: systematic network inventory

The search begins with networks identified in the earlier national biobank study. Adapted Boolean searches of PubMed and NIH RePORTER and keyword searches of Google, AAMC member institutions, and CTSA sites will identify further networks. Because terminology is inconsistent, the search will include varied descriptions and small or informal arrangements. Key contacts will be approached by email or telephone to verify network existence and organizational details and identify appropriate interviewees.

The inventory will characterize contributing institutions, network scale and maturity, the locations of specimens and data, governance arrangements, and access by contributing or external investigators. These organizational features will guide selection and interpretation of qualitative cases, rather than being treated as a single uniform network model.

### 5.2 Aim 1b: interviews and primary documents

Up to five stakeholders per network and up to 100 interviews in total are planned across years 1–3. An initial contact will provide both structured information about network organization and a semi-structured account of experiences. Snowball sampling and public organizational information will identify scientific and administrative leaders, operations managers, informaticists, ethics advisers, IRB members, community advisers, donor-study investigators or coordinators, and client researchers. In selected networks, the team will deliberately seek less powerful or dissenting stakeholders to reduce reliance on leadership-selected accounts.

The project will not interview specimen donors directly. Community advisory members and others tasked with representing donor priorities will be included; direct investigation of donors' experiences is reserved for subsequent work.

Interviews will last approximately 60 minutes and occur by video conference or around site and consortium meetings. Experienced investigators will use open-ended prompts and nondirective follow-up. Domains include consent harmonization, restrictions on sample use, return of results, access decisions, privacy–research trade-offs, local and network oversight, and stakeholder participation in governance. Prompts will distinguish practical problems from ethical responsibilities: for example, logistical difficulty in sharing is different from a perceived duty of stewardship. Interviews will be recorded, professionally transcribed, and conducted without participant incentives.

**Table 2. Interview domains and sample prompts**

| Domain | Sample prompts |
|---|---|
| Consent | What challenges have arisen? How have consent documents been harmonized across the network? |
| Donor control | What role should donors have in controlling sample use? How are differences among sites addressed? |
| Return of results | How aligned are perspectives? What processes return findings uncovered by client investigators? |
| Client access | How are research access and benefits weighed against privacy? How are client investigators or studies vetted? |
| Privacy and data security | Which measures protect donors most effectively? What trade-offs with researchability arise? |
| Oversight | How are local and network-wide interests balanced? How do local and community bodies participate? |
| Governance | How do stakeholders participate in governance? Which stakeholders should be more involved? |

Framework Analysis will begin after ten transcripts are available. Initial themes from previous research and interviews will be divided into subthemes and defined in a shared codebook. Two coders, one at each lead site, will independently code the same ten interviews in ATLAS.ti. The team will compare results and revise definitions or recode as necessary; after achieving at least 90% agreement, remaining interviews will be distributed across the team. All three aims will use a common ATLAS.ti project and shared codes to support comparisons across data types.

Consent documents, data-use agreements, IRB protocols, and other primary policies will be collected from as many networks as possible. These will be coded with the same thematic framework, allowing comparison between stated policies, reported experiences, and observed deliberations.

### 5.3 Aim 2: longitudinal participant observation

The prospective component asks what challenges arise in everyday efforts to build and sustain networks, how stakeholders identify and deliberate about them, and why different organizations adopt different solutions. It observes both the deliberative process and resulting practices. Investigators may ask for clarification or explanation to understand participants' assumptions and preferences.

Five provisional cases provide variation in maturity and configuration:

| Network | Development stage | Relevant arrangement |
|---|---|---|
| eMERGE | Mature | Study-based pooling and data-use agreements, including a central pharmacogenomics resource |
| Kentucky Cancer Registry | Mature | Locally stored tissue linked through a state registry, with potential national SEER networking |
| Newborn Screening Translational Research Network | New | State-held biosamples with de-identified phenotype information in the Virtual Repository of Dried Blood Spots |
| Sage Bionetworks–Synapse | New | Cloud-based sharing and tracking of data, results, methods, and tools |
| Mayo Clinic Biobank | In development | Locally held specimens with a planned system-wide cloud repository for genotype and phenotype data |

Findings from Aim 1 may justify replacing or adding cases within time and budget. The five cases demonstrate both organizational variation and initial access. Network leaders have agreed to observation of relevant activities.

One investigator and one research coordinator will be primary contacts for each case. They will track meetings, calls, and other relevant sessions with network staff, maintaining continuity whenever possible and arranging substitutes when needed. Observations will include consortium meetings, advisory groups, conference calls, and relevant governance bodies. In-person visits are planned twice annually per bank. Field notes will be recorded immediately after sessions in a secure digital workspace.

Every two weeks, investigators will discuss observations and reflect on how their presence may have influenced deliberation. Colleagues will ask clarifying questions and contribute interpretations; significant points will be incorporated into field notes before coding. This reflexive process is intended to make the effects of embedded participation visible, rather than assuming observation has no influence.

### 5.4 Aim 3: empirical and normative integration

The team will first review coded interviews, documents, and field notes to identify recurrent challenges in domains such as consent, privacy, security, and access. Priority will go to challenges distinctive to networks or requiring solutions different from those for individual repositories. The empirical findings will identify the problems and practical context; ethical and legal reasoning will be used to assess possible responses.

A normative core will examine conceptual and legal literature and evaluate candidate solutions according to their compatibility with ethical/legal standards, costs and practical trade-offs, and usefulness across different network configurations. For each domain, one investigator will lead a report describing the challenge, proposed recommendations, and supporting normative reasoning and empirical data. Team deliberation will integrate the reports.

Up to ten focus-group sessions will test these preliminary recommendations, each devoted to one report. Purposive recruitment will select stakeholders with practical experience in that domain. Groups will be composed with attention to power differences so that participants can speak freely. Participants will receive the draft beforehand, hear a short introduction, and discuss suitability and practical implementation using a moderated guide. Recordings will be transcribed and coded in the shared framework. Recommendations perceived as inadequate or difficult to implement will receive particular attention in revision.

A workshop-style symposium will then bring together national and international experts, case-study stakeholders, and other interested network developers. Large- and small-group discussions will consider whether recommendations address the identified problems across configurations and how future networking arrangements may create additional challenges. Immediately afterward, investigators will meet to synthesize feedback and finalize recommendations.

### 5.5 Outputs and dissemination

Outputs will include a characterization of network structures, accounts of ethical and regulatory problem-solving, domain-specific recommendations, and adaptable governance models or best practices supported by the study. Results will be disseminated through scholarly publications and meetings in genetics, bioethics, human-subjects protection, pathology, and public health. Networks will receive recommendations directly, with calls or webinars offered to discuss them. Partnerships with relevant organizations and agencies will broaden access beyond academic publication.

## 7. Ethics and data practices

Interview and focus-group participants will receive information, opportunities for questions, and consent procedures. They may decline questions, stop recording, or withdraw. Observation in changing groups requires coordination with network leadership and the study IRB: explicit individual consent may not be practicable for every transient participant, so transparency, repeated reminders, and appropriate authorization will be maintained.

Potential harms include discomfort, professional repercussions, and indirect identification of individuals or networks. Transcripts and reports will use study identifiers or pseudonyms. Network names will generally be withheld in publications unless permission is obtained. Access to recordings, transcripts, and linkage information is restricted to the research team; paper records are locked and computer files password protected. Original recordings will be destroyed once transcription and coding are complete. Focus groups will be arranged to encourage free expression and reduce fear of reprisals. Study participants are adult professionals and stakeholders.

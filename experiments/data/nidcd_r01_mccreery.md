# Complex Listening Skills in School-Age Hard of Hearing Children

Principal investigator: Ryan W. McCreery. Mechanism: R01.

## 1. Project overview

This project will investigate why children who use hearing aids continue to have difficulty understanding speech in noise even when amplification improves access to speech. Cross-sectional experiments and repeated assessments over 24 months will examine spectral resolution, individual perceptual weighting of speech frequencies, and susceptibility to competing talkers. The central hypothesis is that selective attention, working memory, and language contribute to listening performance, with hearing loss adding immediate and cumulative effects of reduced audibility.

The project will compare children with normal hearing (CNH), children with mild-to-severe hearing loss (CHL), and a mature normal-hearing adult comparison group for selected perceptual-weighting experiments. Its goal is a developmental explanation that can support future individualized amplification and rehabilitation.

## 2. Background and rationale

Early identification and fitting of hearing aids have improved developmental opportunities, but school-age CHL remain disadvantaged in noise. In our pilot dataset, 80 CNH and 130 CHL show relatively strong sentence recognition in quiet but substantially poorer recognition for CHL in speech-shaped noise at a +6-dB signal-to-noise ratio. Because children learn and socialize in noisy settings, this persistent deficit matters beyond laboratory speech scores.


Figure 1. Sentence recognition for 80 children with normal hearing and 130 children with hearing loss, grouped by degree of loss. Quiet performance is shown at left; performance in speech-shaped noise at +6 dB SNR is shown at right.

The Speech Intelligibility Index (SII) describes the audible proportion of the speech spectrum, weighted by frequency-band importance. Pediatric fitting approaches largely seek to restore access to speech in quiet and use average importance functions. Amplification, however, also increases access to competing sound. Children must attend to useful features, inhibit irrelevant information, segregate a target voice, and retain and process phonological information. Language knowledge further helps interpret degraded speech and may change the relevance of acoustic cues.

These processes can complicate interpretation of ostensibly sensory measures. A spectral-ripple task requires attention to differences and comparison of stimuli held in memory. A poor threshold may therefore reflect sensory resolution, task demands, or both. Similarly, the frequency bands a child uses for recognition may depend on selective listening and vocabulary rather than a universal adult weighting function. Competing speech adds informational masking to energetic overlap, increasing demands on segregation and inhibition.

The proposed framework separates three routes: attention to spectral features, inhibition of less useful frequency information, and segregation of target from masker speech. Working memory can affect all tasks requiring storage and processing. Language is expected to matter particularly when target or masker content is speech. In CHL, audibility can act immediately on access to the signal and cumulatively through language development.

## 3. Objectives and specific aims

1. **Quantify sensory and cognitive contributions to spectral resolution and speech recognition in noise.** Test direct and mediated relationships, manipulate interstimulus interval to vary memory demands, and determine how baseline abilities predict development over two years.
2. **Characterize individual perceptual weights for speech.** Test whether selective attention and language predict concentration of importance in middle frequencies, whether audibility modifies weights in CHL, and whether weights change with development.
3. **Evaluate selective attention in speech masking.** Compare speech-shaped noise with a two-talker masker, examine language and working memory as mediators, and test immediate and longitudinal contributions of aided audibility in CHL.

## 4. Preliminary work and basis

Previous studies from the team showed that aided audibility and daily hearing-aid use vary substantially and have cumulative associations with speech recognition and language. Nearly half of studied children did not have optimally audible fittings. Vocabulary and working memory predicted performance across tasks and ages, and language mediated some effects of audibility. The team also found interactions between signal-processing parameters and masking, and between spectral resolution and benefit from frequency lowering.

In 18 CHL aged 6-12, better visuospatial working memory was associated with detecting more spectrotemporal ripples per octave after controlling for age (partial r = .42, p = .04). The combined spectrotemporal task could not separate spectral from temporal information and used a broadband carrier without a level rove. The proposed tasks address these limitations with narrower carriers and level roving. A further pilot in ten CNH compared 0.4-, 1.2-, and three-second interstimulus intervals; age and working-memory associations increased with interval duration, motivating a direct test of task demands.


Figure 3. Spectrotemporal-ripple thresholds versus visuospatial working memory. The age-adjusted partial correlation is r = .42, p = .04.

Reanalysis of consonant-vowel-consonant nonword data from 96 CNH revealed substantial variation in band importance; better language was associated with greater weight at two kHz (r = .41, p < .001). The older filtering method required about four hours per child. A newer procedure yields repeatable estimates in less than 30 minutes. Pilot data from five CNH suggest that older children place more weight at two kHz and less at four kHz than younger children.

## 5. Research design and methods

### Shared participants and assessments

Children will enter at ages 6-12, with CNH and CHL matched on age, sex, and maternal education. Adults aged 19-40 provide the mature comparison in Aim 2. Normal-hearing participants must have bilateral pure-tone averages below 20 dB HL at 0.5, one, and two kHz. CHL will have better-ear averages of 20-80 dB HL, including sensorineural, conductive, or mixed losses. Cochlear-implant users and children with unilateral hearing loss are excluded because their mechanisms may differ. English is the primary language. Nonverbal cognition must be within 1.5 standard deviations of the age norm on WASI Matrix Reasoning, and children must recognize at least 25% of words in quiet at 65 dB SPL. Eligibility additionally requires absence of secondary disabilities and at least one hearing parent using spoken English at home.

The main experiment groups generally contain 80 CNH and 80 CHL, reused across aims when feasible. Overall recruitment will include approximately 150 CNH and 200 CHL across five years, plus 25 normal-hearing adults, allowing for roughly 10% attrition and additional recruitment as children age out. These overall numbers are distinct from per-experiment targets.

Receptive vocabulary will be measured with PPVT-IV and expressive vocabulary with the WASI Vocabulary subtest; standardized scores can be combined to represent language. Selective attention will be assessed with PEBL Eriksen Flanker inhibition and Switcher attention-switching tasks. AWMA Nonword Recall, Listening Recall, Block Recall, and Odd One Out assess verbal and visuospatial storage and working memory. Visual or nonverbal tasks reduce confounding by audibility and language where possible. Highly correlated measures can be combined into construct Z-scores.

Audiometric thresholds from 0.25-8 kHz will be measured at each visit. Monosyllabic word recognition in quiet at 65 dB SPL supplies a baseline and eligibility check. Most speech testing in CHL uses personal hearing aids at their ordinary settings, in sound field at zero-degree azimuth; the headphone simulator procedure in Aim 2 is a specified exception. Hearing history, fitting age, daily use, data logging, and remote-microphone use will be documented. Electroacoustic measurements at each visit will quantify aided SII for 65-dB-SPL speech, deviation from Desired Sensation Level targets as RMS error, and enabled processing features.

### Shared analysis and reproducibility

Linear mixed models will include participant intercepts to account for repeated conditions and visits. Random slopes, heterogeneous variance terms, and alternative within-person covariance structures may be added, with AIC or BIC guiding model selection. Path models will distinguish predictors, mediators, outcomes, and controls. Scientific reasoning and empirical variable reduction will address collinearity before hypothesis testing.

Where feasible, power calculations used 1,000 simulated datasets with alpha .05 and 80% power. Detectable effects are specified for each experiment below. Speech responses will be recorded and a subset independently scored by a blinded examiner. Experimental designs and analyses will be preregistered, and experimental software, analysis code, and appropriately protected data will support replication.

### Aim 1: spectral resolution and cognitive demands

**Experiment 1a** will enroll 80 CNH and 80 CHL. The stated design detects group differences above d = .2 and associations above r = .3 in a three-predictor model. An adaptive three-alternative forced-choice task will measure spectral-ripple discrimination under headphones at 20 dB sensation level (SL). Octave-wide carriers centered at 500 and 2,000 Hz and ripple densities of one and two ripples per octave permit examination of frequency dependence, especially for sloping losses. Narrowband carriers and trial-level roving reduce reliance on overall loudness.

Pediatric AZ-Bio sentences at 65 dB SPL will be tested in speech-shaped noise matched to the corpus. A one-up/one-down procedure estimates the signal-to-noise ratio for 50% keyword recognition (SNR50). Mixed models will assess hearing status, attention, memory, and spectral resolution; path analysis will test whether spectral effects are direct, mediated, or both. Better-ear aided SII is a CHL covariate. Participants unable to tolerate 20 dB SL will be tested at a lower SL, and absolute presentation level will be included in interpretation. Failure to find the predicted mediation would still characterize the sensory-cognitive relation.

**Experiment 1b** will reuse the same participants and spectral-ripple task with interstimulus intervals of 0.4, 1.2, and three seconds. Increasing the retention interval is predicted to strengthen associations with attention and working memory. Models will examine hearing status, interval, and cognitive measures, with attention expected to predict lapses or variability and working memory expected to preserve performance at longer intervals. An alternative is that children judge each interval independently as containing or lacking a feature, reducing the need to compare retained representations. Weak interval effects would therefore help identify a task less dependent on cognition or motivate a revised measurement strategy.

**Experiment 1c** will repeat baseline measures at 12 and 24 months. The longitudinal experiment will include the 80 CHL and 80 CNH from the baseline experiments. Models containing baseline attention, memory, and spectral resolution will be compared with models containing only cognitive measures, allowing individual growth slopes. We predict that baseline spectral resolution may contribute to prediction through cognitive development without retaining a direct later association. Bidirectional relationships are an alternative to be examined. Repeated unaided SII will account for hearing changes that could alter both audibility and spectral performance.

### Aim 2: individual frequency-band importance

**Experiment 2a** will compare 80 CNH and 25 normal-hearing adults. The stated detectable effects are d > .10 for weighting differences and r > .30 for a three-predictor model. Participants will repeat filtered CVC nonwords and real words through headphones in one randomly selected ear. Six octave bands centered from 250 Hz through eight kHz will be included or omitted. Forty all-band words establish baseline recognition. Six blocks of 50 words then present three of six bands per item: all 20 combinations appear 15 times, using a different word on each presentation.

Logistic regression of recognition against band presence estimates each band's contribution averaged across its combinations with other bands. Each listener's weights are centered by subtracting the mean log odds across all six bands, preserving relative band importance while reducing baseline-performance effects. Adult mean functions will be compared with prior adult results as a validation check. CNH weights will be related to attention and language, and then to sentence recognition using the Aim 1 noise procedure.

The hypothesis is that stronger selective attention predicts higher weights at one and two kHz and lower weights at four and eight kHz, with more evenly distributed weights for nonwords when lexical support is reduced. Working memory and language will be controlled when relating weights to speech recognition. Missingness and within-block variability will be examined against attention, because failure to complete the task could selectively exclude the children most important to the hypothesis.

**Experiment 2b** will assess 80 CHL, with stated sensitivity to weighting differences above d = .15. CVC words and nonwords will be presented at 65 dB SPL through a hearing-aid simulator under headphones, permitting precise control of band levels. Both ears will initially be tested. Models will include attention, working memory, language, sensation level or band stimulus level, and spectral resolution. Bands that are poorly audible or poorly resolved are predicted to contribute little to recognition. Symmetric audiograms are expected to produce more similar ear-specific weights; remaining asymmetry can help distinguish shared cognitive influences from ear-specific audibility or resolution.

For Experiment 2b, the expected association of selective attention is higher weighting at one and two kHz and at four and eight kHz. If pilot data show strongly correlated weights between ears, subsequent 2b testing can use the better ear to reduce burden.

**Experiment 2c** will follow 50 CNH and 80 CHL at baseline, 12 months, and 24 months, with stated sensitivity to changes above d = .10 in three-predictor mixed models. CVC-word weights, attention, and language will be repeated; CHL additionally undergo sensation-level and aided-audibility assessment. Growth in selective attention is predicted to shift weighting from four/eight kHz toward one/two kHz after controlling language. Increased aided sensation level is predicted to increase the contribution of affected bands. Stable weights are a meaningful alternative that would support the current assumption of temporal stability rather than the proposed developmental shift.

### Aim 3: selective listening in competing speech

**Experiment 3a** will test 80 CNH, with stated sensitivity to associations above r = .2 between masking conditions. Pediatric AZ-Bio targets fixed at 65 dB SPL will be presented in unmodulated speech-shaped noise and a two-talker masker. Two interleaved adaptive tracks will estimate performance functions, and SNR50 will be based on the final six reversals per track. Attention, language, and working memory will be entered into mixed and mediation models.

Stronger attention is predicted to reduce susceptibility to competing speech. A remaining attention effect after cognitive-linguistic controls would support a specific role in attending to the target and inhibiting masker talkers. Mediation by vocabulary or memory would instead indicate a more indirect mechanism. If SNR50 tracks have excessive excursions or poor within-child repeatability, the procedure may target 71% correct to improve reliability.

**Experiment 3b** will apply the same comparisons to 80 CHL using their personal hearing aids. Aided SII will be measured separately for each ear and included with attention, language, memory, and masker type. Path analysis will test whether audibility has an immediate association with recognition and an indirect association through language. The expected result is better performance with greater audibility in both masking conditions. Alternative outcomes include language as the primary predictor or cognitive effects that are not specific to competing speech; either result will revise the proposed account.

**Experiment 3c** will repeat 3b measurements at 12 and 24 months in the 80 CHL. Growth models will test whether higher baseline audibility predicts gains in language and selective attention that in turn accompany better sentence recognition, with larger effects expected for competing talkers. Age will be included because older children may approach an asymptote. If the selected attention measures become less informative near adolescence, other age-appropriate PEBL tasks may be considered, including distinct measures for younger and older participants.

## 6. Implementation, resources, and schedule

Approximately half the child sample will be recruited through Boys Town National Research Hospital and half through the University of Iowa. Existing participant databases and experience with pediatric audiology, cognition, language testing, hearing-aid simulation, and longitudinal analyses support recruitment and implementation. Shared measures and reuse across aims reduce duplication while retaining the separate sample requirements.

The five-year schedule begins Aim 1 cross-sectional data collection in years 1-2, with manuscripts in year 3, and longitudinal data collection through year 3 with manuscripts in years 4-5. Aim 2 begins in year 2: cross-sectional collection extends through year 3, while longitudinal collection extends through year 4 and reporting into year 5. Aim 3 begins in year 3, with cross-sectional collection through year 4 and longitudinal collection through year 5. Each longitudinal series uses baseline, 12-month, and 24-month visits.

## 7. Ethics and data management

Children and caregivers will complete the applicable consent and assent procedures, with questions addressed at visits. Auditory signals will be limited below potentially damaging levels using hardware and software safeguards. Probe-microphone discomfort will be addressed by removing and repositioning the flexible tube. The study is described as minimal risk and will proceed under institutional review with a single-IRB arrangement for participating institutions. With caregiver permission, clinically relevant audiological, language, or cognitive results may be shared with the child's care or education team.

Research records and recordings will be protected by access controls and password-protected storage. Published reports will not identify children. Speech responses will be audio-recorded for analysis, and video recordings will support reliability checks. De-identified data and documentation will be shared through the Open Science Framework under agreements restricting reidentification and requiring secure use and return or destruction after analysis. Experimental and R analysis code will be released through the Boys Town GitHub repository. Preregistration will precede collection.

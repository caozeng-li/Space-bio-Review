# Defining Trajectories of Linguistic, Cognitive-Communicative and Quality of Life Outcomes in Aphasia

Principal investigators: Allen W. Heinemann and Leora R. Cherney. Mechanism: R01.

## 1. Project overview

This prospective observational study will describe recovery after stroke-related aphasia from inpatient rehabilitation through 18 months after onset. It will integrate language performance, cognitive-communicative function, patient-reported participation and quality of life (QoL), rehabilitation exposure, and biological markers. Individual growth-curve (IGC) models will estimate both typical recovery patterns and variation associated with patient, stroke, and treatment characteristics.

The study will consent 400 patients across three inpatient rehabilitation facilities (IRFs), aiming to retain 300 at 18 months. A subsample of 100 at the Shirley Ryan AbilityLab will undergo resting-state functional MRI and associated structural and diffusion imaging. Saliva-based candidate-polymorphism measures and clinical imaging will complement behavioral assessments. Treatment remains part of ordinary clinical care; the study measures its timing, amount, content, and continuation rather than assigning an intervention. Final models will be examined for internal stability and used to develop an interactive trajectory tool.

## 2. Background and rationale

Aphasia can affect comprehension, speech, reading, and writing and can restrict participation in rehabilitation, employment, social relationships, and community life. Improvement on an impairment test does not necessarily describe a person's communication confidence or ability to participate. Most previous aphasia studies have emphasized linguistic outcomes, used small or retrospective samples, or measured only a few endpoints. Differences in participants, outcome instruments, lesion characteristics, and assessment timing have contributed to inconsistent findings about recovery predictors.

Rehabilitation is also heterogeneous. The amount of speech-language therapy within an inpatient stay competes with other rehabilitation needs, and post-discharge services vary across outpatient clinics, private treatment, community groups, and intensive programs. Treatment amount, intensity, and duration may be modifiable, but their associations with longer-term communication and QoL need to be measured alongside initial severity and other patient characteristics. Clinical billing records provide time and service codes but do not adequately describe what language modality or task was treated.

The proposed framework links demographic and clinical characteristics, genetic and brain biomarkers, and treatment exposure to three outcome domains. Lesion volume may matter, but location and the status of surviving networks may contribute additional information. Genetic variants related to neuroplasticity may interact rather than act independently. The project therefore integrates candidate gene-gene interactions and resting-state connectivity with clinical predictors instead of treating any one marker as determinative.

Recovery is a trajectory rather than a single admission-to-discharge difference. Patients may improve at different rates, approach different plateaus, and reach those plateaus at different times. IGC models retain this individual variation while estimating population tendencies. Linear, polynomial, and negative-exponential forms can describe different patterns; the analysis will select among them using observed data. Patient-reported instruments extend the analysis beyond impairment and permit investigation of whether participation and QoL continue improving after linguistic recovery slows.

## 3. Objectives and specific aims

1. **Establish the cohort and define recovery trajectories.** Measure linguistic, cognitive-communicative, and health-related QoL outcomes at admission and discharge and at six, 12, and 18 months after stroke. The hypothesis is that linguistic improvement slows around six months, whereas communicative participation and QoL improve toward 18 months.
2. **Identify associated factors within the recovery model.** Examine demographic and clinical characteristics; inpatient, outpatient, and informal aphasia services; and genetic and neuroimaging biomarkers. Smaller lesions, fewer comorbidities, and less severe initial aphasia are expected to accompany greater gains. Earlier, more intensive, and longer treatment is expected to accompany better outcomes. Biomarkers are hypothesized to interact with clinical and demographic characteristics.
3. **Evaluate model stability and prediction.** Assess how consistently predictors are selected in bootstrap samples and how accurately models predict held-out observations. Stable models should retain key variables frequently, and prediction errors should be no greater than the minimally clinically important difference for the relevant outcome and time point.

## 4. Preliminary work and basis

The team has characterized inpatient therapy use and its relation to functional gains. Earlier work associated longer rehabilitation stays and greater function-focused occupational-therapy intensity with some outcomes after controlling stroke severity. That work underrepresented aphasia and only measured admission and discharge, motivating the current condition-specific longitudinal study. Prior IGC applications in spinal cord injury demonstrated how demographic, injury, and rehabilitation characteristics can describe individual plateaus and rates.

Extraction of billing records from 71 stroke admissions showed that therapy time could be assembled and related to length of stay. The median stay was 12 days, with a reported standard deviation of 9.2. These records establish extraction feasibility but lack the task content that the proposed therapist logs will capture.

In a pilot of eight people with left-hemisphere stroke and aphasia, structural and resting-state imaging and behavioral testing were obtained before and after four weeks of intensive therapy. Naming, Aphasia Quotient (AQ), repetition, nonverbal reasoning, and attention improved, while reading and writing changes were trends. Principal-component analysis produced language-related and attention-related factors explaining 85% of baseline and 88% of post-treatment variance. Changes in language and attention were not correlated, suggesting that their recovery may involve distinguishable mechanisms.

## 5. Research design and methods

### Cohort and assessment schedule

Recruitment will occur at Shirley Ryan AbilityLab in Chicago, Alexian Brothers Rehabilitation Hospital in Elk Grove Village, and Mary Free Bed Rehabilitation Hospital in Grand Rapids. Participants will be at least 21 years old, on their first IRF admission after a first stroke with aphasia. Eligibility further requires a first left-hemisphere infarct confirmed by CT or MRI and excludes right-hemisphere lesions and other neurological conditions likely to affect cognition or speech. Sufficient capacity to consent and participate in testing is required, with aphasia-supportive communication used during recruitment and assessment.

Four hundred participants will be enrolled over approximately 30 months to retain 300 at 18 months. We anticipate 15% loss by six months and an additional 10% by 18 months. The imaging subsample is drawn from eligible AbilityLab patients. Participants unable to undergo MRI can still contribute the core observational data and available clinical imaging.

The IRF Patient Assessment Instrument will be extracted at admission and weekly during rehabilitation. NIH Stroke Scale assessments occur at admission and discharge. The principal behavioral and self-report measures are collected at admission/discharge and six, 12, and 18 months according to Table 3. Follow-up visits last approximately three to four hours and can be split across consecutive days or conducted at home where appropriate. Saliva is obtained near admission, and the specialized MRI protocol is acquired at admission for the imaging subset.

### Outcomes and clinical predictors

The primary linguistic outcome is the WAB-R Aphasia Quotient. WAB-R language and cognitive quotients provide additional information on reading, writing, calculation, praxis, drawing, and visuospatial performance. The primary cognitive-communicative outcome is the ten-item Communicative Participation Item Bank (CPIB), and the primary QoL outcome is the Stroke and Aphasia Quality of Life Scale-39 (SAQoL-39). These measures address impairment, participation, and lived impact rather than assuming they recover together.

Secondary measures include Connors Continuous Performance Test-3 for attention; the ten-item Communication Confidence Rating Scale for Aphasia; the caregiver-rated, 16-item Communicative Effectiveness Index; and seven Neuro-QoL banks covering fatigue, sleep, depression, cognitive function, communication, social-role satisfaction, and ability to participate in social roles. PROMIS Global Health measures overall physical and mental health. The 16-item Apraxia of Speech Rating Scale characterizes co-occurring apraxia after screening speech errors and stress-pattern timing.

Demographic information will be obtained from participants and reconciled with records. Stroke type, lesion site and volume, comorbidities using the Charlson index, and current medications will be documented. Certified examiners administer NIHSS. The IRF-PAI provides repeated functional observations from routine care.

Global ratings of change will ask about perceived communication improvement relative to baseline and prior visits. The response scale runs from -1 for worse through zero for unchanged to one, two, and three for increasing improvement. Sensitivity and specificity across response options will help estimate a threshold for minimal improvement. Ratings will be obtained at discharge and at six, 12, and 18 months.

### Therapy exposure and reliability

Weekly facility records provide minutes of individual, group, and co-treatment speech-language therapy. Clinicians will log each task's start and stop time on tablets and identify its target modality: auditory comprehension, oral expression, reading comprehension, or written expression. Progress notes will be coded for task content and patient participation. The resulting minutes and activity categories will be aggregated weekly, informed by rehabilitation-treatment taxonomy work.

A survey of 56 speech-language pathologists at participating sites supported the feasibility of identifying targeted modalities. Before and during the study, clinicians will receive training and feedback until they exceed 90% reliability in recording timing and modality. Ten percent of treatment sessions will be video-recorded to check logs. For new clinicians or those needing more practice, sessions can be recorded for later coding or observed and logged by a research assistant until reliability is adequate.

After discharge, patients or caregivers will maintain weekly checklists of formal and informal services, including outpatient therapy and community programs. Research assistants will call weekly during the first two months after discharge and monthly thereafter to review exposure and maintain contact. Thus treatment predictors describe actual service use across settings. Their associations with outcomes will be adjusted for patient characteristics, but treatment was not randomized.

Commercial TaqMan allelic-discrimination assays will use allele-specific FAM/VIC probes. Instrument software will assign genotypes, with independent k-means clustering used to check assignments and false positives.

The analysis focuses on epistasis among candidate neuroplasticity-related variants. Multifactor dimensionality reduction will classify multidimensional genotypes into high- and low-risk groups, and a high-risk indicator will be entered with adjusted covariates into regression models. Testing that indicator supplies the proposed gene-gene interaction test. These candidate-marker results will then be integrated with demographic, clinical, treatment, and imaging information rather than interpreted as independent genetic causes of recovery.

### Structural, functional, and diffusion imaging

The AbilityLab subset will receive two resting-state scans to estimate reliability, a one-millimeter T1 anatomical scan, axial T2 FLAIR, and two-shell diffusion imaging. Clinical structural imaging from other sites provides lesion information for the broader cohort. Processing will use validated pipelines adapted from the Human Connectome Project and established packages including FSL, SPM, FreeSurfer, and Caret, together with custom routines.

T1 data will quantify global tissue volume, regional gray-matter properties, subcortical shape, and lesion size and location. SIENAX will normalize global volume for skull size. Voxel-based morphometry and nonlinear registration will be checked carefully for lesion-related distortion; lesion masks and the described virtual-brain-transplant approach will reduce warping. T2 FLAIR will support leukoaraiosis-volume estimation using the specified histoseg method.

Functional connectivity will be examined using whole-brain voxel methods and validated parcellations ranging from 90-500 regions. Network densities will range from two to 50%, allowing assessment of threshold sensitivity. Clustering, global efficiency, modularity, and nodal properties will describe segregation, integration, and network organization. Measures involving language-related inferior frontal and superior temporal regions are among the specified outputs.

Diffusion data will be corrected for eddy currents and motion, fitted with voxelwise tensors, and used to derive fractional anisotropy (FA). Individual FA maps will be aligned to a common one-millimeter space and projected onto a group white-matter skeleton. Probabilistic tractography using PROBTRACKX will produce voxel- or region-based connection matrices for graph analysis. Structural and functional measures will be tested as predictors, with their added information judged alongside conventional clinical variables.

### Aim 1: estimate unconditional recovery curves

Separate IGC models will be constructed for each primary and secondary outcome, initially using time alone. Ordinal outcomes may be transformed to interval measures with Rasch analysis. Candidate forms include linear, quadratic, cubic, and negative-exponential trajectories, with participant random intercepts and slopes and a suitable within-person spatial covariance structure. Residual diagnostics, Akaike information criterion, and Bayesian information criterion will guide selection. SAS PROC MIXED and PROC NLMIXED are specified. Models treating time categorically provide a sensitivity comparison.

Suitable long-term aphasia parameters are unavailable for reliable simulation-based sample-size estimation. A final sample of 300 is feasible and is supported by experience with longitudinal mixed models for robust trend estimation. All available longitudinal assessments will contribute under the missing-at-random assumption. Intermittent missed visits need not remove a participant; dropout is especially important because it may challenge that assumption.

### Aim 2: add explanatory factors

The selected time function will be expanded with demographic, clinical, therapy, imaging, and genetic covariates. Purposeful model selection will examine clinically relevant interactions and appropriate forms for continuous predictors. Models from separate conceptual groups will then be combined to assess added information and derive a parsimonious final model. We will compare demographic plus clinical predictors; demographic plus biomarkers; demographic, clinical and biomarkers; demographic, clinical and treatment; and all four predictor groups together.

The expected associations are poorer outcomes with larger lesions or more severe initial aphasia, and better outcomes with greater therapy exposure and stronger relevant connectivity. Candidate polymorphism associations will be evaluated within the proposed interaction framework. The final, internally assessed models will allow an interactive tool to generate an expected trajectory from entered patient, stroke, and therapy characteristics.

### Aim 3: internal stability and prediction checks

Five hundred bootstrap resamples will assess coefficient distributions and predictor-selection frequencies. Each final model will be refitted, and backward selection from the full candidate model will also estimate selection probability. Variables with low reproducibility will be reconsidered, while those with high selection frequency may inform refinement.

Monte Carlo cross-validation will independently split cases into training and validation sets 500 times. The final predictor set will be fitted in each training sample and used to predict held-out outcomes at scheduled times. Median absolute deviation between observed and predicted scores will summarize performance, and its distribution across splits will provide confidence intervals. Prediction will be considered adequate when this deviation is no greater than the relevant minimally clinically important difference. Model assessment will use bootstrap and internal cross-validation. Slow recruitment or excessive loss may be addressed by longer accrual, added recruitment resources, or a fourth site to preserve the 300-person follow-up target.

## 6. Implementation, resources, and schedule

The investigators combine expertise in aphasia treatment, rehabilitation outcomes, growth modeling, genomics, and neuroimaging. Dedicated research assistants will screen admissions daily, coordinate assessments, and maintain post-discharge contact. REDCap supports common case-report forms and central data checks. NIHSS certification, standardized training, and continuous speech-language supervision support consistency across the three sites.

The five-year schedule begins with site approvals, database setup, and training, followed by approximately 30 months of accrual and staggered six-, 12-, and 18-month visits. Therapy extraction and cleaning proceed during follow-up. Growth-model development, hypothesis tests, bootstrap stability assessment, and cross-validation concentrate in the later period, with dissemination continuing as analyses mature. The schedule allows extension or an additional site if accrual or retention is insufficient.

## 7. Ethics and data management

Consent will use supported conversation techniques to ensure that aphasia does not itself prevent understanding. Participation is voluntary and withdrawal will not affect clinical services. Risks include fatigue, frustration, MRI discomfort, and loss of confidentiality. Frequent breaks, split visits, home assessments where appropriate, and trained communication support reduce burden. MRI eligibility screening includes implants and other MRI contraindications, claustrophobia, and pregnancy considerations; only eligible participants undergo research scanning.

Northwestern will serve as the single IRB. A Data Monitoring and Oversight Committee will include clinical, imaging, genetic, epidemiologic, and patient/family perspectives, reviewing readiness initially and safety, burden, progress, and data integrity annually. Ten percent of assessment sessions will be videotaped and rescored, ten percent of case-report forms rechecked, and a separate ten-percent data-entry sample verified. Adverse events will be communicated through site leadership to the co-PIs and institutional oversight according to the stated reporting schedule.

Research records will be separate from medical records and use codes rather than names. Electronic files will be password protected, physical records secured, and access restricted. The de-identified REDCap dataset will be available on request under an agreement prohibiting reidentification and unauthorized redistribution, defining reporting responsibilities, and requiring destruction after analyses. Shared data will include behavioral and patient-reported outcomes, treatment exposure, relevant imaging measures, and candidate-marker information.

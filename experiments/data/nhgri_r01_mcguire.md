# Clinical Integration of Whole Genome Sequencing: A Policy Analysis

**Principal investigators:** David Kaufman and Amy McGuire  
**Mechanism:** NIH/NHGRI R01  
**Research organizations:** Genetics and Public Policy Center, Johns Hopkins University; Baylor College of Medicine; Duke University; Center for Medical Technology Policy

## 1. Project overview

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) is moving from research into clinical diagnosis and management. The project will investigate how emerging commercial and clinical models interact with the laws, regulatory practices, intellectual-property arrangements, and payment systems that shape access to genetic testing. It will produce a systematic description of the WGS industry, a prioritized set of policy questions, and alternative approaches to three questions selected for both importance and tractability.

The research combines a landscape analysis, interviews with up to 20 industry leaders, a modified Delphi process involving approximately 40 stakeholders, and legal and policy analysis. The Delphi process consists of five online surveys and a two-day meeting over approximately 20 months. Its purpose is to clarify options and the reasons for agreement and disagreement. Complete stakeholder consensus is not the intended endpoint.

Three domains organize the detailed policy work: analytical validity and testing quality; patents and licensing; and reimbursement and payment. The project will disseminate findings throughout its three-year duration, including policy briefings in Washington during years two and three. Products will be designed for use by policymakers as well as the research and clinical communities.

## 2. Background and rationale

Falling sequencing costs and clinical applications in Mendelian disease, cancer, and difficult diagnostic cases create opportunities to incorporate genomic information into routine care. At the same time, WGS differs from a conventional test for a single suspected condition. It can generate findings of heterogeneous validity, clinical significance, and utility, and the technologies and interpretation services may be supplied by different organizations. A policy framework built around individual genetic tests may not adequately accommodate these arrangements.

The scope of existing oversight depends on the activities and business models involved. Equipment manufacturing, sequencing, interpretation, and delivery to clinicians or patients may raise different questions under laboratory oversight and other regulatory systems. A clear description of the emerging industry is therefore necessary before identifying gaps or proposing policy changes. Understanding what firms actually provide, the health claims they make, and their relationships with third-party payers will connect policy analysis to clinical implementation.

The study also addresses the interaction between access and incentives. Patenting and licensing can influence the development and provision of genomic services, while reimbursement policies affect whether patients and clinicians can use them. Uncertainty about the evidence needed for coverage is especially relevant when sequencing produces many possible findings rather than one narrowly defined diagnostic result.

The project recognizes other consequential WGS issues, including privacy, ownership and storage of data, workforce needs, and equitable access. It will allow stakeholders to identify such issues when developing the overall priority list. Detailed policy alternatives will concentrate on the three domains for which the collaborating teams bring specific expertise. Important questions outside those domains will remain visible in the published priorities rather than being treated as answered by this study.

The research uses a structured policy Delphi because empirical evidence is limited and the relevant actors hold different knowledge, responsibilities, and interests. Anonymous rounds give participants opportunities to introduce unconventional options and challenge arguments without an open meeting immediately favoring the most influential voices. The final meeting then provides room to explore the complexity that rankings alone cannot represent.

## 3. Objectives and specific aims

**Aim 1: Conduct a systematic landscape analysis of the WGS industry.** Identify organizations developing sequencing technologies, providing sequencing services, and interpreting sequence data; characterize their technologies and business models; and interview industry leaders about likely pathways of clinical integration.

**Aim 2: Identify and prioritize policy questions and develop alternative approaches to selected questions.** Assemble a diverse stakeholder panel, conduct two Delphi surveys to refine and rank questions, choose one high-priority tractable issue in each of three policy domains, and conduct three additional surveys and a stakeholder meeting to examine policy options.

**Aim 3: Disseminate findings to policymakers and other key stakeholders.** Make the landscape, priorities, legal analyses, policy alternatives, and experience with the Delphi method available in accessible formats, with early engagement and opportunities for feedback.

## 4. Preliminary work and basis

The Genetics and Public Policy Center (GPPC) has investigated oversight of genetic testing laboratories, gaps in laboratory quality regulation, the development of a genetic-testing registry, and direct-to-consumer genetic-testing business models. Its prior landscape work provides a model for systematically documenting companies and services. It has also used interviews, surveys, focus groups, town halls, and discrete-choice methods to examine genetic testing and biobank participation.

McGuire's work includes the regulation of direct-to-consumer testing and ethical and policy questions in whole-genome research. Cook-Deegan and colleagues at Duke have examined DNA sequencing technologies, patents, business models, and intellectual-property issues in multi-allele testing. Tunis and Deverka have studied coverage and reimbursement for molecular diagnostics and pharmacogenomics. Messner brings experience conducting qualitative stakeholder research, organizing technical panels, and implementing Delphi processes.

The investigators have experience convening stakeholders who do not share a single institutional perspective. Previous engagement included a national summit with more than 60 participants from regulatory agencies, professional organizations, industry, and genetic-testing laboratories. This experience supports both the logistics of recruitment and the proposed emphasis on articulating disagreement alongside areas of common ground.

## 5. Research design and methods

### 5.1 Aim 1: Industry landscape and interviews

The landscape will cover companies, universities, and other entities developing WGS technologies and equipment, providing whole-genome or exomic sequencing, or offering clinical annotation and interpretation. Internet and PubMed searches will use terms including whole genome sequencing, exomic sequencing, next-generation sequencing, genomics, personalized medicine, individualized medicine, and personal genome. Media searches through NEXIS will supplement these sources. Company websites, publicly available documents, the US Patent and Trademark Office database, and LexisNexis will provide further information. Staff will contact companies when necessary.

For each entity, the team will collect information about technologies and platform capabilities; services such as instruments, sequencing, or interpretation; customer groups; patents and current patent claims; health-related claims; CLIA certification; and third-party coverage. Additional characteristics may be added as the landscape develops. The team will classify business models and refine the scope to focus on organizations relevant to clinical integration. New entrants and changes in services will be monitored throughout the study.

The documentary landscape will be complemented by semistructured telephone interviews with up to 20 chief executives or senior scientific and business officers. Sampling will be purposive, drawing from sequencing and informatics companies identified in the landscape. The investigators anticipate at least 40 eligible organizations with multiple potentially informative senior personnel. Interviews will continue until thematic saturation is reached or 20 interviews have been completed.

Interviews will last approximately 30–40 minutes and address nonproprietary views of business models, development of WGS, and routes into clinical care. Two recording devices will be used. Recordings will be transcribed and de-identified. Staff will use NVivo version 9 for content and thematic analysis, including themes such as emerging models and pathways to clinical integration. The interview findings and documentary evidence will be integrated into a landscape report, which will be supplied to the Delphi panel and disseminated for wider comment.

### 5.2 Aim 2: Stakeholder panel and preparation

Approximately 40 panel members will represent three broad constituencies. Industry participants will include technology companies, informatics companies, trade associations, and clinical laboratories. Healthcare participants will include public and private insurers, pharmacy-benefit managers, health systems, provider groups, professional associations, clinical geneticists, and genetic counselors. Other participants will include regulatory and public-health agencies, congressional or other policy staff, health economists, patient groups, and intellectual-property experts.

Candidates will be identified through academic and trade literature, discussions with industry leaders, and advisory-board recommendations. Within stakeholder categories, the team will rank candidates by relevant expertise and demonstrated policy interest and approach them in that order. The panel size is intended to accommodate the range of relevant groups while remaining administratively manageable; the design does not treat it as a statistically representative population sample.

Recruitment will explain the five surveys, two-day meeting, approximate 20-month commitment, and confidentiality arrangements. Each member will receive a two-letter code for the surveys. An initial group-building exercise will collect and share participants' names, affiliations, expertise, and hopes and concerns about the process so that participants know the relevant experience represented on the panel, while individual survey responses remain coded.

Panel members will receive the Aim 1 landscape analysis. Staff will develop the first surveys in each phase in advance and pilot them with the advisory board. Later instruments will incorporate the preceding responses.

### 5.3 Identifying and ranking policy questions

Staff and the advisory board will use the landscape and existing standards to prepare an initial list of policy questions. In survey 1, panel members will review, add, and comment on questions and rate their importance, validity, and technical and political feasibility on five-point Likert scales. Other useful forms of classification may be added on the basis of the landscape and board advice.

Staff will summarize the responses into an annotated list. In survey 2, panelists will review that list, add comments, and rate the issues again. We will calculate a Priority composite combining importance and validity, with a range of 0–8; a Tractability composite with a stated range of 0–8; and an Overall composite summing all four items, ranging from 0–16. Median values will be calculated for individual and combined scales. Questions will be sorted by Overall and then Priority, and separately by Overall and then Tractability. The annotated, sortable list and its component ratings will be disseminated.

The investigators will consult the advisory board and select the most important tractable issue within each of the three domains: analytical validity/testing quality, patenting/licensing, and reimbursement/payment. The broader priority list will preserve issues falling outside the detailed study scope.

### 5.4 Legal analysis and development of policy alternatives

GPPC, Duke, and the Center for Medical Technology Policy (CMTP) will each examine the relevant federal and state laws and regulations, professional and association guidelines, and standards being adopted by the WGS industry. Analyses will consider the rules' intended purposes; whether they are sufficiently broad and precise for the selected problem; who enforces compliance and how; and conflicts or redundancies among laws and guidelines. The teams will prepare comparable white papers for panel members. Kaufman and McGuire will coordinate the analyses.

Survey 3 will invite open-ended suggestions for addressing the three questions. Panelists will consider the industry landscape, current policy environment, effects on public health, costs to government and stakeholders, and access to sequencing and other services. Staff will independently develop approaches using the policy-analysis framework of Patton and Sawicki.

The combined ideas will become a set of proposed resolutions with supporting arguments. In survey 4, participants will rank each resolution's desirability and feasibility and each argument's importance and validity. They may add resolutions and arguments for or against them. Staff will return mean and median rankings, comments, and suggestions in survey 5, which will gather final rankings and feedback. Identical arguments will be combined in the summaries.

The intended output is a range of policy choices differing in timing, mechanisms, allocation of burdens, federal and state involvement, and stakeholder support. The study will identify where positions converge, where they diverge, and what would require negotiation or compromise.

### 5.5 Final meeting and analysis of the process

The two-day Washington meeting will include the panel and study staff, with Tunis facilitating. Findings will be provided in advance. The first purpose is to determine whether staff have accurately represented preferred options, apparent agreement, and reasons for disagreement. Anonymous real-time voting will display participants' agreement with specific conclusions and provide starting points for discussion. Each issue will include consideration of implications for clinical WGS and for different stakeholder groups.

The second purpose is to evaluate the perceived usefulness of the Delphi method for bringing together stakeholders, integrating perspectives, identifying priorities, and developing alternatives. With consent, the meeting will be recorded and transcribed. De-identified transcripts and voting responses will be analyzed thematically, including views about the method, specific policies, disagreements, and recommendations concerning the use of findings. Minority views will be included in the resulting account.

### 5.6 Aim 3: Engagement and dissemination

The team will contact policymakers and stakeholder organizations early, introduce the study, invite comments, and offer participation in public events. Landscape findings will be released in formats that can be updated and used by wider research and policy communities. Email and social media will support feedback during the project.

Outputs will include white papers on current policy, concise issue briefs, webcasts, electronic landscape materials, a ranked list of questions, policy alternatives, and an account of the Delphi process. Burness Communications will assist with stakeholder identification, communication strategy, events, and archiving. The advisory board will advise on audiences and venues.

A year-two Capitol Hill briefing will present priorities and solicit feedback. A year-three briefing will present policy approaches and the views of different stakeholder groups. Both will be followed by meetings with relevant federal officials and congressional members or staff. Feedback will inform the final report. Public summaries will be posted on the GPPC website.

## 6. Implementation, resources, and schedule

Kaufman and McGuire will jointly lead the project and share responsibility for the landscape. Kaufman will oversee implementation of the Delphi process and dissemination; McGuire will primarily oversee development of policy approaches. The investigators will communicate weekly, and key personnel will participate in regular project meetings to coordinate products across institutions.

An eight-member advisory board, distinct from the 40-person stakeholder panel, will meet five times by video conference. Its expertise encompasses clinical genetics, sequencing technology, regulation, economics, intellectual property, and consumer protection. Meetings will clarify study goals, discuss the landscape, advise on selecting three issues, review developing policy approaches, and guide dissemination and future research.

Year 1 emphasizes landscape research, interviews, integration, and initial engagement. Year 2 emphasizes panel recruitment and preparation, question identification and prioritization, selection of the three issues, initial policy analysis, and the first briefing. Subsequent surveys, the stakeholder meeting, final analyses, and the second briefing extend into year 3.

The advisory board will meet five times during the three-year project.

Because WGS technologies, business models, regulations, and litigation can change during the study, the team will monitor developments and reassess the research strategy. It will prioritize broader questions that cut across particular technologies and maintain relationships with stakeholders to identify changes. The dissemination plan specifically allocates effort to translating academic materials into forms accessible to policy audiences.

## 7. Ethics and data practices

Human participation includes the industry interviews and the Delphi surveys and summit. Recruitment will not exclude people on the basis of sex, race, or ethnicity, although the demographic composition of the professional populations is not sufficiently defined to specify demographic enrollment targets.

Participants may decline questions or withdraw. The consent process will explain the small possibility that commercially or legally sensitive information could be disclosed, including information potentially subject to subpoena. The investigators will request a Certificate of Confidentiality and inform participants about it and its purpose. Interviews and the summit will use verbal consent after advance provision and discussion of study information.

Identifying information will be held securely and separately from numbered interview recordings and transcripts. Electronic survey access will use individual links or passwords; responses will be encrypted and password-protected. The summit will be closed to outside participants and the press. Transcripts will be de-identified before analysis, and comments will be reported in aggregate. The meeting recording will be destroyed after transcription. There is no expected direct benefit beyond ensuring that participants' perspectives inform policy development.

Study findings will be shared through publications, websites, public and policy briefings, and multiple accessible formats. The public products are the landscape, priorities, policy alternatives, and methodological findings; dissemination will respect the confidentiality of individual contributions.

# Using Ethics and User-Centered Design to Create Templates for EHR-Mediated Return of Genetic Test Results

**Principal investigator:** Diane M. Korngiebel  
**Mechanism:** NIH/NHGRI R21  
**Institution:** University of Washington

## 1. Project overview

Patient portals increasingly provide electronic access to clinical test results, including genetic findings that may require careful interpretation and counseling. This project will determine what patients need and prefer when genetic results are returned electronically and will use those findings to develop and test patient-facing report prototypes.

The study combines qualitative bioethics research with user-centered design (UCD). Two interview studies will explore experiences with genetic and non-genetic results and with genetic results of different kinds. A third aim will iteratively test report prototypes with both experienced portal users and people who do not use the portal. An ethical Points to Consider framework will be refined throughout the work.

The setting is UW Medicine and its Epic-based eCare portal. The intended outputs are electronic prototypes, an ethical framework, and evidence about patient preferences and support needs. The prototypes will provide a basis for subsequent clinical and implementation studies.

## 2. Background and rationale

Electronic access requirements and the growth of genomic medicine create a practical question: when, how, and with what support should patients receive genetic results through a portal? Genetic professionals traditionally deliver results with interpretation and counseling, while portal systems can release laboratory results within a few days. A portal may provide convenient access and support follow-up, but its content and timing need to reflect patients' information needs.

Genetic results are heterogeneous. Pharmacogenomic findings may inform medication choices without indicating illness. An inherited cancer-risk result can have substantial consequences for a patient and relatives. A negative result may appear reassuring but can have different implications depending on the test and family history. Treating every genetic result as equally sensitive, or every negative result as simple, could miss these distinctions.

Portal use also depends on technology access, literacy, health literacy, and trust in the clinician–patient relationship. Some groups may need additional support to use results effectively. Designing around existing frequent users alone could overlook needs that influence whether other patients can use the system at all.

The project will investigate a continuum of arrangements: electronic return alone; electronic return followed by a possible conversation with a genetics professional; electronic availability only after such a conversation; and personal return with portal posting serving a supplementary access function. It will ask which results fit which arrangements from patients' perspectives, rather than presume a single mode is appropriate for all results.

UCD places patient input before and throughout prototype development. Early prototypes permit concrete discussion of wording, visual representation, navigation, and next steps before investing in deployment. They also communicate findings to health-information-technology professionals more directly than a list of general recommendations alone.

## 3. Objectives and specific aims

**Aim 1:** Interview up to 40 eCare users who have received both genetic and non-genetic results through the portal. Explore comparisons between these experiences, thresholds for electronic return, and preferences for accessible and useful information.

**Aim 2:** Interview up to 40 different patients who received genetic results through eCare: ten with pharmacogenomic findings, ten with hereditary hemochromatosis findings, ten with inherited colorectal-cancer-risk findings, and ten with negative results. Explore concern thresholds, information needs, counseling needs, and acceptable delivery arrangements.

**Aim 3:** Conduct cognitive usability interviews with 42 eCare users and 42 non-users across three rounds. Iteratively evaluate and improve electronic report prototypes informed by Aims 1 and 2, including Epic-style and more unconstrained blue-sky designs.

## 4. Preliminary work and basis

Korngiebel's prior work asked medical geneticists, counselors, oncologists, gastroenterologists, pathologists, and primary-care clinicians about returning Lynch-syndrome screening results through a portal. Most preferred personal return for sensitive results, but they did not agree on which results should count as sensitive. This supports examining patient-defined thresholds instead of importing a settled clinician classification.

The team also draws on Clinical Sequencing Exploratory Research work developing categories of genetic conditions relevant to reproductive planning. Patients responded to attributes such as controllability, predictability, visibility, severity, and adult onset. These findings provide initial categories for Aim 2, which will be revised in light of the new data.

The investigative team includes expertise in qualitative inquiry, UCD, bioethics, human genetics, and genetic counseling. Korngiebel has training in formative research, electronic prototyping, and usability testing. Lynne Robins contributes extensive qualitative-methods experience; Malia Fullerton contributes genomic bioethics; and Laura Amendola contributes genetic counseling and clinical-sequencing experience. Clinical, laboratory, informatics, and external prototype advisors will support recruitment and scientific accuracy.

## 5. Research design and methods

### 5.1 Ethical framework and design sequence

The initial Points to Consider framework covers three domains. Beneficence and nonmaleficence address worry, family implications, potential benefits and harms of the mode and presentation, and effects on the clinician–patient relationship. Respect for autonomy addresses preferred delivery, informed decisions, cultural sensitivity, and family communication. Justice and equity address limited electronic access, technology and health literacy, additional support needs, and use of limited resources.

**Table 1. Initial Points to Consider framework**

| Principle | Questions |
|---|---|
| Beneficence and nonmaleficence | Are results likely to cause worry? What are the implications for family members? Which mode, content, or presentation benefits or harms the patient? Does the mode support the clinician-patient relationship? |
| Respect for autonomy | What mode does the patient prefer? Does the mode and content support informed decisions? Is return culturally sensitive? Does it support appropriate family communication? |
| Justice and equity | Can low technology literacy or limited access be accommodated? Can results address different health-literacy levels? Is additional support needed to avoid inequity? How should limited resources be considered? |

The framework will change as patient findings accumulate. The UCD sequence is to investigate needs in Aims 1 and 2, generate content and visual ideas, develop prototypes, test them, and revise them across three rounds. Ethical analysis and usability findings will inform one another throughout this process.

Recruitment will use UW's clinical and translational informatics services, the eCare team, and relevant clinical departments. The exploratory study will recruit English speakers because eCare is available in English at UW Medicine. Later work is intended to develop reports with and for non-English-speaking patients.

### 5.3 Aim 1: Comparing genetic and non-genetic results

Participants must have received at least one genetic and one non-genetic result through eCare. They must have been UW Medicine patients for at least 24 months, had a clinical visit within the preceding 12 months, and used at least one eCare function in the preceding 12 months. Potential participants will be contacted by email, telephone, or postal mail according to preference.

Semistructured interviews will take place in person or by telephone. Questions will examine experience receiving each type of result, portal use, perceived thresholds for electronic return, and changes that would make information more usable and accessible. The study team will develop the protocol, seek advisory review, and pilot it before collection. Individual interviews permit confidential discussion and follow-up on unanticipated issues.

Recruitment will continue until diversity targets are met and the team judges that major themes have reached idea saturation, up to the planned 40 participants. Interviews will be recorded, transcribed, de-identified, and checked for accuracy.

Analysis will combine prior coding categories derived from work on result thresholds with new themes emerging from the interviews. Korngiebel and Robins will lead directed content and thematic analysis. Two coders will code all transcripts, periodically clarify coding, and resolve interpretive disagreement by consensus. Atlas.ti will support transcript organization, coding, and codebook development. The study team and advisory board will review results, and the ethical framework will be refined accordingly.

### 5.4 Aim 2: Experiences with different genetic findings

The four result categories provide variation in concern and complexity. Pharmacogenomic results are an initial example of lower concern because they can support medication choices in people otherwise consistent with normal health. Hemochromatosis represents a condition often emerging in middle age with an established treatment, regular phlebotomy, while also creating implications for relatives. Inherited colorectal-cancer risk represents substantial possible consequences for patients and families. Negative findings are included because their interpretation may depend on personal and family history and can be misunderstood.

These categories are starting points, not fixed classifications of how every patient should react. Aim 2 will test and refine them through participants' accounts.

**Table 3. Initial result categories for Aim 2**

| Category | Rationale |
|---|---|
| Mild: pharmacogenomics and negative results | Pharmacogenomic findings can improve medication selection or targeted therapy; negative findings may be favorable but nuanced. |
| Moderate: hemochromatosis | Usually begins in middle age; treatable with a regular, established, low-risk intervention. |
| Serious: inherited colorectal-cancer risk | Multiple factors affect the patient and family, with potential physical and psychosocial consequences. |

Participants must have received the relevant results within the preceding 12 months. UW eCare, Laboratory Medicine, and Medical Genetics will identify eligible patients retrospectively. Ten participants will be recruited in each category, using the demographic strata above. Aim 2 participants will not overlap with Aim 1.

Hour-long interviews, in person or by telephone according to preference, will ask about portal use, experience receiving the result, reasons electronic return was appropriate or inappropriate, and circumstances in which returning results without accompanying personal counseling would be acceptable. Protocols will be piloted and informed by Aim 1. Recordings, transcription, de-identification, and checking will follow the same procedures.

Analysis will use the Aim 1 coding and thematic methods to identify the elements that determine concern thresholds and support needs. Results from both aims will further refine the ethical framework and directly inform prototype content, presentation, and interaction design.

### 5.5 Aim 3: Prototype development

The team will create approximately six to eight prototypes, providing Epic-style and blue-sky exemplars across the selected result categories. The Epic-style versions address a widely used platform and practical compatibility. The blue-sky versions permit alternative arrangements and adaptation to other EHR settings. Content will include result interpretation, visual elements as appropriate, family-communication resources, and links to reliable information such as Cancer.net.

Prototypes will represent negative findings and results that patients defined as less, more, and very concerning. The design will allow the findings to indicate that particular results should be delivered personally and only supplemented through the portal.

### 5.6 Aim 3: Three iterative usability rounds

Each round will recruit 28 new participants, producing 84 participants overall, equally divided between eCare users and non-users. Each person will examine three or four prototypes. Each prototype will receive the same number of reviews, half from users and half from non-users. No participant will return in a later round, and presentation order will vary to counterbalance learning about prototype functions and information organization.

Non-users are included for two reasons: they provide a novice perspective on usability and have not been primed by portal experience for the way genetic results are presented. This comparison will help explore whether electronic return can substitute for personal delivery in some settings or should serve a supplementary function. The sample-size rationale draws on usability problem-discovery studies; we estimate a minimum discovery rate above 98%, with a mean of 99.6%, for each participant type under its proposed approach.

In-person cognitive interviews will use a laptop and interactive prototypes. Participants will think aloud while completing tasks, such as identifying recommended next steps from a report. The assessment will address the experience users expect, desire, and value as well as whether they can perform tasks. The initial protocol will be piloted, with further piloting if later revisions substantially change it.

Korngiebel will analyze each round using UCD usability methods, including guidance for quantitative analysis of small samples, and identify redesign priorities. The team will review proposed changes for accuracy and resolve conflicting feedback. Korngiebel and a graduate assistant will revise the prototypes before the next round.

After round 2 and its revisions, external consultants will conduct heuristic walkthroughs. Their recommendations will be considered before round 3. The ethical framework will be updated in light of the usability findings. The three rounds therefore test successive design iterations rather than expose all participants to an unchanged product.

## 6. Implementation, resources, and schedule

The study will use UW Medicine's eCare and informatics infrastructure, clinical genetics and laboratory expertise, and the Harborview Family Medicine Clinic. The advisory board will meet twice yearly and be consulted as needed. Outside experts from settings with high and low portal use will assess generalizability. There are ten advisors across the board, prototype consultants, and mentoring group, eight of whom are clinicians.

The two-year schedule begins with Aim 1 collection and analysis, followed by Aim 2. Prototype creation and round 1 testing follow the generative work. Rounds 2 and 3, with revision and external review between them, occupy the later phase.

| Activity | Project quarters |
|---|---|
| Aim 1 interviews: genetic versus non-genetic return | Q1-Q2 |
| Aim 1 analysis | Q2-Q3 |
| Aim 2 interviews: genetic results received electronically | Q3-Q4 |
| Aim 2 analysis | Q4-Q5 |
| Aim 3 prototype creation and round 1 testing | Q5-Q6 |
| Revision, round 2 testing, consultant review | Q6-Q7 |
| Revision and round 3 testing | Q7-Q8 |

If underrepresented groups are disproportionately non-users, recruitment will prioritize these groups through informatics searches, direct clinic approaches, and same-day data collection. That pattern would also identify questions about equitable access for future study. Epic staff will assess compatibility of Epic-style reports; external consultants will review both design types.

Deliverables will include downloadable prototypes, the Points to Consider framework, and publications describing how patients use and wish to use portals for genomic information. Subsequent work may involve clinician and technical co-design, multilingual report sets, implementation in multiple settings, and studies of patient engagement and health outcomes. Those later evaluations are distinct from the present formative work.

## 7. Ethics and data practices

The project will seek a HIPAA waiver for access to information used in identifying eligible patients and an IRB waiver of written consent documentation to reduce unnecessary identifying links. Recruitment materials and consent discussions will explain procedures, confidentiality, voluntary participation, risks, and the absence of direct clinical benefit.

Potential risks include discomfort discussing inherited risk, concern about audio recording, recognition by others during waiting-room recruitment, and inadvertent disclosure of information about relatives. Participants may stop at any time. They will be reminded not to provide identifying information about third parties; such details will be removed from recordings and transcripts if supplied. Participants experiencing distress will be encouraged to discontinue and directed to assistance if needed.

Transcripts will be de-identified. Information needed for incentive administration will be stored separately. Study data will be kept on secure servers or password-protected computers, with physical materials locked and access restricted to the team. Data will be destroyed at study close. No incidental clinical findings are expected from this interview and prototype study, and no children will be enrolled.

# Multimodal Vestibular Perception

Principal investigator: Benjamin T. Crane. Mechanism: R01.

## 1. Project overview

This project will establish methods for studying how humans combine visual and inertial information to perceive movement, determine whether the two signals have a common cause, and adapt when the signals remain systematically inconsistent. Experiments will examine healthy participants and people with unilateral vestibular loss. They will distinguish the direction perceived in each sensory modality from the judgment that the two modalities describe the same movement. The work will also determine whether persistent heading errors after vestibular damage can be reduced through repeated perceptual adaptation.

The project has basic mechanistic goals and will provide methods and evidence for subsequent rehabilitation trials. Inertial perception includes vestibular, somatosensory, and proprioceptive contributions.

## 2. Background and rationale

Dizziness, vertigo, motion sickness, and simulator sickness concern the perception of movement. Vestibular evaluation has nevertheless emphasized reflexes, particularly the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) and vestibular-evoked myopotentials (VEMPs). These measures do not fully explain perceptual symptoms. Similarly, experiments on isolated vestibular thresholds do not reproduce the multisensory circumstances of everyday movement. A clinically useful account must explain how visual and inertial information are interpreted together and when visual motion is attributed to movement of the external environment.

Visual motion is ambiguous: the observer may be moving through a stationary world, the environment may be moving around a stationary observer, or both may move. Inertial cues provide additional information but are limited during constant-velocity translation. Causal inference therefore precedes, or interacts with, the weighting of sensory signals. Previous visual-inertial studies have often examined small discrepancies near straight ahead, asked for only a single combined estimate, or measured one modality without independently measuring the other. Such designs can miss a difference between explicit common-cause judgments and continued cross-modal influence.

Reliability-based integration models offer testable predictions, but individual strategies and absolute heading direction may matter. Preliminary observations suggest that visual motion can influence an inertial estimate even when participants explicitly report that the stimuli do not share a common cause. The project will test this possibility across the entire horizontal plane. Timing, duration, acceleration, visual-field size, and vestibular pathology provide systematic ways to alter the evidence for common causation.

Adaptation is established in the VOR, but perceptual asymmetries may persist after reflex recovery. Heading errors following unilateral loss may reflect otolith asymmetry, compensation for prolonged rotatory vertigo, or both. Understanding the development and persistence of those errors will guide the design of a perceptual intervention. Two candidate adaptation methods are sustained offsets between visual and inertial headings and exposure to a rotating virtual environment.

## 3. Objectives and specific aims

1. **Define causal inference for visual and inertial headings.** Establish reproducible measurements of perceived unity and separate heading estimates; determine effects of timing, motion profile, duration, and visual-field size; and compare healthy participants with people who have unilateral vestibular lesions. The central prediction is that cross-modal influence spans a larger discrepancy range than explicit common-cause perception.
2. **Determine how heading perception adapts.** Test systematically offset visual-inertial translations and rotating virtual environments. Measure changes in visual heading, inertial heading, rotational perception, and common-cause judgments. Determine whether adaptation generalizes beyond the heading range used in training.
3. **Characterize and modify heading errors following vestibular loss.** Follow acute unilateral lesions to establish the time course and possible origin of heading bias, then test whether either adaptation method produces durable correction in chronic unilateral hypofunction.

## 4. Preliminary work and basis

The laboratory has developed synchronized visual and inertial stimulation, forced-choice psychophysics, and continuous heading-report methods. Prior work found that visual headings are represented in retinal coordinates whereas inertial headings are represented in body coordinates. Combined estimates approximately reflected relative reliability but placed more weight on inertial information than predicted, including after vibration reduced its reliability. Previous vection experiments also showed that not every participant treated visual self-motion as equivalent to inertial movement, motivating direct heading estimates.

In four preliminary participants, each combination of 12 visual and 12 inertial directions was presented repeatedly. With zero offset, agreement judgments were about 95% at cardinal directions but about 55% at other directions; agreement fell below 4% at offsets of at least 90 degrees. Inertial estimates were nevertheless biased toward the visual heading at offsets of 30-120 degrees in every participant. The effect was especially strong for noncardinal inertial directions, whereas visual headings were minimally shifted by inertial direction. These pilot observations motivate the distinction between unity and influence.

Adaptation pilots demonstrate feasibility with both proposed methods. One participant required a 16.2-degree rightward heading to perceive straight ahead after exposure to visual headings offset leftward; a rotating virtual environment produced a 12-degree shift. These are distinct illustrative and planned conditions.

Five participants with chronic unilateral vestibular hypofunction had a mean heading deviation of 7.5 ± 3.4 degrees in preliminary testing. Visual headings were not systematically displaced. Five chronic participants undergoing two or three rotational-adaptation sessions showed strong first-session correction, with some retention a week later in four of five. Their baseline Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI) averaged 23 ± 14; mean change after adaptation was -0.8, ranging from -4 to 2. These small pilots support feasibility and sample-size assumptions but do not establish long-term therapeutic benefit.

## 5. Research design and methods

### Shared population, apparatus, and measurements

We anticipate approximately 120 normal controls and 30 participants with vestibulopathy over five years, with participants reused across experiments when appropriate. Existing resources include a screened institutional database, recurring healthy participants, and patients with unilateral lesions from otolaryngology clinics. Eligible ages are 13–89; recruitment is expected to yield primarily adults aged 18–89. Medical history, medication use, migraine and dizziness history, visual acuity, audiometry, and vestibular tests will inform eligibility and characterization. Age and sex will be considered in analysis.

A six-degree-of-freedom hexapod motion platform will deliver controlled translations and rotations synchronized with a 98-degree azimuth visual display. A second platform facilitates clinical access and parallel configurations. Virtual-reality systems provide head tracking and binocular disparity; a 110-degree headset is available. A custom multi-axis sled/rotator offers greater translation and unlimited yaw range for conditions limited by the platform. Motion tracking, video eye tracking, accelerometers, and rate gyros characterize stimulus delivery and participant movement.

Before testing, participants receive standardized explanations and large, unambiguous practice stimuli to establish comfort and comprehension. Experimental trials provide no correctness feedback. Forced-choice tasks use interleaved staircases when feasible to reduce predictable stimulus sequences. For continuous judgments, participants rotate a mechanical dial toward the perceived heading. Visual and inertial estimates are obtained in separate trial blocks because reporting both in the same block proved confusing. Unity judgments can accompany the selected directional report.

Forced-choice responses will be fitted with cumulative Gaussian functions using Monte Carlo maximum-likelihood procedures. The fitted mean estimates the point of subjective equality and hence bias; spread estimates precision or threshold. Resampling provides confidence intervals and condition comparisons, including individual-level inference. The strategy describes p ≤ .01 as a conservative criterion for this established procedure. Continuous estimates will be analyzed with methods including ANOVA and t tests; repeating each block at least three times permits assessment of precision. Sample-size calculations for individual experiments generally use 80% power and a 5% type-I error rate.

### Aim I: causal inference

**Experiment IA** will present a 15-cm translation over two seconds. Visual and inertial directions will each take 12 values across 360 degrees in 30-degree increments, yielding 144 combinations per block. Participants report whether the movements are the same and estimate either the visual or inertial direction. Each report-modality block is repeated three times, producing six unity judgments and three estimates of each modality for every combination. Approximately 20 participants are planned; an assumed 10-degree bias with a 15-degree standard deviation requires 18 under the stated power assumptions.

The outcomes are the discrepancy range producing unity and the discrepancy range producing attraction of one heading toward the other. Absolute direction and individual differences will be examined. If 30-degree sampling misses narrow tuning or encourages expectations about agreement, visual headings close to each inertial heading will be sampled more densely while preserving full-circle coverage.

**Experiment IB** will vary stimulus compatibility using IA as the reference condition. Initial temporal offsets will increase in 50-ms steps through 200 ms, testing either modality first. Duration and acceleration will also vary. Visual fields of 98, 45, and 20 degrees are planned; wider fields, potentially up to 200 degrees, are contingent on equipment availability and results. Both unity judgments and perceived separation will be measured. A 10-degree mean change with a 10-degree standard deviation implies eight participants; approximately ten are planned. Order variation and newly recruited naive participants will help distinguish stimulus effects from adaptation to repeated exposure.

**Experiment IC** will apply corresponding paradigms to chronic unilateral lesions, including patients after labyrinthectomy or tumor removal and other clinically characterized etiologies. Reduced vestibular reliability is predicted to increase visual influence and broaden common-cause judgments. Approximately ten participants are expected to demonstrate differences relative to controls, although clinical variability and slower recruitment are acknowledged. Results will be related to lesion etiology, recovery status, video head-impulse testing, and DHI. Chronic cases reduce ambiguity about recent compensation, but slow-growing lesions may still make the effective onset uncertain.

### Aim II: perceptual adaptation

**Experiment IIA** will expose participants to systematically offset visual-inertial headings while they make a left/right judgment to maintain attention. An illustrative offset is 20 degrees. The planned adaptation sequence contains 80 presentations over approximately 20-25 minutes. Baseline and post-adaptation tests will measure visual and inertial heading separately, with direction of adaptation and training range varied. Forced-choice testing near straight ahead is primary; continuous estimates in selected experiments will establish generalization across the horizontal plane. Common-cause judgments will also be reassessed.

Fifteen normal participants are planned, based on a conservative expected shift of 8 ± 10 degrees, for which 13 would meet the stated power criterion. Repeated participation will permit comparisons across sessions. The design explicitly tests the alternative that learning remains confined to trained headings. If necessary, the training range can be broadened rather than assuming global recalibration.

**Experiment IIB** will use a rotating virtual star field with reduced coherence, achieved by regenerating stars at random positions, to limit reliance on stable landmarks. Searching for objects encourages attention and voluntary head movement. Preliminary exposure at 40 degrees per second became subjectively unobtrusive after one or two minutes and produced robust inertial adaptation; the illustrated pilot lasted ten minutes. The study will assess visual and inertial heading, generalization, and rotational thresholds or bias before and after exposure. Approximately 15 participants are planned, overlapping with IIA when feasible. Motion sickness, movement amount, and attention may influence adaptation; minimum movement requirements or adjusted stimulus parameters can standardize exposure.

### Aim III: vestibular loss and durable correction

**Experiment IIIA** will recruit recent unilateral lesions, including surgery, vestibular neuritis, and recent gentamicin treatment for Meniere's disease. Because neuritis and gentamicin may produce incomplete lesions, etiologies will be analyzed separately. Heading, yaw-rotation, and sway-translation bias and thresholds will be assessed weekly during the first month, monthly for six months, and at nine months and one year. Routine calorics, VEMPs, video head-impulse testing, and DHI provide complementary clinical measures.

An acute intact-side rotational percept can initially dominate the heading judgment. The longitudinal comparison will test whether later lesion-side heading errors accompany sway asymmetry, as expected for an otolith contribution, or follow rotational compensation with relatively little sway asymmetry. We estimate a required sample of 16 from a 7.5-degree difference and standard deviations of about seven degrees. It anticipates substantial attrition, approximately 50%, because serial visits may be difficult for patients travelling long distances.


**Figure 4.** Heading deviations after acute and chronic unilateral vestibular loss and proposed mechanisms.

**Experiment IIIB** will study lesions present for at least one year. Each visit measures visual and inertial heading, rotation, sway, and DHI before adaptation. The two adaptation methods will be tested separately in the same individuals; early enrollees receive the rotation method first and later enrollees the heading-offset method first. Adaptation always targets the direction that reduces the participant's baseline bias. Weekly sessions will track retention before the next exposure. Further adaptation stops when bias disappears or reverses, but measurement continues to assess stability.

The primary questions are durable correction and tolerability. Visual changes will be monitored even though inertial bias is the intended target. A change from ten to two degrees with standard deviations of eight degrees yields a stated sample requirement of eight; a total of 20 or more is sought to examine etiologies separately. Larger baseline deviations, particularly above ten degrees, are preferred. Session number and parameters can be adjusted for tolerance. DHI monitoring guards against worsening symptoms, and reducing exposure duration or rotational speed is a proposed response to discomfort. A subsequent randomized clinical trial would be needed to test functional benefit.

## 6. Implementation, resources, and schedule

The investigator will oversee the experiments and a full-time technician will support recruitment and testing. Clinical access and existing equipment permit the mechanistic and lesion studies to proceed in parallel. Year 1 establishes normal causal-inference measurements, begins offset adaptation, and initiates acute follow-up and chronic-lesion adaptation. Year 2 extends causal inference to timing and lesion groups and begins rotational adaptation. Year 3 investigates stimulus parameters and continues patient adaptation. Year 4 emphasizes duration and rotational parameters, completes key lesion experiments, and refines procedures. Year 5 integrates causal-inference results into a model and completes analysis and dissemination.

| Year | Planned work |
|---|---|
| 1 | Develop causal-inference methods in normal participants (IA); begin heading-offset adaptation (IIA), acute unilateral-loss follow-up (IIIA), and adaptation in chronic unilateral loss (IIIB). |
| 2 | Extend causal-inference testing to timing (IB) and unilateral lesions (IC); test rotation-offset adaptation (IIB); analyze and publish initial experiments. |
| 3 | Test stimulus-parameter effects on causal inference and heading-offset adaptation (IB, IIA); continue lesion studies and adaptation. |
| 4 | Test stimulus duration and visual effects on causal inference and stimulus-parameter effects on rotation adaptation; complete and analyze unilateral heading-adaptation work. |
| 5 | Integrate causal-inference effects into a unified model; complete analysis and publication across aims and follow findings that motivate further experiments. |

## 7. Ethics and data management

Testing is noninvasive. The principal foreseeable risks are motion sickness, worsening dizziness, and loss of privacy. Participants give informed consent and may discontinue at any time; adolescents, if enrolled, require parental consent and an age-appropriate assent process. DHI is administered during adaptation visits. Adverse events will be documented with severity and relationship to participation and reviewed through institutional oversight. Research participation will not prevent other clinical care.

Identifying information will be maintained separately from research measurements, with access limited to designated study personnel. Dissemination will use scientific publications and presentations, with trial registration planned before data collection.

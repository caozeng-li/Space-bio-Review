# Demographic Patterns of Eugenic Sterilization in California

**Principal investigator:** Alexandra Minna Stern  
**Collaborators:** Sharon Kardia and Siobán Harlow  
**Mechanism:** NIH/NHGRI R21  
**Institution:** University of Michigan

## 1. Project overview

This project will combine epidemiologic and historical analysis to investigate patterns and experiences of eugenic sterilization in California. It will use approximately 15,000 sterilization recommendation records from nine state institutions during 1921–1952, link them with census and institutional population data, and examine accompanying correspondence and forms.

The research will ask who was recommended for sterilization, which groups were disproportionately affected, how patterns varied among institutions and across time, and how patients and families responded. Quantitative analysis will investigate associations with age, gender, ethnicity, nationality, diagnosis, and institutional context. Qualitative analysis will examine changing classifications, consent practices, objections, and resistance.

The two-year study is the analytical phase of a larger effort that has already digitized historical records and developed a REDCap database. Findings will also inform a companion digital archive being developed separately with digital-humanities collaborators. The intended contribution is a population-based and contextualized account of reproductive control, with implications for contemporary ethical, legal, and social questions in genetics and genomics.

## 2. Background and rationale

California performed approximately 20,000 of the roughly 60,000 documented eugenic sterilizations in the United States during the twentieth century. Its 1909 sterilization law was the third such state law, and most California procedures occurred between the 1920s and 1950s. Across the country, state laws authorized reproductive control of institutionalized people categorized as having undesirable heredity, intellectual disability, mental illness, or socially disapproved behavior.

The broad history is well known, but detailed empirical accounts of the populations affected remain limited. Descriptive statistics and historical narratives from other jurisdictions have identified important patterns. California's extensive surviving records provide an opportunity to examine demographic associations more systematically and to connect them with institutional practice and individual experience.

The study will investigate whether Spanish-surnamed people, teenagers, women, immigrants, and other groups had different risks of institutionalization or sterilization recommendation. It will distinguish patterns within institutions from comparisons with the broader populations from which residents were drawn. It will also assess whether associations differed by institution, superintendent, diagnosis, family context, or period.

Consent is a central historical question. Although California law did not require consent for sterilization during much of this period, institutional officials sought signatures from parents, spouses, or guardians. Sterilization could be a condition of release. These arrangements create a setting in which a signature, acquiescence, refusal, and meaningful choice may differ. The research will examine how these practices operated and how patients and families contested them.

Historical analysis may illuminate assumptions about disability, reproductive worth, and institutional vulnerability that remain relevant when considering genetic and reproductive technologies. The project seeks evidence about the decisions, categories, and institutional routines through which coercive practices were carried out.

## 3. Objectives and specific aims

**Aim 1:** Conduct extensive quantitative analysis of the de-identified database, which contains 212 discrete variables for each of approximately 15,000 records. Describe patterns by demographics, diagnosis, institution, superintendent, family structure, and related contextual characteristics.

**Aim 2:** Link the recommendation data with individual-level census microdata and tract-level census reports to estimate population-based rates and test associations with sterilization risk. Examine hypotheses that Spanish-surnamed patients and young teenagers were disproportionately recommended for sterilization and assess variation within and across institutions.

**Aim 3:** Analyze the qualitative content of recommendation forms, letters, charts, and supplemental documents. Trace terminology and diagnostic changes, examine patient and family resistance, and assess the meaning and limits of consent in institutional settings.

**Aim 4:** Incorporate suitable findings, visualizations, historical interpretation, and patient narratives into a companion digital archive under parallel development, subject to confidentiality requirements.

## 4. Preliminary work and basis

Stern located microfilm reels containing recommendations, supplementary letters and forms, and sterilization rosters from nine California institutions. Following approval from the California Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects and the University of Michigan biomedical IRB, the team digitized the records and developed a de-identified, HIPAA-compliant REDCap dataset. The instrument includes 212 variables covering demographic characteristics, diagnosis, family circumstances, and institutional allegations about criminality or sexual behavior.

Data entry for all approximately 15,000 records is planned for completion before the main analytical phase. Pilot studies have already identified family resistance among Mexican-origin patients and suggested disproportionate representation of Spanish-surnamed patients in records from three institutions.

### Pacific Colony pilot analysis

A pilot analysis uses 1,003 sterilization recommendations from Pacific Colony during 1935–1944 and the institution's 1,068 residents enumerated in the 1940 census. The recommendation files contain historical institutional classifications based on assessed IQ. Among the recommendations, 571 people, or 56.9%, were assigned the historical label “Moron,” associated in the accompanying text with IQ 51–70. Institutional reports classified approximately 35% of all residents in that category. The comparison motivates examination of whether people in particular institutional diagnostic categories were preferentially selected.

Among people recommended for sterilization, 33.8% of those with Spanish surnames were labeled “sexually delinquent,” compared with 22.0% of those without Spanish surnames. The corresponding proportions recorded for petty crime were 15.0% and 7.5%; no individuals in this recommendation subset were flagged for violent crime. These are the institutions' recorded judgments and classifications, which the project will analyze historically.

## 5. Research design and methods

### 5.1 Source population and historical data

The principal dataset covers approximately 15,000 people recommended for eugenic sterilization in nine California state institutions during 1921–1952. Its coverage represents a large fraction of California's recorded procedures and approximately one quarter of the documented national total. The study will analyze recommendation records and associated authorizations, rosters, and correspondence while preserving their historical context.

The records alone do not contain comparable annual information for all institutional residents who were not recommended for sterilization or for the surrounding population. The proposed census work addresses this by reconstructing institutional population profiles and relevant catchment populations. The team anticipates census information on more than 100,000 residents of sterilizing institutions over the corresponding period.

Data availability varies by source and year. The recommendation data contain individual-level demographic and clinical information, with the available variables changing between 1921–1934 and 1935–1952. Census microdata for 1920, 1930, and 1940 provide individual-level demographic information; 1950 census reports provide tract-level information. Annual and biennial institutional reports provide additional population characteristics. The data availability table will guide construction of identical or commensurate variables.

### 5.2 Aim 1: Descriptive analysis

Using Stata 13, the investigators will tabulate the number of recommendations by year and institution and describe the people recommended according to gender, age, ethnicity, nationality, and diagnosis. The broader set of coded variables will permit investigation of contextual factors such as family structure, institution, superintendent, and changing categories used to justify the recommendation.

Descriptive work will identify temporal patterns and contrasts among institutions to inform subsequent modeling and selection of qualitative themes. It will also provide the numerical basis for visualizations and historical interpretation in the companion archive.

### 5.3 Aim 2: Population denominators and risk comparisons

The team will construct a companion dataset from the 1920, 1930, and 1940 census microdata available through the University of Michigan's Institute for Social Research. It will identify residents of the nine institutions and assemble gender, age, nativity, race, ethnicity, and other comparable demographic variables. Tract-level 1950 reports and institutional annual or biennial reports will supplement the historical population estimates.

The recommendation records will be combined with these population data to estimate rates with residents recommended for sterilization as the numerator and total institutional residents as the denominator. We propose a case-cohort design for comparing risks across institutions, demographic groups, and time. Poisson regression will test whether groups including Spanish-surnamed people, females, those under age 18, and immigrants had higher risks than their comparison groups.

The study will also compare institutional cross-tabulations with statewide and county census aggregates approximating hospital catchment areas. This will permit investigation of disproportionate institutionalization as well as disproportionate selection within institutions. The investigators hypothesize that Spanish-surnamed and foreign-born people and teenagers were disproportionately institutionalized, that some counties supplied unusually large numbers of residents, and that demographic associations with sterilization varied among institutions.

### 5.4 Identifying Spanish surnames

The same systematic approach will be used in recommendation and census records. The team will draw on standardized Spanish-surname lists developed by the US Census Bureau and manually review surnames for misspellings introduced by enumerators or institutional staff. This procedure will support comparisons using a consistent indicator across sources whose racial and ethnic classifications differ.

The historical data differ in their classifications. For example, the 1920 census includes information related to Mexican birthplace, the 1930 census uses a Mexican racial category, and selected 1950 tracts provide Spanish-surname cross-tabulations. The analysis will construct comparable variables from these sources rather than assume their original categories are interchangeable.

### 5.5 Aim 3: Qualitative and historical analysis

The qualitative corpus includes forms, interdepartmental correspondence, patient communications, and other documents associated with the files. REDCap-captured data will support coding of diagnoses and terminology over time, including changes in the descriptions of patients who fit institutional categories.

Letters of protest and non-consent will be manually coded to identify themes. The team will analyze all cases in which a patient, parent, next of kin, or guardian refused consent. Preliminary work suggests religious objections, family concerns, and disagreement with diagnoses as recurring reasons. Approximately 50 handwritten letters from patients at Patton, a psychiatric hospital, contain objections to their own sterilization and appeals to officials in Sacramento.

These documents will be situated in the histories of eugenics, reproduction, and institutionalization. Analysis will examine how consent was requested, how refusals were handled, and how release conditions constrained choice. It will also trace institutional routines and changes in the language used to legitimize procedures.

The qualitative work is intended to complement population patterns with accounts of individual experience and resistance. It will preserve the distinction between institutional claims about a person and that person's own responses. Numerical associations and close reading of the documents will jointly support interpretation, rather than reduce all records to demographic counts.

### 5.6 Aim 4: Companion archive and dissemination

Stern and digital-humanities collaborators are developing a companion archive using Omeka. The existing prototype provides a starting point for interactive graphs, charts, historical explanations, and narratives. The analytical findings from this study will inform that archive, while its further development is a parallel project for separate humanities support.

Suitable visualizations and narratives will communicate the relation between demographic patterns and patient experience to researchers and the public. The archive and all other dissemination will follow IRB and confidentiality requirements; individual identification will not be disclosed through the research outputs.

The team will present findings to public-health, history, ethics, and ELSI audiences through conferences, lectures, posters, and peer-reviewed publications. The work will make the quantitative evidence and interpretive approach available across disciplines studying the history and implications of human genetics.

## 6. Implementation, resources, and schedule

Stern contributes historical expertise; Kardia and Harlow contribute epidemiologic and quantitative expertise. The project uses the existing data-entry and secure-computing environment at the University of Michigan School of Public Health and the team's experience constructing the REDCap instrument.

The 24-month analytical plan has overlapping phases. Year 1 will create the census companion dataset for all nine institutions, produce descriptive statistics, begin population-based analyses and hypothesis tests, and initiate qualitative coding. Year 2 will expand hypothesis testing, examine differences within and across institutions, develop analysis of refusals and consent, and prepare publications and public-facing materials. Dissemination begins while analysis is ongoing.

## 7. Ethics and data practices

This is retrospective research on historical records, without direct contact with the people represented. The files include adults and children, with recorded ages spanning approximately 7–58, and people with intellectual or psychiatric diagnoses. Source files contain identifiable information, but the analytical dataset will be de-identified.

Team members must be included on the IRB protocol and complete research-responsibility, confidentiality, and REDCap training before access. Digitized authorizations will be accessed through a password-protected server. Data will be extracted into HIPAA-compliant REDCap instruments. The identifier used during data entry will not be included in exported data or reports.

Digitized source copies will also be protected on a password-protected drive stored in a locked safe. Coded data will remain on protected computers; identifiable source information will not be distributed outside the study or published. Any transport of records will use a secure carrier.

The resource-sharing plan provides for release of suitable data after acceptance of the principal findings, with identifiers removed and disclosure risk minimized. Requests and distributions will be coordinated and documented by the project manager, using institutional data-use agreements as appropriate. The plan's commitment to sharing is subject to the privacy restrictions on the historical records.

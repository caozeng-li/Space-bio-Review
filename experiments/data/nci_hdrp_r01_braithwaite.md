# Personalized Screening for Lung Cancer: Importance of Co-existing Chronic Conditions to Clinical Practice and Policy

**Principal investigator:** Dejana K. Braithwaite; multiple-investigator collaboration with Michael Gould and Gerard Silvestri.  
**Application mechanism:** R01.

## 1. Project overview

We propose to determine how multimorbidity changes the benefits and harms of low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) lung cancer screening (LCS) in ordinary clinical care. Smoking increases lung cancer risk but also increases chronic respiratory and cardiovascular disease, competing mortality, and complications of invasive procedures. Patients at greatest cancer risk therefore need not obtain the greatest net benefit from screening. Our objective is to characterize patients for whom that benefit is uncertain and provide quantitative evidence for individualized screening decisions.

The study combines an observational cohort of approximately 34,039 adults aged 55–80 undergoing LCS in three geographically distinct health systems with the University of Michigan Lung Cancer Screening model (UM-LCS), a Cancer Intervention and Surveillance Modeling Network (CISNET) model. Retrospective data cover 2016–2019 and prospective data 2020–2022. Electronic health records (EHR), claims, cancer registries, and mortality records will describe comorbid disease, functional limitations, pulmonary function, screening findings, diagnostic procedures, complications, and cancer outcomes. Observed estimates will then refine microsimulations of long-term screening strategies.

## 2. Background and rationale

The National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) reported a 20% reduction in lung cancer mortality after three annual LDCT screens compared with chest radiography, approximately three fewer lung cancer deaths per 1,000 people screened. Its participants were healthier and younger than many adults eligible for screening in clinical practice: only one quarter were at least 65 and none were older than 74. Comparisons with screening-eligible respondents in the Health and Retirement Study found more comorbidity, more current smoking, and shorter estimated life expectancy in the community population. Estimated life expectancy was 21.2 years for NLST participants versus 14 years for a Medicare screening-eligible subgroup.

COPD illustrates the central problem. It is associated with roughly two- to threefold greater lung cancer risk, but advanced disease also increases procedural complications, mortality after lung resection, and competing respiratory mortality. In the NLST, false-positive findings affected almost 40% of participants over the screening rounds; some people with benign nodules underwent needle biopsy, bronchoscopy, or surgery. More stringent nodule thresholds may reduce false positives, but their effect on the mortality benefit must also be considered. In older and sicker populations, the balance may shift further.

The conceptual framework is that net benefit need not rise monotonically with baseline lung cancer risk. It may peak at intermediate risk and decline when competing mortality and treatment-related harms dominate. The proposed work will estimate this relationship rather than presume that all high-risk patients should receive the same screening. Guideline descriptions such as being in reasonably good health or able to tolerate treatment do not themselves supply transparent quantitative selection criteria.



The innovations are the combination of real-world outcomes with simulation, assessment of functional and pulmonary status beyond comorbidity counts, and a diverse population spanning three care-delivery systems. The intended contribution is to identify subgroups likely to benefit, subgroups in whom harms may outweigh benefit, and the clinical characteristics that distinguish them.

## 3. Objectives and specific aims

1. **Characterize multimorbidity in patients actually undergoing LCS.** Measure chronic co-existing conditions, functional limitations, and impaired pulmonary function, emphasizing patients with uncertain net benefit. Explore variation by race/ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and age.
2. **Quantify screening benefits and harms across multimorbidity levels.** Examine false-positive findings, procedure-related complications, and early-stage diagnosis. The hypothesis is that co-existing illness, functional limitation, and impaired pulmonary function increase screening-related harms.
3. **Compare long-term screening effectiveness across patient subgroups.** Refine validated CISNET model parameters with cohort estimates, then compare screening strategies and their benefits and harms. The hypothesis is that screening will not be effective for some groups with moderate-to-severe comorbidity, functional limitation, or severe COPD.

## 4. Preliminary work and basis

The team has combined EHR and claims information across institutions, studied pulmonary nodule management, and contributed to lung screening research and simulation. Latent-class analysis of lung cancer patients at Kaiser Permanente Southern California (KPSC) identified five comorbidity profiles that were independently associated with treatment selection and survival. These profiles provide a basis for extending characterization to functional and pulmonary status.


In prior SEER-Medicare analyses, elderly patients with stage IA lung cancer and at least two comorbidities were twice as likely to die within 90 days of surgery as those with at most one. Five-year overall survival was 47% versus 74%. These surgical outcomes motivate examining how comorbidity influences the benefits and harms of screening.

An NLP algorithm developed in the team's prior work identified pulmonary nodules in radiology reports with 96% sensitivity and 92% specificity, with similar accuracy in external validation. An expanded version captures laterality, lobe, size, attenuation, edge, and calcification with reported unpublished sensitivity and specificity above 95%. It will be used to identify screening-detected nodules and their characteristics. The team's UM-LCS model combines multistage carcinogenesis and discrete-state microsimulation and has been used in comparative screening analyses. These prior activities establish the data and modeling basis for the proposed study.

## 5. Research design and methods

### 5.1 Cohort, settings, and data sources

We will include people receiving baseline or repeat annual LDCT screening who have at least one primary-care visit at KPSC, the Medical University of South Carolina (MUSC), or the VA Portland Health Care System during 2016–2022. Diagnostic scans and people with a lung cancer history within five years will be excluded. The design specifies ages 55–80; its predictor definition specifies this age range at the start of 2016. The cohort description anticipates about 34,039 screened people and approximately 1,902 early-stage lung cancer patients treated across the participating institutions.

KPSC provides integrated primary and specialty care to a large, ethnically diverse population. Its screening order sets distinguish screening, diagnostic, and nodule-follow-up examinations. MUSC serves as a state safety-net hospital and identifies eligible individuals through EHR notifications and referral to its LCS program. VA Portland uses an EHR eligibility algorithm, clinical reminders, medical suitability assessment, and a dedicated screening nurse coordinator. These settings offer geographic, socioeconomic, racial/ethnic, and care-delivery variation.

The data coordinating center at KPSC, led by Gould and Shen with additional statistical input from Fan, will oversee capture, mapping, transfer, management, and analyses for Aims 1 and 2. We will reuse the common vocabulary and data infrastructure of Watch the Spot (WTS), which includes 13 groups of elements covering eligibility, demographics, smoking, nodules, utilization, comorbidity, and vital status. WTS itself addresses different questions and predominantly incidentally detected nodules; the proposed cohort concerns screening-detected findings.

Sites will use structured EHR exports, NLP, and text mining. Sources include administrative and membership records, ICD-9/10 diagnoses and procedures, CPT and HCPCS procedure codes, local LDCT orders, radiology reports, pathology, tumor registries, and mortality data. Census linkage will supply area-level socioeconomic measures; Medicare linkage will help identify diagnoses or treatment outside the participating systems. A common data dictionary will be implemented using each institution's available systems. Lung-RADS reporting will standardize LDCT findings.


### 5.2 Exposure and outcome definitions

**Comorbidity.** We will calculate a combined Charlson/Elixhauser score from EHR information in the 12 months before each LDCT, excluding lung cancer from the score. Scores of 0, 1, and at least 2 define the proposed best-, average-, and worst-health categories. Comorbidity is time-varying because it can change between screens.

**Functional limitations.** Claims and EHR indicators will be obtained primarily at KPSC and VA, which together represent nearly 80% of the cohort. Indicators include mobility aids, blood transfusion, oxygen or supplemental nutrition, hip/pelvic fracture, skin ulcer, pneumonia, delirium/dementia, bone marrow failure, depression, urinary catheter use, respiratory failure, sepsis, malnutrition/weight loss, fall injury, and syncope. We will use binary function-related indicators and an FRI score on a 0–13 scale, and groups patients as 0, 1, or at least 2. Baseline ascertainment uses the preceding 12 months; subsequent models update the indicators by screening interval.

**Pulmonary and related clinical status.** Structured spirometry will characterize airflow limitation and COPD severity. Impaired pulmonary function is defined as FEV1/FVC below 70%. The protocol proposes GOLD categories A–D, FEV measures, and ejection fraction data, including analyses of patients with heart failure. Smoking status and pack-years will be extracted from charts. Race/ethnicity comes from EHR records; census-derived socioeconomic variables include education, income, diversity, transport, health insurance expenditure, and internet access.

**Outcomes.** Aim 1 estimates prevalence of multimorbidity. Aim 2 follows baseline and repeat LDCT findings, biopsies, lung cancer detection and stage, and procedure-related complications. Registry follow-up for cancer within one year of LDCT will classify true/false positive and true/false negative examinations; cancer detection is expressed per 1,000 screened people. Serious complications within 7 and 30 days of diagnostic biopsies include pneumothorax, pulmonary hemorrhage, acute respiratory or renal failure, serious contrast reactions, and myocardial infarction. The protocol excludes procedures after lung cancer diagnosis from this diagnostic complication analysis and evaluates complications among all screened people, not only cancer cases. Stage, histology, metastases, surgery versus radiotherapy for early-stage disease, repeat imaging, and adherence to Lung-RADS recommendations are additional outcomes.

### 5.3 Aim 1: prevalence and subgroup characterization

We will calculate weighted prevalence using inverse selection probabilities informed by target-population risk-factor distributions. Descriptive statistics will report continuous and categorical characteristics by comorbidity, FRI, and pulmonary-function category. Two-sample proportion tests will compare prevalence by age, socioeconomic status, and race/ethnicity. Multivariable logistic regression will estimate adjusted associations for comorbidity (0 versus at least 1), functional limitation (0 versus at least 1), and FEV1/FVC below versus at least 70%, accounting for smoking, calendar year, geography, and other covariates.

### 5.4 Aim 2: screening outcomes, adjustment, and power

Discrete-time survival models will estimate outcome hazards and cumulative probabilities across repeated screening examinations. Time is indexed by examination number because screening-associated risk accrues at an examination. Each LDCT contributes an observation with the probability of an event during the following year. Comorbidity, FRI, pulmonary function, age, and other characteristics may vary between observations. Separate models will be fitted for each outcome, with appropriate covariates and interactions. Discrete hazards will be combined into cumulative risks over the proposed six-year screening course. Backward selection using Akaike's Information Criterion will balance predictive performance and model complexity.

We will address selection and confounding using demographics, smoking, clinical information, geographic location, and generalized propensity scores. Multinomial logistic models with generalized logit links will estimate probabilities for nonbinary exposures or screening intervals; scores will enter outcome models with flexible terms and interactions. 

Power calculations project 34,039 screened people and 902 incident cancers, based on an estimated 2% cancer rate. They assume at least 70% have a consequential comorbidity, approximately 20% have COPD, and a 20–30% procedure-complication rate in the specified comparison population. At 80% power and two-sided alpha 0.05, the proposed detectable risk difference for procedure-related complications is 5%; detectable false-positive risk differences range from 1.3% to 1.9% across comorbidity levels.

Logistic models will examine predictors of missingness. Sensitivity analyses will use inverse-probability weighting. If variables are missing at random, ten multiply imputed datasets will be analyzed separately and combined. These procedures address possible loss of precision and bias from incomplete clinical data.

### 5.5 Aim 3: microsimulation of individualized screening

UM-LCS has natural-history and screening components. Given smoking history, the natural-history model simulates lung cancer events and death from other causes. For a cancer, it generates diagnosis age, histology, stage, and cancer-specific survival conditional on age, sex, histology, and stage. The screening component adds preclinical sojourn time, screening schedules, test results, and stage-dependent survival after screen detection.



Model inputs include age/sex-specific cancer risk by smoking history; stage distributions with and without LDCT by histology and sex; survival by age, sex, stage, and comorbidity; preclinical sojourn times; sensitivity, specificity, and false positives; adherence to Lung-RADS recommendations; biopsy and complication rates; and competing mortality. Cohort-derived comorbidity, functional, and pulmonary measures will refine these inputs. Results will be compared with the original trial-based model.

The reference strategy is annual screening from ages 55 to 80 for people with at least 30 pack-years who smoke currently or stopped within 15 years. Alternative scenarios vary start/stop ages, frequency, pack-years, quit years, COPD, and comorbidity eligibility. Outputs include screens and follow-up scans, false positives, biopsies, incidence, cancer mortality, life-years and quality-adjusted life-years gained versus no screening, number needed to screen, overdiagnosis, and radiation-related cancer deaths. We will compare strategies across multimorbidity groups and find thresholds where benefits and harms resemble those of a predefined average-health subgroup. Using the Caverly et al. approach, additional analyses will examine how individual preferences jointly with comorbidity affect quality-adjusted net benefit.

## 6. Implementation, resources, and schedule

The multidisciplinary team covers epidemiology, thoracic oncology and surgery, health services research, disparities, statistics, real-world data, and simulation. Patient advocates will inform interpretation and dissemination for patient-centered decision support. The team will hold monthly calls and annual meetings.

The study lasts five years. Regulatory approvals begin in Year 1 and cohort development in its second quarter. The first three years emphasize assembling the real-world cohort and generating inputs through Aims 1 and 2. Years 4–5 emphasize model refinement and Aim 3. Manuscript preparation and dissemination accompany the later analyses. Expected products are a harmonized clinical cohort, subgroup-specific estimates of screening harms and benefits, refined simulation models, and evidence supporting screening discussions and future guideline development.

## 7. Ethics and data practices

Regulatory approvals will be sought at all study sites. Each institution will maintain a secure patient database. Names, medical record numbers, and dates of birth will be linked to unique study IDs and kept separately from study data. The coordinating center will use a common HIPAA-compliant structure. REDCap will support transfer from MUSC and VA Portland to KPSC; transmissions will be encrypted, with authenticated and authorized access. The centralized repository is intended to support consistent integration across sites.
